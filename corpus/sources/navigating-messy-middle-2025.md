NAVIGATING THE MESSY MIDDLE: THE MOVE TO MORE SUSTAINABLE TRUCKING

Given all the powertrain solutions now available in trucking’s messy middle, it can be difficult to determine which solution is best suited for a particular fleet’s operation.

By Mike Roeth, Executive Director

Before we begin looking at the available powertrain options, it’s important to understand what is meant by the Messy Middle. The first time we heard the term Messy Middle was back in 2018 while working on a Guidance Report, *Viable Class 7/8 Electric, Hybrid & Alternative Fuel Tractors*. Report author, Rick Mihelic, NACFE’s director of emerging technologies, credited Steve Hamburg, Chief Scientist for EDF, with coining the phrase. Back then we hypothesized that, “The messy middle will be marked by a good deal of trial and error through significant real-world experimentation.”

Now we are in the middle, where fleets have a range of powertrain solutions to choose from, but at NACFE we believe the end — or the future — is going to be dominated by battery electric and hydrogen fuel cell vehicles. ## A Variety of Powertrain Solutions

Here is a brief look at the current powertrain solutions available for heavy-duty trucks.

**Diesel:** While diesel has been the powertrain of choice for more than 100 years, it has undergone significant change in recent years as EPA regulations have mandated lower emissions. Upcoming regulations will continue to require changes to the diesel powertrain. In its current state, diesel provides the best range, lowest cost, widest available fueling and best packaging in the vehicles. Current vehicles are significantly lower emitters of harmful pollutants than older models while also being capable of achieving much higher fuel efficiency, but they still are significant creators of greenhouse gas emissions.

**Renewable diesel:** Renewable diesel is a biomass-derived fuel suitable for use in diesel engines. Renewable diesel is a hydrocarbon produced most often by hydrotreating and also by gasification, pyrolysis, and other biochemical and thermochemical technologies. It can be made from nearly any biomass feedstock. It is made by hydroprocessing fats, vegetable oils and waste cooking oils. Renewable diesel has reduced GHG emissions compared to legacy diesels and can be used in existing diesel engines. It can be transported via existing pipelines and sold at conventional truck stops and fueling stations.

**Biodiesel:** Biodiesel is mostly produced using a feedstock mixture of used and/or new cooking oil that has been extracted from plant-based sources. Biodiesel is produced by a process called transesterification. This process uses a small amount of alcohol (usually methanol), a catalyst and heat combined with the vegetable oil to produce the biodiesel.

Once the product is ready for use, it is usually blended with fossil-based diesel fuel up to a concentration of 20% or less (often referred to as B20). Today’s engine manufacturers have approved the use of biodiesel blends of up to 20% in their engines. Running higher concentrations of biodiesel are likely to cause problems including compromised cold weather performance. However, it is possible to add hardware to a truck to run 100% biodiesel, but you must have a tank of traditional diesel and switch back and forth when temperature requirements are met.

Biodiesel blends have about the same emission performance and the same energy content as standard diesel fuel. NOx has been shown to be slightly higher and particulates slightly lower, but the differences are very small. The portion of the blend that is biodiesel has been shown to reduce $CO_2$ emissions by about 75% because the carbon in the fuel came from plants that were the source of the feedstock.

**Compressed natural gas/renewable natural gas:** Compressed natural gas (CNG) technology for heavy-duty trucks has been in use for a decade, having been through several iterations of engine development since 2009. CNG is made from methane that is extracted from the source and compressed to a high pressure.

There are several benefits to using natural gas, including the fact that the gas for CNG can be sourced locally. It burns cleaner than diesel and therefore has less harmful emissions. It also is less expensive than diesel. However, CNG fueling can be a problem as there is a growing but not widespread fueling network.

Renewable natural gas is obtained from biogas that is the result of the fermentation of organic waste including cow manure and landfill organics. Renewable natural gas can be used in any natural gas engines and has become part of climate goals in some states. Most renewable natural gas has a lower carbon intensity than fossil natural

**Hydrogen:** Burning hydrogen in an internal combustion engine can provide zero carbon emissions, assuming the hydrogen is produced through green processes using energy from renewable sources. It also produces very low NOx. The major concern with this approach is the availability of green hydrogen and the cost of using a high-cost fuel in an engine with around 40% efficiency.

**Hybrids:** Hybrids can be powered by diesel or renewable fuels and can provide adequate range. Hybrids are more complex, having both engine and battery powertrain components. They provide regenerative braking and thus recover energy while going downhill and in stop-and-go traffic. They provide limited benefit in long-haul applications because of the lack of regenerative braking opportunities and because of the weight of the batteries needed for those distances.

**Battery electric:** The BEV provides a zero-emission vehicle solution with the simplest vehicle configuration of all these options. The BEV also provides a viable solution in that it is the best in terms of overall energy efficiency. It has an overall efficiency of about 65% to 85% from the charger to the wheels. The three major challenges for the BEV are infrastructure/charging, range and cost. Today, many fleets are finding that the BEV arrives from the OEM before the charging infrastructure is in place.

## Bringing Clarity to Long Haul

The next Run on Less event in September 2025 will focus on heavy-duty Class 8 trucks — sleepers and day cabs — in long-haul return-to-base and over-the-road duty cycles.

We are calling this demonstration Run on Less – Messy Middle and we will be focusing on the multiple powertrain solutions that are currently available to fleets, including battery electric, hydrogen fuel cells, renewable natural gas, renewable and bio diesel, and hybrids. We will be looking at energy efficiency technologies for all fuel types. We hope to bring some clarity to long haul.

Our goal with this Run is to explain the various alternative fueling options and investigate the need for infrastructure changes to accommodate alternative-fueled vehicles.

Run on Less – Messy Middle will feature around 10 fleets deploying trucks with differing decarbonization solutions. As in previous Run on Less demonstrations, the technology will either be commercially available or representative of the manufacturer’s plans.

While long-haul represents a small portion of the total trucking industry (all classes), it contributes a significant portion of emissions. If we are going to decarbonize trucking, we need to better understand the options available to fleets operating in that duty cycle and help them determine the powertrain solution that is best for their specific application.

## Factors in Decision-making

Once fleets are aware of all the options, they can begin evaluating them based on their own specific needs. The decision-making process should start by evaluating a variety of factors.

Here are some things fleets need to factor into their evaluation of these powertrain solutions. These factors need to be evaluated against the capabilities of each of the powertrain solutions being considered. See Figure 2.

**Duty Cycles:** What type of duty cycle does your fleet run? Is it long haul at 65 MPH for 500 miles? Is it regional haul with a lot of stop-and-go runs, lightly loaded? Does the vehicle return to base each day? Is your vehicle in operation for 8 to 10 hours per day or running two to three shifts? Do you run in flat regions or mountainous ones? Are temperatures moderate, or are you running in extreme desert conditions or cold areas?

**Energy/Fuel Pricing:** Consider the cost of fuel, both from a capital standpoint and an operational one. For

electrification, work with the utilities to understand their pricing and the capital investment needed. Diesel and other fuels fluctuate with many market conditions or world events. For renewable natural gas (RNG), understand the costs of infrastructure and the costs of RNG in a Diesel Gallon Equivalent (DGE) format. There also might be incentives like the Low Carbon Fuel Standard in California to lower the costs of the various energy types. Also, understand the efficiency of these different fuel types and how much fuel will be consumed per mile or ton-mile of work.

**Energy/Infrastructure:** Work with your utilities to determine if electricity is available. Make sure you have an estimate of the energy demand of your fleet. Also work with the charging infrastructure provider to determine what type of chargers are best for your operation and the schedule required to install the chargers. For renewable fuels, determine where the fueling stations are and whether they are on the routes your fleet travels.

**Regulatory Factors:** What are the upcoming regulatory factors facing your fleet over the next 5 to 10 years? Are you in an area where a zero-emission vehicle is required or will be required? Consider the upcoming EPA ultra-low NOx requirements. Consider the additional costs of an engine that meets regulations, where cost increase estimates range from $10,000 to $40,000 or more over the life of each vehicle. Consider possible reliability problems that could create more downtime.

**Customer/Shipper Goals:** A focus on sustainability is clearly one of the largest changes in the trucking industry in recent years. In the past, all that mattered was cost and whether the goods arrived on time. Today shippers are asking fleets what they are doing to reduce their carbon footprint along their shipping routes.

**Well-to-Wheel Impact:** Consider the source of the fuel/energy. If you are considering electrification, work with the utility to understand the mix of energy being used to produce the electricity. Is it all coal, natural gas or renewable from wind or solar? What percentage is renewable or nuclear? The average mix of sources throughout the US is approximately 40% renewable or nuclear — and growing. If the energy production source is at or above this threshold, electrification is good for the environment. If it is not at this level, work with your utility to understand their transition to decarbonization. Renewable natural gas has a negative carbon intensity because of the use of methane as the feedstock, which is very harmful to the environment. Natural gas that is not renewable still emits 10% less $CO_2$ than diesel. How green — meaning produced from renewable energy — is the hydrogen today and how green will it be in the future?

**Other Considerations:** There are numerous items to consider in this category. This has to do with capital considerations, buying cycles, life of vehicle, etc. When possible, work to monetize as many of these considerations as possible when considering alternative fuels. Today customers and shippers have environmental, social & governance and sustainability goals. They will place some weight on parameters such as well-to-wheel carbon footprint, ZEV usage, etc. The fleet must understand the different powertrain solutions and how they affect $CO_2$, NOx, and PM.

When considering all these factors, ask yourself if solutions other than diesel are more reliable or cost effective. After reviewing the attributes of each powertrain solution and then evaluating them based on a variety of considerations, choose the one or ones that makes the most sense for each application and duty cycle.

## Use NACFE’s Framework to Evaluate Powertrain Solutions

NACFE developed a framework that fleets might find useful, and it offers a sequence for evaluating the various solutions. See Figure 3.

If you are going to switch from diesel, first consider moving directly to battery electric, especially if you are looking at replacing terminal tractors, vans and step vans, medium-duty box trucks and Class 8 trucks in regional haul. If battery electric trucks meet your needs and the cost and timing are right, this can be the simplest path forward and a single change for your fleet.

If BEVs don’t work, the next consideration might be hydrogen fuel cell vehicles, giving another zero-emissions pathway with probably a better solution for long haul. This assumes that the technology matures, and green hydrogen becomes more readily available.

Natural gas, hybrids, renewable fuels and hydrogen internal combustion engines are other options that fleets might deploy if their strategies to decarbonize cannot be supported in a timely manner by zero-emissions solutions.

In some cases, it may make sense to stay with a diesel strategy working to make the equipment and operations as efficient as possible, ahead of a later decision to move to zero-emission vehicles.

Choosing the right powertrain solution can be difficult especially for fleets that operate in more than one duty cycle. The reality is they may end up with a variety of powertrain options based on operating conditions.

It is also likely that there will be multiple decisions on a fleet’s decades-long journey to zero-emission transportation. For instance, a fleet may move from diesel to natural gas, using as much renewable fuel as possible, then later move to hydrogen engine solutions before moving to battery electric or hydrogen fuel cell tractors.

Fleets need to find the option(s) that work best for them today and keep working with them into the future. For the short-term that could include taking strides to improve the efficiency of their diesel-powered trucks while continuing to monitor developments in other powertrain options. One thing is certain, fleets cannot just sit back and wait. They must begin evaluating different options today. It is likely that there will be multiple correct technology choices for each fleet or for multiple parts of a fleet’s business at the time they make procurement decisions.

## ABOUT US

### ABOUT NACFE
The North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) works to drive the development and adoption of efficiency enhancing, environmentally beneficial, and cost-effective technologies, services, and operational practices in the movement of goods across North America. NACFE provides independent, unbiased research, including Confidence Reports on available technologies and Guidance Reports on emerging ones, which highlight the benefits and consequences of each, and deliver decision-making tools for fleets, manufacturers, and others. NACFE partners with RMI on a variety of projects including the Run on Less demonstration series, electric trucks, emissions reductions, and low-carbon supply chains.

### ABOUT RUN ON LESS BY NACFE
Run on Less is a biennial demonstration showcasing advancements in freight efficiency. The event takes place over the course of three weeks and has fleets across North America moving freight in their normal operations to show how it is possible to operate efficiently with today’s technologies. Each truck in the Run is outfitted with a telematics device allowing NACFE to track key metrics.

The first Run took place in 2017 and featured seven fleets that averaged 10 MPG in long-haul routes over the course of the Run. Run on Less Regional, held in 2019, featured 10 fleets that averaged 8.4 MPG in regional haul routes during the Run. Run on Less – Electric, held in 2021, featured 13 trucks operating electric vehicles on real routes carrying real freight. Run on Less – Electric DEPOT featured 10 fleets with 15 or more electric trucks at a depot.
