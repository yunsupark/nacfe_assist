## 2013 REPORT CONFIDENCE FINDINGS

The study team for the 2013 Confidence Report found that 6x2 configurations resulted in an average fuel savings of 2.5% and that fleets should have a medium to high confidence in a two-year payback. While the fuel savings as well as the benefits and challenges still largely hold today, the relative value of the benefits versus challenges has changed significantly, primarily due to lower fuel prices.

## 6X2 GENERATIONS

While there are not distinct product generations in 6x2 axles, and products introduced prior to the 2013 report continue on the market, it can be helpful to think about how fleets are adopting the technology in terms of generations. It is crucial to know which generation of design is being discussed or evaluated when sharing information about the benefits and challenges of a particular technology. Based on interviews with the industry, this study team categorizes 6x2 generations as follows:

| Generation | Configuration | Comment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | 6x2 with tag axle, no load-shifting technology, manual differential locks | 2–3% fuel savings, weight savings of 300–400 lbs. vs. 6x4s, issues with accelerated tire wear and traction under certain conditions resulted in poor driver perception |
| II | 6x2 with tag axle, manual or automated load-shifting, traction control, engine parameters adjusted to reduce low speed, clutch engagement, and engine brake torque | Load shifting and traction control mitigates traction issues, limiting torque can improve tire wear significantly |
| III | 6x2 with liftable pusher axle, automatic load-sensing/load-shifting, traction control, engine parameters to limit low speed/brake torque | An option for fleets expecting to carry less than 60,000 lbs. at least 30% of the time, potential additional 2% fuel savings, improved traction and tire wear vs. Gen II under lightly loaded conditions; a heavier front axle, suspension, tires, and other equipment may optimize this configuration |

## LESSONS LEARNED BY EARLY ADOPTERS

Early adopters started with Generation I systems and realized the shortcomings in tire wear and low-traction situations, leading to damage in driver perception:

* Tire wear is higher when compared to equivalent 6x4 vehicles, with some early adopters experiencing a 50–70% reduction in tire life on their drive tires. Fleets that have adopted best practices have cut this penalty significantly, decreasing the reduction in life to 20% on the driven axle.
* Driver perception, particularly as it relates to safety and traction of 6x2-equipped vehicles, has not improved. Many drivers have not actually experienced or been trained on this configuration and often only hear feedback from a negative and sometimes vocal minority.

Fleets that have implemented a complete Generation II system have found tire wear and traction issues can be managed at a much reduced level and are able to benefit from the fuel savings. Unfortunately for some fleets, the damage in driver perception was done before a full Generation II system could be implemented.

**Tire Wear**—Mitigating accelerated drive tire wear is one of the keys to a successful long-term implementation of 6x2 systems. Fleets that have implemented equipment upgrades and effective driving practices such as using retreads on the tag axle and limiting torque have greatly reduced their tire wear penalty.

**Tire Selection**—Selecting an appropriate tire is crucial. Considerations to take into account when selecting tires for 6x2 vehicles include:
* **Steer Axle**—Fleets should choose steer axle tires based on factors such as usage, whether line haul or regional operating conditions dominate, and the potential for scrub wear.
* **Driven Axle**—Fleets should continue with the same drive tires as they are currently using on a 6x4, and then update the vehicle to optimize torque parameters.
* **Free-Rolling Axle**—Fit both the driven and non-driven axles with the original drive tires used in a 6x4 application, or use an energy trailer tire retread.

**Traction**—Increasing weight on the driven tires is an effective solution when traction is compromised. Fleets that have implemented load shifting technology combined with driver training are able to operate 6x2s in all conditions.

**Liftable 6x2**—Since the initial NACFE 6x2 Confidence Report, the industry has seen the introduction of a 6x2 tandem axle with a liftable pusher axle. Various fleets have cited fuel efficiency benefits of a liftable axle in long-haul duty cycles, as well as better traction and improved tire life and drivability. However, liftable systems do have some challenges vs. standard 6x2 systems such as increased cost and weight.

**Driver Communication and Training**—Not only do drivers require an understanding of how 6x2 systems function and the best way to handle them in challenging conditions, many drivers start with a strong negative perception of 6x2s. Fleets must implement a thorough communications and training plan to offset such biases.

## FUTURE DEVELOPMENTS

The fuel-savings potential combined with a skeptical industry on the technology has spurred some interesting innovations with respect to 6x2s. New products in development include:

**Dual range disconnect**—Dana has a system similar to a standard 6x4 but with the ability to mechanically disconnect the inter-axle shaft and internal gearing of the tag axle when operating at higher speeds.

**Regenerative Braking**—Hyliion has developed an aftermarket system to hybridize tractor-trailer combinations using the trailer as the platform for capturing regenerative braking energy during downhill portions of a trip.

## CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

While 6x2 systems offer an undeniable fuel consumption improvement, they are still not widely viewed as a net positive by the majority of the line-haul industry. Adoption rates have been less than anticipated by the 2013 study team. Many early implementations suffered from very high tire wear combined with poor residual values that more than offset the fuel consumption gains. New 6x2 axles, in particular liftables, are entering the market. They may reduce some of the challenges related to 6x2s but there is limited feedback from fleets at this time.

* Drive tire wear on 6x2s will not achieve parity with 6x4s. However, measures can be taken to reduce the accelerated

wear. Selecting a retread trailer tire for the free-rolling axle may result in the lowest cost option for fleets.
* Fleets should take a system-wide approach and implement the full Generation II package including load-shifting technology, traction control, and engine parameters to limit torque in low gear, at clutch engagement, and under engine braking.
* Good and consistent driver communication and training is critical with this technology. Drivers should understand the overall benefit of 6x2s, how the systems work, and when a manual intervention is beneficial.
* Traditional 6x4 systems are improving, reducing the potential efficiency gains of a 6x2 system albeit at a greater cost.
* Residual values for 6x2s remain a problem due to the reputation of Generation I systems. Fleets that have persisted and implemented Generation II 6x2s do not report a problem with residual values, although these are typically smaller fleets and would not turn over a large number of trucks at once.
* Fleets should gain knowledge of 6x2 tag axle systems and test not only the technology but also their internal processes for managing engine parameters and driver communication and training.
* Fleets that are below 60,000 lbs. GCWR at least 30% of the time should consider testing a liftable 6x2 axle system.

## Introduction

This addendum is an update to the original 6x2 Confidence Report published in January 2013. As with new technologies in many other industries, a process of refinement is often required before a technology moves from early adopters to wider acceptance among mainstream users. Depending on the technology and industry, this process may happen very quickly and directly, while in others it is an evolutionary process that occasionally suffers setbacks along the way. In some industries, particularly ones where product cycles are short, improvements and refinements are apparent as new generations of products are introduced in quick succession. While new 6x2 axles have been announced since the publication of the original 6x2 Confidence Report, many of the refinements that have occurred are in how fleets are implementing 6x2s and the accompanying technologies that are being used alongside 6x2s. Therefore, the conclusions on fuel savings as well as the benefits and challenges from the original report still largely hold. This addendum focuses on the lessons learned by some of the early adopters of 6x2s, the refinements implemented by fleets that continue to realize the benefits, and the new 6x2 products that have been introduced or announced.

Since the January 2013 Confidence Report, 6x2 adoption among North American long-haul fleets has increased from approximately 2% to 4–5% in 2016. While the increase could be interpreted as a 100% increase, the fact is that 6x2s are still viewed by many in the industry as a niche offering suited only for the southern regions of the U.S. While every OEM NACFE interviewed agrees that 6x2s offer real fuel savings and remain a tool for achieving the phase II targets of the greenhouse gas (GHG) regulations, the industry pushed back hard on the initial proposal by the EPA, which projected an adoption rate of 60% by 2024 for high roof tractors. The target in the final published regulation is 30% by 2027 (the adoption rate includes liftable 6x2s as well as 4x2s for all class 8 tractor cab variations), which most OEMs and industry suppliers interviewed believe is achievable though still a long way from the current status.

However, when OEMs and suppliers introduce new axles, such as the highly downsped axles recently introduced by Detroit, Meritor, and Dana, they are only available in a 6x4 configuration even though their purpose is improving fuel consumption—an indicator that the market has not yet accepted 6x2 axles as a mainstream solution for achieving greater efficiency.

Some factors that are contributing to limited adoption of 6x2 axles include:

* **Declining price of fuel**—At the time of the 2013 report, fuel was peaking at $0.64 per mile (2012 data, ATRI) having surpassed the cost of driver wages and benefits. While fuel prices remain volatile and have increased somewhat in recent months above the 2015 average of $0.40 per mile, they remain well off of their 2012 peak.
* **Increased tire wear**—Some fleets, particularly early adopters, experienced a 50–70% reduction in tire life on their drive tires compared to equivalent 6x4 vehicles. Fleets that have adopted best practices have cut this penalty significantly, reducing the reduction in life to 20% on the driven axle. But tire costs loom large in the eyes of truck fleets. Even though fuel costs outweigh tire costs by a factor of 10 at typical long-haul fleets (ATRI), an improvement of 2–3% in fuel cost is easily overlooked in the face of volatile fuel prices while a 10% increase in tire costs is easily identified.
* **Driver perception**—Driver perception, particularly as it relates to safety and traction of 6x2-equipped vehicles, has not improved. This is in part self-fulfilling, as trucks with 6x2s remain a small niche. Many drivers have not actually experienced or been trained on this configuration and often only hear feedback from a negative and sometimes vocal minority.
* **Lower residual values**—Residual values for 6x2 trucks suffer due to all of the above factors. For fleets, particularly ones that were not able to mitigate significant tire wear and received largely negative feedback from their drivers, this tipped the balance away from further 6x2 purchases. The study team interviewed several large early adopters that have switched back to 6x4-equipped trucks. In all cases, all of the above factors influenced the decision, including a significant setback in residual value.
* **More efficient 6x4 axles**—Meritor announced the 14X HE in February of 2017 with an advertised 1.5% axle efficiency improvement. Meritor states that traditional tandem axles are about 94–95% efficient. If an equally efficient drive axle were spec'd in a 6x2, the claimed improvement could reduce the benefit of a 6x2 system to 1–2%. While not impacting 6x2s to date, recently announced highly efficient 6x4 axles will be an easier choice for fleets wishing to improve efficiency while avoiding the challenges associated with 6x2s. As shown in Figure 1, the expected adoption rate of highly efficient 6x4 axles is expected to reach 80% by 2027 rapidly outpacing 6x2s.

<!-- page: 12 -->

However, there is reason for optimism with 6x2 technology. Through a combination of best practices and adoption of complementary technologies, fleets have found that 6x2 axles work for their application and are benefitting from the improved fuel consumption. For some fleets, a 300–400 lb. weight reduction is an even bigger benefit, translating to additional revenue in weight sensitive applications or offsetting the weight of other equipment (refer to the Lightweighting Confidence Report). Therefore, in addition to exploring the newest 6x2 axles, this addendum is heavily focused on tire selection and wear, an area where best practices can make a significant improvement in ROI for fleets that want to benefit from this technology.

## Background

### 2013 Confidence Report Findings

The study team for the 2013 Confidence Report found that 6x2 configurations resulted in an average fuel savings of 2.5%. There were 10 separate, robust tests presented in that report (http://www.truckingefficiency.org/chassis/6x2-axles). Savings ranged from 1.9% for OEM data to 3.5% for the fleets. It was concluded that the technology does deliver a substantial improvement in fuel economy and that fleets should have a medium to high confidence in a two-year payback.

Additional benefits and challenges as described in the 2013 report are summarized in the following table.

| Benefit | 2013 Study Comments |
| --- | --- |
| Weight savings of 400–450 lbs. | 6x2s allow an overall reduction of 400–450 lbs. A weight savings reduction like the 6x2 is an enabler for other fuel-saving but weight-adding features. |
| Cost parity with 6x4s | There is currently a $1,000–$2,000 upcharge. However the industry is in agreement that within a few years, 6x2 configurations will be approximately cost neutral. |
| Maintenance | The reduction in mechanical components should result in an estimated annual savings of $100 per year. |
| Stability improvement | An overlooked benefit of the 6x2 configuration is that it might give a truck improved lateral stability when operated in slippery or poor road conditions. One fleet manager described the tag axle as a rudder that keeps the tractor pointed straight ahead. |
| **Challenges** | |
| Traction | Testing confirmed that 6x2 tractive effort is reduced versus a 6x4. A manual dump valve or automated load shifting system allows a 6x2 to achieve improved tractive performance. |
| Tire wear | Data from tire manufacturers and several fleets indicates that the useable tire life is one third that of 6x4 tires. Depending on tire type and cost, the fuel savings are typically three to seven times greater than the increased tire cost. |
| Resale value | This study finds that the effects a 6x2 configuration can have on resale or residual value of a used truck at time of sale vary widely. Many shared the belief that the resale value is rising as the technology is better understood and the industry becomes better educated. |

## Greenhouse Gas Regulations

In the final published regulation of the phase 2 Greenhouse Gas and Fuel Efficiency Standards for Medium and Heavy Duty Engines and Vehicles (commonly referred to as GHG phase 2) 6x2s are mentioned 68 times. While this includes tables as well as text related to feedback received on 6x2s from industry, it points to a view by the EPA that 6x2 axles, given their current low adoption rate and fuel savings, hold significant potential for the industry to reduce fuel consumption.

As mentioned earlier, the adoption rate the EPA assumed in the GHG phase 2 regulation was reduced significantly from the initial proposal due to industry input. Table 4 summarizes the assumed adoption rate in the final rule.

The regulator assessed the potential fuel-savings by examining several sources (including the original 2013 Confidence Report) and through data from industry participants and concluded that the “reductions are in the range of two to three percent.” However, rather than providing a fixed value to calculate the greenhouse gas benefit, the agencies decided to include 6x2s (as well as 4x2s) as a manufacturer input to the Greenhouse Gas Emissions Model (GEM). In addition to the efficiency benefit, 6x2-equipped vehicles will also be credited with a 300 lb. weight reduction.

## 4x2 Configuration

As mentioned earlier, in the GHG phase 2 adoption rate assumption, 4x2s are grouped with 6x2s. Data on the current adoption rate of 4x2s in Class 8 line-haul is difficult to find due to the categorization of trucks, applications, and grouping of GCWR classes. The Vehicle Inventory and Use Survey from the U.S. Census Bureau shows data at a very detailed level. Unfortunately, it was last published for 2002 and even that data requires making some assumptions to sort out the Class 8 tractor 4x2s. More current data can be found by examining registrations through R L Polk although it does not specifically break out 4x2s for line-haul applications. In the end, following discussions with several industry experts, the study team concluded that 4x2s likely represent approximately 5% of the Class 8 line-haul market.

While 4x2s are very common in Europe (in combination with three-axle trailers), in North America they are used primarily for pulling doubles (and some triples) by LTL carriers or for lighter duty cycles in dedicated operations. This study team did not focus on 4x2s specifically although the fuel savings when compared to 6x4s should be even greater than for a 6x2 in a corresponding duty cycle. Truckload carriers will not find 4x2s to be a viable option given the nature of their operation. However, fleets that do not need the full carrying capacity of a three-axle tractor can benefit from this configuration.

## Tire Fitment

All the major tire manufacturers offer product ranges that fleets can choose from to tune wear and fuel economy trade-offs in 6x2 applications in order to arrive at the most beneficial tire configuration for a specific fleet’s application. In all cases, fleets should discuss tire fitment choices with the tire manufacturer. In addition, there are tools which can help a fleet select the appropriate tire, such as the worksheet provided in the Technology & Maintenance Council RP 223D “Tire Selection Process.” Considerations to take into account when selecting tires for 6x2 vehicles include the following:

**Steer Axle**
* Generally speaking, a fleet should choose steer axle tires based on their current normal tire selection process. This choice is based on factors such as the usage, whether line-haul or regional operating conditions dominate, and the potential for scrub wear.

<!-- page: 21 -->

* If a fleet is considering leveraging a 6x2 lift axle to place more weight on the steer axle, fleets should evaluate the need for tires with higher load capacity. For typical steer tire sizes in use today, a load range H, or 16-ply rating, will provide weight capacity in the range of 13,000 lbs. to about 14,000 lbs. on the steer axle. If higher steer axle loading is envisioned when the vehicle is in lift mode, it may be necessary to move to less conventional tire sizes that can support the load (as well as using steer axles with higher load ratings). If the 6x2 in varying lift/down modes increases the variation of weight on the steer axle versus non-lift 6x2s and 6x4s, there can be an increased risk of irregular wear on the steer axle tires.

**6x2 Driven Axle**
* The starting point for selecting tires for the driven axle of a 6x2 tandem is the current drive tires in use on 6x4 vehicles running in the same usage. A fleet may see a 50% reduction in wear life of the drive tires when first moving to an equivalent 6x2 configuration until engine torque parameters are optimized.
* Vehicle updates that can help mitigate accelerated wear include the use of automated transmissions (AMTs) and torque management systems. It should be noted that this same equipment is expected to improve drive tire wear on 6x4 tandems, but it is anticipated that the percent improvement can be greater on 6x2s. Vehicle equipment improvements plus good driving practices have limited the wear penalty to about 20% in some cases, when using the same tires on equivalent 6x4 versus 6x2 configurations. The study team has not seen evidence of reductions in wear penalty to levels below 20%.
* It should be noted that current vehicle development trends to achieve numerically lower drive axle ratios, known as downspeeding (refer to Downspeeding Confidence Report) for fuel economy improvements are expected to generate a wear penalty on drive tires for all axle configurations.
* A move to longer wearing drive tires on a 6x2 can improve wear life but will degrade fuel economy benefits. Although there are many factors in play, moving from a “low fuel” to a “mileage” drive fitment will cost about 1–3.5% in fuel economy. This deficit typically overcomes fuel savings for a 6x2. Therefore, the study team does not recommend that fleets change to a higher rolling resistance tire in order to improve wear life unless they are certain that the fuel savings are maintained.
* Depending on the level of increased torque on 6x2s, certain tread patterns which are appropriate for use on 6x4s may no longer be a good fitment due to potential negative impacts of high torque on tread pattern durability. Fleet users should discuss their specific applications with tire manufacturers.
* Selecting a new drive tire model for wear is generally clearer than a selection for traction, although the two performances are often complementary. While a more traction-oriented tire may help in marginal traction situations (for either 6x2 or 6x4), load-biasing controls on the air suspension and differential lock are more reliable solutions for low traction (as discussed later in this report).

**Free-Rolling Axle of 6x2 Configurations**
* To get the greatest fuel economy benefit, an energy trailer tire is the most frequently recommended fitment on the free-rolling axle of a 6x2 for line-haul operations. For regional

<!-- page: 22 -->

applications, a SmartWay “mileage” tire is recommended. If a fleet user finds that tires on the non-driven axle are more subject to scrub, non-SmartWay regional trailer tires are available. As for the drive tires, scrub-resistant regional trailer tires may be acceptable under greenhouse gas rules, but not accepted in states requiring the use of SmartWay designated tires. For the purposes of the report, trailer tires on the non-driven axle are assumed to exhibit the same wear life as on the trailer axles. Switching to higher wear life tires or more scrub-resistant tires will usually come at a cost to fuel economy.
* In some regional or combined regional/line-haul applications, fleets may choose to put SmartWay or non-SmartWay regional steer tires on the non-driven axle. One goal of this approach is to match the pull point of the tire on the free-rolling axle to the pull point of the tire on the drive axle (which is experiencing accelerated wear).
* Although somewhat counter-intuitive, fitting both the driven and non-driven axles of a 6x2 with the original drive tires used in a 6x4 application can be a reasonable choice, or at least first approach, for a fleet moving to 6x2 vehicles—particularly for ”low fuel” drive tires. The success of this solution is highly dependent on the given fleet usage, but can offer the following benefits:
  * Depending on the specific tire model, a drive tire on the free-rolling axle of a 6x2 may exhibit considerably better wear life than a trailer tire on the same position, with potentially little fuel economy penalty, and also exhibit better wear life than the same tire on the driven axle.
  * Following a tire rotation schedule between the driven and non-driven axles may boost the overall drive tire wear life across both axles. Alternatively, new drive tires can be run on the drive axle to a given tread depth, and then run out to final removal on the free-rolling axle.
  * Using the same tires on all tandem wheel positions can simplify tire management for the 6x2 tandem.
  * Use of a low-rolling-resistance drive retread on the free-rolling axle can help lower tire cost/mile with low fuel economy penalty.
  * Use of the same drive tire on both axles of a lift-axle 6x2 allows the most straightforward operation of the lift mode technology, which seeks to maximize the load on the driven axle at all times.
  * However, if the drive tire is directional, users should check with their tire manufacturer to determine if the direction of rotation should be reversed when the tire is mounted on the free-rolling axle.
* Use of an energy trailer tire retread on the free-rolling axle may provide the best combination of fuel economy with reduced tire cost/mile.

## Traction

Traction concerns associated with 6x2 axle configurations stem from the concentration of torque needed to accelerate the vehicle onto a single axle instead of two axles. Mobility concerns expressed by 6x2 users have been during low speed maneuvers under conditions when traction is compromised, including:

* Snow-covered, icy, or wet pavement
* Gravel or uneven dirt lots

Concerns are often related to instances when backing the unloaded tractor underneath trailers, moving out from underneath trailers, or pulling away from a loading dock under these compromised conditions.

The 2013 Confidence Report presents test data that supports the idea that a 6x4-equipped tractor has better traction than a 6x2, even one equipped with load-shifting technology. Conversations with engineers at an OEM that conducted their own internal traction tests confirm this result. However, personnel at the OEM involved in traction testing as well as fleets interviewed by this study team are all in agreement that while specific tests show an advantage for 6x4s, under real world conditions, there is almost no practical difference. The 2013 report and fleets interviewed by this study team confirmed lower tow costs for 6x2 trucks. In the interview with the OEM personnel involved in traction testing, the study team was told, “(I) don't know if we have ever gotten into situations that we couldn't get out of… it comes back to understanding your equipment and using it in the proper way.” Granted, an OEM test driver will not experience all of the situations an actual driver may see, but even fleets that have abandoned 6x2s in favor of 6x4s believe the traction issue was more of a driver training and perception issue than a real operational problem. As one maintenance manager explained to the team, when a driver gets stuck, emotion and pride can come into play. Therefore, it is critical to avoid such situations through training and technology for 6x2s to be successful.

<!-- page: 24 -->

An analytical approach is helpful in understanding the reason tractive effort is lost in a 6x2 and why load biasing is the preferred approach for offsetting the problem. The starting point for most traction evaluations is the well-known equation describing the relationship between the weight or load carried by a driven tire, which we will call ($F_L$), and the total amount of horizontal force that can be generated to propel the vehicle ($F_H$), through a coefficient of friction ($\mu$), by a given number of driven tires ($N$). This equation is given as:

$$F_{H,\text{drive}} = N_{\text{drive}} * \mu * F_{L,\text{drive}} \quad \text{(Equation 1)}$$

If the total driving forces provided by all the driven tires on the vehicle is greater than the force needed to move the vehicle—which is most often the case—then the truck is able to start up or accelerate normally. If the tires cannot generate the force necessary to move the vehicle, then the tires may spin without the vehicle advancing.

Examples of the maximum value of coefficient of friction that can be achieved on various road surfaces are summarized in Table 11. Generally speaking, the nature of the road surface is the primary determining factor of friction coefficient.

| Road Surface | Peak $\mu$ |
| --- | --- |
| Dry Asphalt or Concrete | 0.7 – 1.2 |
| Wet Asphalt | 0.7 – 0.9 |
| Wet Concrete | 0.5 – 0.95 (wet or damp) |
| | 0.4 – 0.5 (very wet) |
| Dry Gravel | 0.5 |
| Asphalt, Snow | 0.1 – 0.5 |
| Asphalt, Ice | 0.1 – 0.2 |

Table 11: Coefficient of Friction for Various Road Surface Conditions

*(Source: Lex, Cornelia, Maximum Tire-Road Friction Coefficient Estimation, Graz University of Technology, Austria, 2015)*

Once the friction coefficient of the road surface is significantly degraded, traction must be improved by other factors. Looking back at Equation 1, to increase traction requires either: 1) an increase in the number of driven axles ($N_{\text{drive}}$); or 2) increased weight on the driven tires ($F_{L\text{ drive}}$).

It should be clear from Equation 1 that a change from two to one driving axle will reduce the available force to move the vehicle by half (i.e., driven tires are carrying only half the load they would carry in a 6x4 application). If at the same time the road surface coefficient of friction ($\mu$) drops from 1 to 0.5, the available horizontal force ($F_H$) is again cut by half. A change in tire type alone typically will not be enough to offset this condition. Although significant differences in friction coefficient exist when comparing drive and trailer tires, the differences between viable drive tires intended for line-haul use are relatively small in comparison to the differences resulting from changing road conditions. However, shifting load onto the driven tires can increase $F_{L\text{ drive}}$ substantially, particularly when lightly loaded or bobtailing, conditions that fleets report as being particularly difficult for 6x2 traction, resulting in enough tractive force to allow the vehicle to move.

### A Side Note on Tire Traction

The term *coefficient of friction* used in Equation 1 is most often used to describe the sliding of a block against a surface. The same equation is being used here for illustrative purposes to describe torque being applied to a tire that advances by rolling. Friction is generated between a tire and road or other surface by a number of chemical and physical processes, which are not equally important across all operating conditions. Road surface is generally taken to be the most important factor for traction. Important road surface factors are texture, porosity, and type of aggregate used. Temperature is an important environmental factor that affects the performance of tread compounds, as well as road surface “contamination” such as the presence of water and its depth, snow, and ice (and even “warm ice” versus “cold ice”). Tread pattern can play a role, and some fleets may find that dedicated snow tires or tires described by manufacturers as “high-traction” are obligatory for successful operations, even at a cost to fuel economy. However, for typical tire usage in line-haul fleets similar traction performance across a range of practical drive tire tread patterns has been found. (Source: National Research Council Canada, “Packed Snow Performance of Low Rolling Resistance Class 8 Heavy Truck Tires,” ST-GV-TR-0002, November 9, 2012)

* The practice of increasing load on the tire to enhance traction is not a guaranteed strategy for every condition a truck may encounter. Nonetheless, if a vehicle is immobilized, it is certainly logical since, in general, increasing the effective contact area helps traction.
* Reducing tire pressure at constant load is another approach that increases contact area, thereby increasing the coefficient of friction. However, since central tire inflation systems (CTIS) for drive tires are not currently common in over-the-road applications in North America, this may be viewed as an extreme remedy. (At the 2017 TMC Annual Meeting in Nashville, a session of the Future Truck Committee explored options for CTIS systems combined with load sensing to optimize tire pressure across a range of vehicle loads for improved wear, traction, and durability.)

## Load Biasing Systems

In some cases, fleets have installed manual dump valves that can be activated by the driver in low traction/low-speed conditions. Depending on how they are configured, these systems may permit high load transfer between non-driven and driven axles. Caution and good driver training must be used to prevent overloading of axles and tires, or to automatically rebalance axle loads once the vehicle is moving. The effectiveness of these systems depends on the driver’s understanding of how the system should be used and the consequences of misuse that can lead to tire or axle failure. At least one fleet interviewed by the study team prefers the manual system because it requires a greater level of driver awareness of conditions where traction may be limited.

Many fleets prefer the solution of an automated system for transferring load between axles. Pressure in the suspension air bags of the non-driven axle is automatically reduced based on input from the traction control system. Weight transfer is monitored in order to ensure that axle capacities are observed and load regulations are respected. One of the benefits of an automated system is that the transferred load will be maintained up to a given vehicle speed. Automated systems can also include a manual suspension height adjustment and manual load shifting capability below a set speed, giving the driver complete control of the system. Some systems will continue to hold a low-level weight bias on the drive tires for traction

<!-- page: 26 -->

purposes. It is critical that tire inflation pressure is set to the proper level to support potential increased load/speed combinations.

### A Note on Load Biasing and Fuel Economy

So far, we have considered cases of load biasing in order to transfer increased weight to the driven axle for improved traction. Load-biasing controls could potentially improve fuel economy of a vehicle operating under normal road conditions and speeds.

As demonstrated by at least one SuperTruck team, transferring weight to the axle fitted with tires that have the lowest rolling resistance can increase fuel economy at steady highway speeds. Several example cases of optimized weight distribution between the free-rolling and driven axles are presented in a patent publication in 2013 (Source: Clayton, W.B. and C. R. Bradley, “Improvement of vehicle fuel economy by optimizing effective tire rolling resistance,” patent publication WO/2013/119252A1, August 15, 2013). The patent uses the term “lift axle” to cover a range of fully lifting axles as well as load biasing configurations in which both axles of the 6x2 tractor tandem remain in contact with the ground.

Briefly, some of the main conclusions of the patent analysis were:

* When the free-rolling axle is fitted with tires that are lower in rolling resistance (such as trailer tires) than the drive tires mounted on the drive axle, the best fuel economy is obtained by transferring the maximum weight possible to the free-rolling axle.
* Using different tires on the driven and free-rolling axles, under certain conditions, it is more beneficial to maintain both of the 6x2 axles in contact with the road. Full lifting of the free-rolling axle generates higher rolling resistance from the drive tires in comparison with splitting the load between the two axles.
* In the case of a 6x2 axle configuration in which **both** the driven and non-driven axles are fitted with the same tires, it is most beneficial under all the operating conditions studied to maximize the load on the driven axle.
* For all the cases studied above, the load on the steer axle was taken as constant, given that current steer axle loads are generally dominated by the position of the engine and exhibit relatively low variation as vehicle payload varies.

It should be noted that none of the weight transfer systems currently available on the market transfer load to the non-driven axle. At least one OEM interviewed by this study team has analyzed and tested such a system and confirms the fuel-savings benefit. However, at this point, the study team is not aware of any intention of bringing this feature to production.

## Liftable 6x2

Since the initial NACFE 6x2 Confidence Report, the industry has seen the introduction of a 6x2 tandem axle with a liftable pusher axle. The 6x2 tandem tag axle systems do not have a liftable axle (liftable tag axles are not available due to potential stability issues that may result with truck configurations typical to North America) and are targeted at fleets requiring the 34,000 lb. axle carrying capacity of a tandem all

or most of the time. Many truckload carriers cannot accurately forecast the shipper’s loaded weight. Therefore, having the full tandem axle capacity makes sense for them.

For fleets with diminishing or variable loads where the full carrying capacity of a tandem axle (up to 34,000 lb.) is not needed always, the liftable tandem axle 6x2 configuration may be a more cost-effective solution. The U.S. bridge formula legal maximum weight rating for a single axle (the effective configuration with the pusher axle raised) is 20,000 lbs. Applications such as tank or bulk haulers and ones operating dry or refrigerated vans with diminishing, small, and/or empty loads such as package delivery, beverage, grocery, refuse, and less-than-truckload may benefit from this technology. Many of these customers operate on a local or regional basis and often have dedicated routes. Truckload or dedicated carriers hauling empty (dead-headed) could also benefit from this system. It is estimated that GCWRs of less than 60,000 lbs. represent 50–60% of on-highway miles traveled, and GCWRs between 60,000–70,000 lbs. comprise 15% of total miles traveled.

Unlike the 6x2 tandem axles studied in the 2013 report, where the drive axle is forward and the free-rolling axle is rearward (tag), the liftable tandem axle system has the drive axle in the rearward position. Currently only Volvo and Mack offer a liftable tandem axle system, branded Adaptive Loading at Volvo. Introduced in March 2015, Volvo described its product as “a new 6x2 liftable forward axle that automatically adjusts to load weight changes and offers 4x2 operation under certain conditions.” The Volvo product began production in 2016. Component supplier Hendrickson announced a similar system branded OPTIMAAX, marketed as a “Forward Liftable Tandem Axle,” in 2013. The 2013 system was not put into production and the system has undergone significant revision. A production announcement with a truck OEM is expected later in September 2017.

While operating in the down position, a liftable system performs like conventional 6x2 systems with one drive (live) axle and one free-rolling (dead) axle resulting in the same fuel economy benefits (2.5%) as the non-liftable tag axle systems. Both systems will have similar traction and tire wear when operated in the

<!-- page: 28 -->

down position. While in the down position automatic weight shifting sensors can be programed to distribute weight on both axles 50/50 or biased to the drive axle. This is fully automated and requires no driver intervention.

On-board sensors in the tandem suspension determine when the pusher axle is deployed (down) or retracted (raised). Users can pre-program their weight thresholds to determine when the lift axle is to be retracted. This is an automatic event without driver intervention. When the full capacity of a tandem axle is no longer needed the pusher axle is retracted off the road surface and allowed to free wheel in the raised position. A tandem axle (on a tractor or trailer) is bridge-formula legal up to 34,000 lbs. or 17,000 lbs. per axle. A single-drive axle can legally carry up to 20,000 lbs. With the pusher axle retracted, all of the weight on the tractor tandem is on the drive axle. In some conditions this, in combination with the fact that the rearward positioned axle is driven and thereby loaded first when backing under a trailer, may allow better traction to be maintained than with a 6x2 using a tag axle configuration. As with the automatic load transfer systems available for tag axles, the fifth wheel can also be lifted a few inches to unload the landing gear on the trailer making it easier to couple or uncouple a trailer. Also, similar to 6x2 tag axle systems, load shifting occurs to provide enhanced traction at the drive axle when sensing wheel slippage while the pusher axle is deployed.

## Benefits of Liftable 6x2 Axles

The study team spent considerable effort in trying to understand whether a liftable system provides a fuel consumption benefit and how this is possible. A common perception is that when an axle is raised, rolling resistance of the vehicle is reduced since fewer tires are on the ground. However, this perception is not supported by the facts. In simple terms, rolling resistance ($F_{RR}$) is a function of the rolling resistance coefficient ($C_{RR}$) of a tire and the load ($F_L$) on the tire as given by the following equation:

$$F_{RR} = C_{RR} * F_L \quad \text{(Equation 2)}$$

Therefore, when tires are lifted, the rolling resistance those tires would have generated is simply transferred to the remaining tires still in contact with the ground. The overall rolling resistance of the vehicle remains unchanged. As a matter of fact, since many fleets fit trailer tires on the free rolling axle, and trailer tires (with their reduced tread height and smoother tread face) generally have lower rolling resistance than drive tires, the overall average rolling resistance of a vehicle is actually increased when the free rolling axle is lifted. As mentioned in an earlier section, SuperTruck teams took advantage of this phenomenon and increased loading on the free rolling axle during steady highway speed operation to the benefit of about 1% in fuel consumption.

However, Volvo and Hendrickson both cited numerous customer testimonials to the fuel efficiency benefits of the liftable axle in long-haul duty cycles. They both also stated that customers who operate at least 30–35% of the time with the pusher in the retracted position could financially benefit from their liftable axle systems. Additionally, there are some studies available on liftable trailer axles pointing to fuel efficiency gains similar to what Volvo and Hendrickson claim for their systems.

To gain an insight as to how that is possible, the study team received help from Ricardo, the global engineering consultancy based in England. Ricardo has studied liftable axle systems, primarily for trailers in Europe. While a comprehensive engineering analysis was not performed, the study team engaged with several Ricardo engineers experienced in vehicle dynamics. They confirmed that vehicle rolling resistance

<!-- page: 29 -->

is not reduced when lifting an axle but pointed to several other factors that are improved including aerodynamics (a spinning tire in contact with the ground creates more drag than a stationary one where air is able to pass under the tire), brake drag, and bearing seal resistance. Based on this conversation as well as a few others, the study team came to the conclusion that there are a number of favorable factors with respect to fuel economy when an axle is lifted including:

* Aerodynamics
* Brake drag
* Bearing seal friction
* Rolling inertia that has to be overcome during acceleration
* Tire scrub in turns
* Imperfect axle alignment
* Suspension movement
* Tire slip in lightly loaded conditions
* Effect of mismatched tire diameters when using dual tires

The contribution to fuel economy from any one of these factors is small (fractions of one percent) but they can combine (in varying degrees based on duty cycle and the vehicle) to result in a large enough improvement to offset the unfavorable rolling resistance effect (when using trailer tires on the free rolling axle) to give fleets a measurable fuel consumption benefit. The study team did not receive any fleet data on the magnitude of the benefit. Volvo and Hendrickson claim an additional 2% improvement over non-lifting 6x2 systems. One fleet the study team spoke with pointed to a 0.2–0.3 mpg improvement when the axle was raised. Given the lack of data and small number of fleets using or testing liftable systems, it is difficult for the study team to assess the level of confidence in these numbers.

Additional benefits above a non-lifting 6x2 system may include:

* **Better Traction**—The ability to shift more weight to the drive axle by raising the pusher axle off the ground, and the positioning of the drive axle behind the fifth wheel can result in better traction in certain situations.
* **Improved tire life over 6x2 tag axle systems**—Tire life improves on the free rolling axle as well as on other tires due to more weight being transferred to the remaining axles when the liftable axle is raised resulting in less tire bounce, scrubbing, and/or squirm during lightly loaded conditions.
* **Improved drivability (i.e., better ride characteristics)**—By lifting the forward axle, additional weight is transferred to the tractor’s steer axle thus giving drivers a more loaded front axle with more positive steering feel. And with the axle in the up position, the truck has an effective longer wheelbase thus a potentially smoother ride.
* **Fewer axles mean lower tolls**—Tollways typically charge trucks per axle on the ground, so with the pusher axle retracted there are potential savings to the fleet. Most toll authorities recognize that the axle cannot be lifted manually so are granting toll relief. This savings can be even greater if a liftable axle suspension system is also installed on the trailer.
* **Fleet Image**—Liftable axle systems on the tractor and/or trailer are gaining popularity with fleets focused on greenhouse gas reduction since liftables, unlike 6x2 tag axle systems, provide a visible cue to the fleet’s commitment to improved fuel economy.

## Challenges of Liftable 6x2 Axles

Liftable systems do have some challenges versus standard 6x2 systems.

* **Increased cost and weight compared to 6x2 tag axle systems**—The pusher axle requires a more expensive drop center axle for the prop shaft to connect to the live axle plus there are additional components needed to raise and lower the pusher axle.
* **Additional component costs**—Due to the higher potential of front axle loading when the lift axle is retracted, some fleets spec a heavier capacity front axle along with higher rated kingpins, bushing, and shock absorbers plus heavier front suspension components and higher capacity tires (load range H is rated to 14,000 lbs.—ratings above H require different tire sizes) are required or recommended. One supplier also requires disc brakes in lieu of drum brakes and recommends a sway bar on the front axle.
* **Legal restrictions**—Utah does not allow liftable axles that are not steerable and most of Canada has restrictions that are not met by liftable axles. Other regulatory limitations may also exist (the study team did not research this topic extensively as the laws may be changing).

Since liftable systems are very recent, there is no residual value comparison between standard 6x2s and liftables. Given their advantages in some duty cycles and the fact that they are priced at a premium (Volvo and Mack), even when compared to equivalent 6x4s, the residual value may vary considerably from that of the early 6x2 systems entering the used market today.

## Driver Communication and Training

Almost all technologies require some driver communication and training to ensure a smooth and lasting implementation. Even automated transmissions or downsped powertrains, features thought to make a driver’s job easier (or quieter), endured some level of driver resistance at early adopting fleets. The same is true today with 6x2 systems. Not only do drivers require an understanding of how the systems function and the best way to handle them in challenging conditions, many drivers start with a strong negative perception of 6x2s. Some fleets interviewed by the study team shared anecdotal information on potential driver recruits that refused to drive for them when the recruits found out that the fleet had 6x2s.

As was pointed out in the 2013 study, even drivers that have not heard specific feedback on 6x2 systems may start with a bias, equating a switch from a 6x4 to a 6x2 to a change from a 4x4 to a 4x2 pickup truck. Drivers may view this change as having something taken away or even worse, as the fleet sacrificing driver safety in pursuit of greater profit.

Fleets must implement a thorough communications and training plan to offset such biases. Major points that should be addressed include:

* A more efficient fleet is at an advantage over its competitors, which is a benefit to all employees. Technologies that the industry has not yet widely adopted, such as 6x2s, helps build that advantage.

<!-- page: 31 -->

* There is no evidence that 6x2s are less safe. Fleets that have implemented 6x2s report no safety issues. In fact, many believe they are more stable in slippery highway conditions.
* Traction issues can be addressed with the right combination of training and technologies including load shifting, traction control, and locking differentials.

The fleets that have been successful implementing 6x2s are ones known for achieving high fuel efficiency. In some cases, drivers receive an incentive for achieving fuel efficiency goals, which may help offset a feeling by drivers that something is being taken away from them. For fleets not already using a fuel incentive, implementing an ongoing incentive system to adopt a new technology is not a likely option. There are other methods to give drivers a stake in the successful implementation of the system including one-time bonuses for completing training and using the system or additional equipment geared to driver comfort (afforded by the weight savings of the 6x2).

With respect to training, the focus should be on the load-shifting technology, specific techniques for handling poor traction situations, and understanding the factors that accelerate tire wear. Fleets can also use this training as an opportunity to reinforce the fact that the driver must make the decision on when conditions are unsafe to operate the vehicle. If the load-shifting system has a manual mode (available on all systems but may be an option on some), it is also critical for a driver to understand the situations where a manual override can be advantageous. Some truck OEMs and axle, and load shifting manufacturers offer some driver tips that may be a good starting point for a training program.

## Future Developments

The fuel-savings potential combined with a skeptical industry on the technology has spurred some interesting innovations with respect to 6x2s.

**Dana Dual-Range Disconnect:** Dana announced this system in April 2015 at the Mid-America Trucking Show as a variation on the 6x2 with the added benefit of downspeeding but without the 6x2 traction issues. The system is comprised of two drive axles connected with an inter-axle prop shaft, exactly as with a standard 6x4 but with the ability to mechanically disconnect the inter-axle shaft and internal gearing of the tag axle when operating at higher speeds. The benefit is 6x4 operation at low speeds and 6x2 efficiency at all other times. Additionally, through the use of different axle ratios in each of the driven axles, when the inter-axle shaft is disconnected, the vehicle operates in a highly downsped mode, but only at higher speeds when downspeeding is most beneficial and least likely to damage the driveline. The benefits come at the cost of greater mechanical complexity and weight versus a 6x2 or even a 6x4. The system is still in prototype form although available for demonstration drives. Dana was not able to share any data relative to the fuel consumption benefit but claims 2–5% in a line-haul duty cycle. Pricing and a production date have not been announced.

The study team also had the opportunity to speak with two smaller highly innovative companies. One, Hydraulic Innovations (hydraulicinnovationsllc.com), sees the disadvantages of the 6x2 platform as an opportunity. They have patents on several concepts. One uses idler wheels between the driven and free-rolling axle to provide low-speed traction while another accomplishes the same with hydraulic motors. Both have the ability to lift the free-rolling axle during low load conditions. Hydraulic Innovations has not built any full-scale prototypes and it is much too early to assess the impact on fuel consumption, weight, or maintenance, but the ideas point to possible solutions that could help the industry overcome some of the concerns with 6x2s.

Hyliion (hyliion.com), an early stage start-up based in Pittsburgh, is further along in its development. Its innovation is not directly aimed at the efficiency gains of a 6x2, but rather has the potential to use 6x2s as a platform for even greater gains. They have developed an aftermarket system to hybridize tractor-trailer combinations using the trailer as the platform for capturing regenerative braking energy during downhill portions of a trip through a motor/generator on one of the axles and sending that power back to the truck during uphill portions when fuel consumption is greatest. As is implied in its name, the technology uses a lithium-ion battery to store the energy. Once the recovered energy is stored in a battery, an algorithm determines how, where, and how quickly it is used. Ideally, the energy is released when the diesel engine is at its least efficient or it can be saved to serve as an APU to avoid idling during long stops which also allows it to qualify for the 400 lb. weight exemption.

As mentioned, the system is configured to be retrofitted to a trailer but it does not take much imagination to realize that its utilization, thereby ROI, will be greater when fitted to a tractor, and a 6x2 platform would fit the concept well while also providing 6x4 traction when needed. Fleets appear to be interested—the company has about half a dozen systems in the field and is receiving input and investment from some major names in the industry. Hyliion expects to be in production by the end of the summer of 2017.

## Conclusions

6x2 systems, while offering an undeniable fuel consumption improvement, are still not widely viewed as a net positive by the majority of the line-haul industry. Adoption rates have been less than anticipated by the 2013 study team. Many early implementations suffered from high tire wear combined with poor residual values that more than offset the fuel consumption gains. While new 6x2 axles, in particular liftables, entering the market may reduce some of the challenges related to 6x2s, there is limited feedback from fleets at this time.

### 6x2 Tag Axle Systems

At current fuel prices, given the potential fuel-savings and the volatile nature of fuel pricing, NACFE recommends that fleets gain knowledge of the systems and test not only the technology but also their internal processes for managing engine parameters and driver communication and training. Implementations should include a full Generation II system and following NACFE’s recommendations for tire fitment. As fuel prices return closer to their five-year average, these fleets will be in a better position to determine their actual ROI on these systems.

<!-- page: 33 -->

### 6x2 Lift Axle Systems

For fleets that are below 60,000 lbs. GCWR at least 30% of the time, NACFE recommends that fleets consider testing a liftable 6x2. Tire wear will be accelerated as with a tag axle system, and fleets should follow recommendations and best practices to achieve the best return on investment.

### Conclusions regarding all 6x2 systems:

* Drive tire wear on 6x2s will not achieve parity with 6x4s. However, measures can be taken to reduce the accelerated wear to the range of 20–35% versus 6x4s. Selecting a retread trailer tire for the free-rolling axle may result in the lowest-cost option for fleets.
* Fleets that take a system-wide approach and implement the full Generation II package including load shifting technology, traction control, and engine parameters to limit torque in low gear at clutch engagement and under engine braking have a much better chance to realize the ROI potential of 6x2s. This may be even truer of the Generation III liftable systems. Given the premium position of liftable systems, fleets that optimize spec’s to maximize lift time are likely to achieve a better return on investment.
* Good and consistent driver communication and training is critical with this technology. Automated load shifting/axle lifting technology, while it does make a driver’s job easier, is not a substitute for good training. A common complaint is that 6x2 systems are less safe. This is not supported by any data. In fact, fleets that use the systems believe 6x2s are potentially more stable in slippery highway conditions since the non-driven axle can serve as a rudder to prevent power slides. Drivers should understand the overall benefit of 6x2s, how the systems work, and when a manual intervention is beneficial. Given the negative perception 6x2s have in the industry, this may be more important now than ever. Fleets may also find that giving drivers a stake in the success of 6x2s can help to overcome initial negative perceptions. NACFE has not studied the benefits of incentive systems relative to fuel consumption. However, in this case, one-time incentives can be designed around completion of training, use of 6x2-equipped vehicles over a period of time, or a myriad of other similar goals.
* As happens occasionally with technologies, the baseline (traditional 6x4 systems) is also improving. High-efficiency drive axles reduce the potential efficiency gains of a 6x2 system albeit at a greater cost.
* Residual values for 6x2s remain a problem. The study team believes these are particularly low due to the reputation of Generation I systems. Fleets that have persisted and implemented Generation II 6x2s do not report a problem with residual values, although these are typically smaller fleets and would not turn over many trucks at once. As discussed above, it is too early to know how Generation III systems will fare on the used market, but for now, they are sold as a premium product even to a 6x4.
