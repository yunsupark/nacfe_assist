1 INTRODUCTION

Successful freight movement requires timely delivery of goods from a vendor to a recipient, often using multiple carriers. Ensuring that freight is delivered to the end recipient in the condition the vendor intends requires ensuring the goods being delivered are handled according to the vendor’s specifications throughout their journey. This may require maintaining product orientation (“THIS END UP!”), package integrity (“LIFT HERE”; “DO NOT STACK MORE THAN THREE HIGH”), avoidance of shock, duration of shipment, arrival time at recipient location, etc. See Figure 1.

And many shipments must be kept within a specified temperature range from the point of origin to the final destination.

Careful temperature control — the cold chain — has enabled long-distance shipping of temperature-sensitive goods. According to the recent book *Frostbite*, “nearly three-quarters of everything on the average American plate is processed, packaged, shipped, stored, and/or sold under refrigeration” [122]. Temperature-controlled vehicles — also known as reefers — ensure that freight is kept at the proper temperature throughout its journey. Reefers keep freight within a prescribed temperature range by providing refrigeration or heat as needed. While many loads — such as ice cream — require cooling to keep them in the correct temperature range, some loads — such as fresh produce or electronics in freezing weather — require heating to keep them above ambient temperature.

With careful temperature control, shipment of some goods can be moved to lower-cost shipping methods. For example, carriers are successfully shipping flowers by sea rather than air, significantly reducing transportation costs and emissions while still maintaining their freshness [46]. In addition to providing temperature control in transport, refrigerated trailers — known as “reefer vans” — are widely used as temporary or supplementary stationary cold storage (e.g., for turkeys in November and ice cream in the summer) [107].

There also are some surprising uses for refrigerated shipping. Lay’s Potato Chips bags are filled with gas to provide the right amount of volume to protect the chips from breakage in handling at their final destination. Bags destined for lower altitudes must be refrigerated when the shipping route passes through high altitudes to prevent the bags from popping [118].

Temperature control in shipments using vans, trucks, and trailers is enabled by a transport refrigeration unit, or TRU. Refrigerated vehicles generally can be identified by the presence of the TRU, a large device located at the front end of the truck box or trailer, as seen in Figure 3, or on top of a cargo van.

In some cases, such as direct-to-consumer shipments of medicine or frozen steaks, temperature control is provided within the shipment packaging itself by using insulation and dry ice or other cold material, and shipping in a dry truck (a truck without temperature control).

Today TRUs are powered almost exclusively by internal combustion engines. TRUs for larger trucks and all trailers are powered by dedicated, compact diesel engines. These are called self-powered TRUs. Smaller trucks — generally Class 4 and below — use power take-off from the traction engine. These are called vehicle-powered TRUs [121]. Gasoline vehicles typically use vehicle-powered TRUs [121].

The emissions and noise associated with TRUs are subject to regulation. The most relevant set of regulations has been put in place by the California Air Resources Board (CARB). These regulations, summarized in Section 14 of this report, require that all self-powered TRUs on all straight trucks be zero emission — which almost certainly means electric — by 2030. TRU electrification outside of California is being driven by companies proactively working to reduce their emissions, and those preparing for the spread of California rules and regulations to other US states and to Canada and Mexico, as well as to satisfy export markets.

In addition, as part of the broader movement toward truck electrification, non-regulatory factors are expanding interest in electric TRUs (eTRUs). These include corporate sustainability goals, customer demand for lower-emission shipments, integration with electric trucks, noise reduction and air quality improvements at customer sites and depots, as well as pursuing incentives and rebates for early technology adopters.

This report provides basic information about TRUs and eTRUs and identifies and discusses key challenges and opportunities facing the trucking industry and its support system as the CARB eTRU rules come into effect, and as the industry begins to transition away from fossil-fuel powered refrigeration.

5.2 A BRIEF HISTORY OF REFRIGERATION AND REFRIGERATED TRANSPORT

This report uses the term refrigeration as shorthand for temperature control — keeping the temperature of freight within the range specified by the shipper. Thus, as we are using it, refrigeration may require both heating and cooling to be applied during shipment, depending on the ambient temperature and desired temperature to be maintained.

Refrigerated truck transportation is one part of the large, complex, cold chain that maintains control of many environmental elements beyond simply temperature, particularly for sensitive produce being shipped long distances or stored in warehouses for long periods of time. These may include maintaining a particular level of humidity or — in the case of some fruits — controlling the mix of gases to prevent or enhance ripening. Some of these controls need to be maintained in reefer trucks and vans.

The cold chain is the immense and largely invisible and underappreciated system that allows grocery stores to have mangos, bananas, apple, asparagus, and other foods year-round even in areas where none of those foods can be grown. It allows people in Topeka, Kansas, to grill lamb chops from New Zealand any time they wish, and it allows temperature sensitive vaccines and other medications to be shipped safely around the world.

The 2024 book *Frostbite: How Refrigeration Changed Our Food, Our Planet, and Ourselves* provides a detailed history of refrigeration [122]. The summary here is informed by that book.

Most food deteriorates quickly at ambient temperature. Hunter-gatherers who had more meat, fish or other foods than they could immediately use had many ways to preserve food: pickling, salting, fermenting, drying, smoking, adding sugar. These and other techniques resulted in many foods we still enjoy, including sauerkraut, beef jerky, smoked salmon, salt cod, wine, jam and jelly, cheese, ghee, olive oil, crackers, kimchi, raisins, ham, and salami. Many of these techniques pre-date written history, with evidence of dried meat from more than 14,000 years ago found in the Middle East.

The need for armies and sailors to carry food with them spurred development of the next major anti-spoilage technology: canning (placing fully cooked food in a sealed container and then heating that container in boiling water for a period of time). The development of canning was spurred by a cash prize offered by the French government in 1795. By 1803, food including beef, beans, peas, peaches, corn, and fish was canned in glass jars and found to be tasty and safe months later. Special canning jars and metal cans were soon invented, and both home and commercial canning became widespread.

However, the quest for better food preservation continued, as canned food — while perfectly fine — was not as appealing as fresh food. For thousands of years, humans had been extending the life of fresh food using naturally occurring cold in the form of caves, root cellars, and ice and snow. The first evidence of the use of ice and snow to keep food cool dates back almost 4,000 years to present-day Syria, China, Greece, and Rome. Because of the expense of moving ice and snow, and its rapid melting, it was not used for everyday foods, but for luxury foods for the elite.

The use of ice slowly became more widespread but accelerated rapidly when people took advantage of the ice on New England’s many lakes starting in the early 1800s. Ice was harvested on lakes, stored in ice houses using sawdust and double walls as insulation. By 1879, “an estimated eight million tons of ice were harvested annually” in the US and were used for everything from making ice cream to beer making to preserving fish caught at sea [122]. This demand for natural ice spurred the development of ice making machinery.

By the turn of the 20th century, ice making machinery was becoming well established, and allowed cold storage warehouses. However, cold transport relied on ice until Fred McKinley Jones “invented the world’s first truly mobile mechanical refrigeration unit” in 1937 [122]. The diesel-powered refrigeration unit — named the Thermo King — caught on after World War II. Reliable food transport from rural farms to urban markets transformed the rural economy and led to supermarkets filled with convenient and inexpensive food that would otherwise not be available.

Further development of refrigeration technology throughout the 20th and 21st centuries allowed food and other goods to be reliably stored, transported, and delivered at any temperature a customer desired.

5.3 HOW TRUS WORK

TRUs create cold or warm air which is then pumped into a box that needs to be at a certain temperature to protect its contents. There are several ways to do this. Diesel fuel has been the primary energy source for TRUs for the past century but there are several lower emissions technologies emerging.

5.3.1 Vapor-compression refrigeration

The most widely used method of refrigeration is known as vapor-compression refrigeration (VCR). VCR is used for air conditioning in buildings, domestic freezers and refrigerators, food warehousing, as well as essentially all modes of transportation [49]. VCR is a closed system with a working fluid called a refrigerant. This refrigerant, combined with a compressor and heat exchangers, allows a closed space to be controlled at a temperature that is significantly different than the outside environment.

A compressor circulates refrigerant from evaporator coils inside the freight compartment to condenser coils outside the truck. The refrigerant is forced through an expansion valve turning it from a liquid into a cool liquid-vapor mix before entering the evaporator, where a fan circulates air over the evaporator coils to remove heat from the freight compartment. This vapor is compressed, and enters the condenser coils, which transfers the heat to the outside air. The cooled refrigerant is returned to the evaporator coils via an expansion valve to continue the cooling cycle. This cycle is illustrated in Figure 6. Note that if the condenser is air-cooled, it generally will have a fan as well.

5.3.2 Electric TRUs

The primary way to eliminate emissions from TRUs is to replace the diesel-fueled ICE driving the compressor with an electric motor. These so-called electric transport refrigeration units, or eTRUs, are generally powered by either the traction battery when installed on an electric straight truck chassis or by dedicated batteries when installed on a trailer. Smaller units may be powered from the alternator/battery under the hood of an internal combustion engine while the vehicle is in motion and via grid electricity when available to run the eTRU. When the vehicle is not moving, eTRUs using batteries can be charged.

One operational advantage of eTRUs is that if electricity is available where the vehicle is parked for delivery, loading, or staging, it can be plugged into “shore power.” This not only preserves battery power (traction battery or eTRU battery) for on the road use or vehicle fuel for eTRUs power by power take-offs from an ICE. This can provide a significant cost savings in comparison to diesel TRUs.

Powering eTRUs, including the use of shore power is discussed below in more detail.

5.3.2.1 Other Zero-emission TRU Technologies

CARB has published information on zero-emission TRUs in addition to eTRUs powered by batteries [6] [100] [101]. None of these are widely used in general refrigerated transport because of availability issues, practicality, or total cost [104]. These include:

- **Cryogenic systems** using liquid nitrogen or other cryogenic agents as a coolant. In such a system, the cryogen is either injected directly into the freight compartment at a rate needed to keep the compartment in the specified temperature range or is circulated through a heat exchanger to remove heat from the freight compartment. Challenges include availability and cost of equipment, cost of replacement of the cryogen, and safety issues related to handling cryogens [101].
- **Reusable cold plates** that are frozen when the vehicle is charging. Cold plates essentially are relatively thin sheet metal enclosures holding both refrigeration coils and a liquid that will freeze. The plates are frozen overnight when the truck is not in use by running refrigerant through them using a grid-connected external refrigeration unit. The plates can keep the truck’s freight compartment cold during the truck’s duty cycle. The plates are plugged back in when the truck’s route is completed. Challenges include weight, unfamiliarity with the technology, and limited cooling capacity [101].
- **eTRUs powered by hydrogen fuel cells rather than batteries.** The electricity to power the eTRU can be provided by hydrogen fuel cells rather than rechargeable batteries. Challenges include the weight and cost of the fuel tanks, the lack of hydrogen fueling infrastructure, the cost of hydrogen, and potential safety hazards associated with operating hydrogen fuel cells in a seaport facility or intermodal railyard [101].

5.3.3 Heating

To keep the cargo within a specified temperature range, at times a TRU may need to provide heat as well as cooling, for instance when the ambient temperature is below the low end of the specified temperature range. TRUs — including eTRUs — can provide both cooling and heat to meet temperature requirements. Some TRUs use the heat from the refrigeration cycle and direct it to heating the freight container, and others use electric resistance heating. The application and unit architecture for future units may use similar or combined methods for temperature control [121].

7 ENVIRONMENTAL IMPACTS OF OPERATING TRUS

The primary environmental impacts of TRUs come from three sources: Air emissions associated with powering the TRU, noise, and refrigerant leakage.

7.1 EMISSIONS FROM OPERATING TRUS

Emissions from dedicated diesel engines powering TRUs or by the vehicle engine’s powering alternators or compressors can be major sources of unwanted emissions, including particulate matter (PM2.5), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), and greenhouse gases (GHG) [2]. CARB states, “Although TRU engines are relatively small, ranging from 9 to 36 horsepower, significant numbers of these engines congregate at distribution centers, truck stops, and other facilities, result in the potential for health risks to those that live and work nearby [27].” These emissions are the primary driver for electrifying TRUs.

7.2 TRU NOISE

Although not a core focus of this report, the noise produced by truck refrigeration units is a concern, particularly in communities where deliveries are made in close proximity to residences or for stationary reefer vans parked near residential areas [60]. This report will note how electrification of TRUs significantly reduces reefer noise.

7.3 REFRIGERANT LEAKAGE

The refrigerants used in trucking are generally the same as those used in stationary applications. The refrigerant is circulated in a sealed system under high pressure through many coils. Wear and tear on seals in the compressor, as well as corrosion on the coils, can result in leakage [51].

Many refrigerants have very high global warming potential (GWP). GWP is a unitless measure of how powerful a greenhouse gas a particular material is relative to carbon dioxide, which is defined as having a GWP of 1.

The most common refrigerant in use, R-22, has a GWP of 1,810, meaning that one pound of leaked R-22 has the same GWP as almost a ton of carbon dioxide [52]. Both CARB and the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) have programs both to reduce refrigerant leakage and to shift users of refrigerants to those with lower GWPs [52] [53] [78] [96]. These new refrigerants have no impact on TRU performance [78]. These regulations apply to all TRUs. While refrigerant type is not a focus of this report, it is an important context of which a user of refrigeration should be aware.

9 PRIMARY TYPES OF TRUS AVAILABLE TODAY

There are four types of power configurations used for TRUs today [93]. In all cases, the primary use for power is the compressor. In addition, fans and control equipment consume some power.

9.1 CONVENTIONAL TRUS

Compressors in conventional TRUs are powered by an internal combustion engine. In larger TRUs on box trucks or reefer trailers, these engines are integrated into the TRU housing and fuel using a separate dedicated fuel tank.

As noted above, smaller TRU compressors can be mounted in the engine compartment and driven directly from the engine using a belt drive, like that used for an alternator or water pump. The coolant is circulated to the TRU.

9.2 ELECTRIC TRUS

> In a standby electric TRU (also called a standby plug-in eTRU), the refrigerator’s compressor is both mechanically driven by an integral diesel engine and electrically driven by an integral electric motor. 11.1 FACTORS DRIVING TRU SELECTION

TRUs are selected based on their cooling capacity, which needs to be sufficient to keep the product at the required temperature throughout the time it is in the truck box or trailer. Ken Marko, fleet sustainability senior manager at US Foods, summarizes the use of reefers in US Foods operations as having three distinct phases before they leave the depot [113]:

1. **Pre-cooling:** Before cold cargo is loaded, the temperature inside the truck or trailer needs to be brought to its final operating temperature by running the TRU. Pre-cooling usually takes place when the truck or trailer is parked away from the loading dock. Pre-cooling can require a lot of energy and is one of the places where an eTRU plugged into the grid can reduce operating costs significantly. However, as noted in Section 13.3.2, providing shore power in a truck yard is challenging.
2. **Loading:** After the truck or trailer is pre-cooled, it is moved to the warehouse’s loading dock for loading. Because the warehouses of distributors of refrigerated or frozen goods are themselves refrigerated, it may not be necessary for the truck or trailer’s TRU to be running while it is being loaded. However, it is relatively easy to provide shore power at a loading dock.
3. **Staging:** After the truck or trailer is loaded, it is moved from the loading dock to a staging area in the yard to await the driver and/or tractor to take it from the depot to carry out its delivery duties. Depending on the particulars of the load and route, a truck or trailer may be in the staging area for many hours, with the TRU running. This is another area where an eTRU plugged into the grid can reduce operating costs significantly. However, as noted in Section 13.3.2, providing shore power in a truck yard is challenging.

The factors to consider when selecting a TRU vary greatly with the duty cycle and application [108]. These include:

- Quality of insulation
- Number and duration of door openings
- Quality of door seal (roll up vs. swing doors)
- Delivery to a refrigerated vs. not refrigerated warehouse (including cross-dock deliveries)
- Operating temperatures and relative size of temperature-controlled zones (refrigerated, frozen, ambient)
- Ambient temperature
- Size of trailer (28’, 38’, 48’, 53’)
- Availability of shore power at the loading dock
- Length of time parked in the yard
- Direct sun exposure

A 28’ trailer with an operating temperature of -20°F with 10 door openings a day will require a TRU with a higher cooling capacity than will a 53’ trailer with a 35°F operating temperature. Depending on the carrier, a given reefer truck or trailer may carry loads with different set points on different days and fleets will want to size their TRU to be able to handle all anticipated loads.

## 13 BARRIERS TO EXPANDED ETRU USE

There are numerous barriers to the expanded use of eTRUs. These include both operational and cost 
challenges.

### 13.1 OPERATIONAL CHALLENGES OF ETRUS

Most of the operational challenges of eTRUs are related to meeting the energy requirements of the TRU 
compressor with electricity as opposed to diesel fuel. While the right technology in the right application 
works well, trying to use eTRUs in applications they are not suited for will lead to frustration and failure. 
This section discusses the challenges of eTRUs that must be considered by a potential user.

#### 13.1.1 Battery Size

A diesel-fueled TRU is supplied by a dedicated fuel tank that typically holds 50 gallons of fuel. Depending 
on the load and its requirements, that fuel can last several days, and can be readily and quickly 
replenished wherever the truck or tractor is fueled. When parked and running in a yard, waiting to be 
unloaded, or a fully loaded reefer trailer waiting to be picked up, or precooling prior to loading, it can be 
easily fueled. The fuel has a cost, but it is easy to manage and readily available.

In contrast, an eTRU has a limited run time on its battery. Because of the cost and weight of batteries, 
users of eTRUs want to use the right sized battery for the duty cycle. Having too large a battery adds 
both unnecessary capital cost and weight. Having too small a battery risks failure during the duty cycle. 
Reefer trailers for food tend to be heavy, so keeping tare weight low is important. Because the energy 
required to keep the load at the correct temperature varies greatly with the duty cycle and application, 
selecting the optimal battery size is a challenge. 

In contrast, a diesel-powered TRU will simply run through its fuel faster or slower with different duty 
cycles and applications. Although using additional diesel fuel adds operational cost, it allows a degree of 
operational flexibility not offered by battery eTRUs.

It is technically feasible to run eTRUs using hydrogen fuel cells, however, these present both operational 
and economic challenges, as noted in Section 5.3.2.1.

#### 13.1.2 Weight

All-electric eTRUs require batteries and power electronics in addition to the eTRU itself. These result in a 
weight penalty compared to conventional TRUs. CARB estimates this weight penalty for a reefer trailer 
as 728 lbs. [94]. However, industry experts estimate the weight penalty as 2,000 to 2,500 lbs. [107]. 
While federal regulations provide for an additional 2,000-lb. weight allowance for electric-powered 
tractor-trailers, the regulations do not address the extra weight that may be associated with an all-electric reefer trailer [81]. Reefer trailers for food tend to be heavy and often weigh out, so it’s 
important to keep tare weight low [108].

For reefer straight trucks, weight is important as well.

#### 13.1.3.1 Operational Changes Related to Energy Efficiency Importance

Energy efficiency always has been important to reefer operations. Unnecessarily extended door 
openings, leaky doors, and skimpy insulation waste energy. For a diesel-powered TRU, this is a cost 
issue: burning more fuel than necessary to keep the TRU running costs money. However, it does not put 
the delivery operation at risk. More fuel means an extra fill-up of the tank, but with the size of the fuel 
tank and the ease and ubiquity of fueling, that is a minor issue.

On the other hand, for battery-powered TRUs, the energy supply is inherently limited — batteries are 
sized to last a single shift as battery size has a large impact on both cost and weight. And charging a 
battery takes both much more time and cannot be done in as many locations as filling a diesel tank. This 
means that keeping doors closed, seals well maintained, and plugging into shore power for temperature 
pulldown and maintenance at loading docks and in yards are all critically important to assuring eTRU 
batteries can provide the cooling required for a shift. It also may require moving from rollup doors to 
swinging doors, because of the better seal they provide. In addition, the driver will have to avoid quick 
acceleration and learn how to properly use regenerative braking [11].

In those cases where the eTRU is powered from the traction battery — such as on an electric box truck 
— the driver must be aware of how to drive to not unnecessarily deplete the traction battery. And for 
all-electric reefer trailers parked in a yard, making sure they are kept plugged in can be a challenge 
(assuming there is even shore power in the yard). Reefer trailers that are parked and operating for 
extended periods of time, such as those used for temporary stationary cold storage at grocery stores, 
are excellent candidates for eTRUs if, and only if, shore power is available to allow them always to be 
connected to grid electricity.

While none of these changes are particularly difficult, ensuring the cultural changes required within a 
fleet to make them second nature takes time, patience, and dedication.

#### 13.1.3.2 Proper Training

Electric TRUs run at a much higher voltage than the 12V or 24V electricity used on a diesel truck. They 
operate at 230V or 408V and may be powered from electric truck traction batteries operating at 400V or 
800V or more. In addition, as noted above, there are differences in operating eTRUs. Thus, it is critical 
that dealers working to supply their fleet customers with eTRUs are properly trained. For example, 
Thermo King recently announced an Electrification Readiness Program [79]. This program focuses on the 
following areas.

* Providing Thermo King technicians with knowledge of electrification and additional training in 
decarbonization solutions,
* Providing dealerships with knowledge so they can ensure electric charging and power is 
available at the fleet location,
* Ensuring dealerships have a sufficient understanding of regulations and managing risks in 
shifting to eTRUs to provide viable consultative skills at dealerships in eTRU technology, and
* Ensuring dealerships and customers are trained in and equipped for safely operating and 
maintaining eTRUs.

### 13.2 COST CHALLENGES OF ETRUS

There are a variety of cost challenges associated with moving to eTRUs. These include the initial 
purchase price, which is higher than that of a diesel-powered TRU. In addition, there is the cost of the 
electric charging infrastructure needed to support the battery-electric eTRU units as well as the cost of a 
shore power connection.

#### 13.2.1 Initial Cost

PLM’s Gassler stated that a new diesel 53’ TRU reefer trailer costs PLM about $110,000 to $115,000. A 
new standby-electric eTRU reefer trailer costs them about $130,000 to $145,000. An all-electric eTRU 
reefer trailer costs about $170,000 to $250,000, depending on the manufacturer. The actual “out the 
door cost” paid by a particular carrier will depend on a range of factors, such as federal, state, local, and 
utility incentive available. These differ widely by location and can change frequently. Manufacturers and 
vendors of eTRUs should have this information available to potential customers.

#### 13.2.2 Charging Infrastructure and Shore Power Cost

CARB estimates the electric infrastructure cost for a battery-electric eTRU as ranging from $4,300 to 
$5,700 [101]. It is not clear whether this estimate includes a shore power connection, or only a charger. 
In addition to charging infrastructure, a facility may face a significant cost for getting electricity to a site 
that does not currently have sufficient electrical service. In addition, a facility may wish to either charge 
or provide shore power to electric reefer trailers when parked in the yard. To avoid the challenges of 
collisions between vehicles and electric infrastructure, some adopters of eTRUs have had the cords drop 
from overhead, adding additional cost.

This challenge is exacerbated by the lack of compatibility between the connectors and standards used 
for charging electric trucks, for charging eTRU batteries, and providing shore power to eTRUs. The 
current systems have all evolved separately, resulting in increased challenges for designing systems that 
will provide for these different electrical requirements in a coherent manner. As discussed below, work 
toward converging these systems is starting, but there is a long way to go.

#### 13.3.2 Shore Power Availability

Shore power promises significant fuel savings for standby electric and hybrid electric TRUs, battery load 
relief for all-electric eTRUs, and essential yard power for all-electric reefer trailers.

However, shore power is both limited in its availability and subject to a variety of incompatible voltage 
and connector standards. TMC is working on recommended practices (RP) for shore power in RP185, 
which is under the auspices of TMC Study Group 1 (S.1 – Electrical). TMC was expected to establish a 
process for developing RPs for charging eTRUs at its fall 2024 meeting. The development of this RP is 
expected to be jointly housed under S.11 (Sustainability & Environmental Technologies) and S.7 
(Trailers, Bodies & Material Handling). The current thinking is that the charger RP process will start its 
work by focusing on both efficiency of truck refrigeration and energy transfer [112].

In addition, the physical placement of chargers and shore power in depot yards can be challenging, as 
any infrastructure in a yard is subject to damage from vehicle traffic. This may be addressed by elevating 
the infrastructure and having the cords drop down from overhead.

Shore power at a depot may not only be of value for outbound freight operations but also may be 
important to inbound carriers delivering cargo using eTRUs. NACFE spoke to a large private carrier which 
uses very few reefers in its own operations but does receive some materials delivered using reefers. As 
of August 2024, this carrier does not permit suppliers to use power at their depot. However, they 
acknowledge that as regulations change, this policy may change.

Prologis, which owns many logistics warehouses, and provides truck electrification services through its 
Prologis Mobility operation, understands this issue and works with its customers to provide both shore 
power and charging for eTRUs [116].

Another location where shore power might be beneficial is at grocery store loading docks. The 2022 
California Green Nonresidential Building Standards Code (CALGreen) contains “charging readiness 
requirements for warehouses, grocery stores and retail stores, office buildings, and manufacturing 
facilities with planned off-street loading spaces” suitable for charging medium- and heavy-duty trucks 
[117]. However, there are no readiness requirements for providing charging for eTRUs or for shore 
power. This points yet again to the value of integrating the connector and electrical standards for 
vehicle charging, eTRU charging, and shore power.

Long-haul reefer operations can, in theory, be enabled using eAxle trailers (see section 12.2). This would 
require them to reliably be plugged into shore power at truck stops while a driver is on break or 
hoteling. However, shore power suitable for eTRUs is currently rarely available at truck stops. This is yet 
another motivation for integrating standards for charging trucks and reefers and for providing shore 
power for both.

Because there is little to no demand for on-road charging of eTRUs, neither Terawatt nor WattEV offer

Terawatt’s sites are “set up to support ultra-fast day cab charging,” as that is what their customers are

#### 13.3.5 Diesel Backup for eTRUs

The value of the cargo in a fully loaded reefer can be very high. For example, the average value of a 
trailer load of pharmaceuticals is around $100,000 and can rise much higher — to well over $10 million 
for a single trailer [89] [90]. While theft presents one threat to such shipments, so does loss of 
temperature control. Many pharmaceuticals are highly sensitive to temperature and can be ruined by 
reefer failure. Similarly, shipments of food can be ruined if a reefer shipment fails on a very hot or cold 
day.

Conventional reefers have plenty of fuel, allowing them to continue to run even if a journey is 
significantly delayed by a severe traffic bottleneck, road closure, or storm. If the journey takes longer 
than expected, the driver can simply refill the reefer tank. The reefer can operate overnight while the 
driver sleeps.

For all-electric reefers, delivery delays can present significant challenges. In vehicle delay or breakdown 
situations, the limited run time provided by the batteries, the difficulty in finding shore power or battery 
charging on the road, and the inability of an eAxle to generate power when the vehicle is not moving all 
raise concerns for shippers. To address these concerns, most eTRUs provide a diesel generator backup 
option that will automatically kick in if the batteries become depleted. While this backup may never kick 
in, it provides shippers with the assurance that they can safely use all-electric TRUs.

## 14 CARB AND OTHER REGULATIONS

California Air Resources Board (CARB) has issued regulations governing emissions from TRUs in an 
attempt to address the harmful emissions associated with them. CARB rules set requirements for the 
number of eTRUs, which are based on truck TRU fleet size. In addition to the CARB rules, 15 states also 
have announced a joint effort to work on medium- and heavy-duty vehicle electrification. Other states 
have announced regulations aimed at TRUs.

### 14.1 ABOUT CARB

CARB is an agency of the government of California that aims to reduce air pollution. It was established in 
1967 and is housed in the California Environmental Protection Agency. CARB has been instrumental in 
driving innovation in reducing emissions in the global automotive sectors through a wide range of 
programs including the Alternative Fuel Vehicle Incentive Program, the Low-Carbon Fuel Standard, the 
Hybrid and Zero-Emission Truck and Bus Voucher Incentive Project, and many other programs intended 
to reduce air emissions from vehicles [91].

CARB was established prior to the US EPA. The federal Clean Air Act establishes California as the only 
state permitted to issue its own emissions standards, subject to a waiver from the US EPA. Other states 
may choose either to follow CARB standards or the federal vehicle emission standards but may not set 
their own.

### 14.2 CARB AND TRUS

CARB first identified the opportunity to reduce air pollution generated by diesel powered TRUs in 2002 
[30]. In 2003, CARB issued its first Transport Refrigeration Systems study, which analyzed emissions and 
technology options to reduce the particulate matter emissions from TRUs where they are most 
geographically concentrated [30].

CARB has stated that their regulation of TRUs stems from a desire to address the toxic and harmful 
emissions from diesel-powered TRUs, especially at areas such as distribution centers and other cold 
storage facilities where large concentrations of these units compromise the health of surrounding

## 15 FINDINGS

Based on the research the study team came to the following conclusions regarding eTRUs.

1. Freight requiring transport in a specified temperature range encompasses about **15% of 
straight trucks and trailers**. Refrigerated and frozen foods, pharmaceuticals, other grocery items 
and electronics are some of the commodities requiring refrigeration. Other freight, such as fresh 
produce and paints, require freeze protection during winters. This is a significant — but often 
unrecognized — portion of North American freight requiring special straight trucks and trailers 
with diesel-powered transport refrigeration units.
2. Regulations and heightened sustainability expectations are converging to require more 
sustainable solutions for moving refrigerated goods. CARB, in particular, has been driving 
regulations that require zero-emission refrigeration and freeze protection for moving goods. 
This can be a large challenge as today’s diesel TRUs run off the diesel truck’s engine or from 
dedicated engines integrated into the TRUs. These have long run times for when the vehicles are 
in motion or sitting at warehouses or stores to be loaded or unloaded.
3. New refrigeration units have been developed to run with zero emissions using electricity. 
Many forms of eTRUs are available or under development to respond to these expectations and 
requirements. These include all-electric units some with significant battery storage systems that 
accept plug-in shore power as a source and hybrid units that run primarily on electricity but 
have diesel or other powertrains to support long run times.
4. Other allied solutions are emerging to support these eTRUs. Solutions include increased 
insulation or even incorporating cold plates into the truck or trailer bodies, solar or axle-mounted generators (eAxles) to deliver more power to the batteries and other innovative 
solutions.
5. Moving and delivering refrigerated goods with zero or significantly lower emissions while 
maintaining quality is a large challenge. Although independent of electric truck propulsion 
units, these “cleaner” solutions compete for resources, but also benefit from developments, 
such as improved battery size, weight and cost. Delivering reliable, durable and cost-effective 
solutions quickly has many challenges.
