6.2 Natural Gas: The Methane Variance

The net $\text{CO}_2$ benefit of a natural gas engine is a reduction of 13% to 18% compared to diesel fuel. Natural gas produces approximately 27% less $\text{CO}_2$ per unit of energy on a fuel-comparison basis compared with No. 2 diesel fuel. However, the natural gas engine is less efficient than a diesel, and natural gas must be compressed for storage on the vehicle [13]. Since compressing natural gas to 3600 PSI requires a great deal of electrical energy. This electricity may be attached to emission creation so natural gas does come with an upstream emission consequence.

## 6.2.1 Combustion Characteristics

Most heavy-duty NG engines use spark ignition and stoichiometric combustion, a homogenous cylinder charge. In other words, fuel is mixed within the intake manifold before it enters the combustion chamber. This lower-pressure combustion produces significantly less $\text{NO}_x$ than the diesel cycle, often 90% below current EPA standards (based on EPA standards in effect at the time of this report). Furthermore, because methane has a higher hydrogen-to-carbon ratio than diesel, it generates less $\text{CO}_2$ at the tailpipe per unit of energy [14].

During the combustion process, some hydrocarbons and $\text{NO}_x$ also are created. These emissions are managed by the aftertreatment system, usually a three-way catalyst system. The $\text{NO}_x$ output is much lower than typical diesel combustion as there is less oxygen available during the combustion process. However, hydrocarbon output from this type of combustion system tends to be significantly higher than that from diesel combustion. The particulate output from natural gas combustion is minimal and does not need to be managed by the three-way aftertreatment system. Hydrocarbon control is managed by the aftertreatment catalyst, but natural gas is somewhat more difficult to oxidize than typical hydrocarbons from either gasoline or diesel combustion as it takes higher temperatures to manage this process.

A three-wat catalyst (TWC) is an emissions-control device integrated into the exhaust system that simultaneously manages three primary pollutants: $\text{NO}_x$, CO, and unburned HC. For the system to function effectively, the engine must operate at a stoichiometric air-fuel ratio, with exactly enough oxygen to burn the fuel completely. To manage $\text{NO}_x$, the catalyst uses a reduction reaction — typically facilitated by precious metals like rhodium — which strips the oxygen atoms away from the $\text{NO}_x$. This chemical process converts the harmful $\text{NO}_x$ into harmless nitrogen gas and oxygen, while the liberated oxygen is then used in the simultaneous oxidation of CO and HC into $\text{CO}_2$ and water vapor.

## 6.2.2 The Issue of Methane Slip

The environmental advantage of natural gas is precarious due to methane slip — the release of unburned methane into the atmosphere. Methane is a potent GHG with a Global Warming Potential (GWP) 27 to 30 times that of $\text{CO}_2$ over 100 years (AR6) and more than 80 times greater over a 20-year horizon [15]. Slip occurs at the vehicle level (crankcase ventilation, dynamic seals) and, more significantly, in the upstream supply chain (extraction leakage, pipeline venting). Studies indicate that if total system leakage exceeds roughly 1%, the climate benefits of natural gas over diesel can be negated, potentially leading to increased radiative forcing in the short term (an action that causes the Earth to trap more of the sun's energy in the immediate future) [16]. The EPA estimates the leakage to be 1.4%, and there are some higher estimates.

Electrical generation accounts for about 35% of the total energy consumption in the US [20]. ## 6.4 Hydrogen Fuel Cell Vehicle

In an ideal scenario, FCEVs emit only water vapor and warm air. The chemical reaction taking place inside the fuel cell is remarkably clean compared to an internal combustion engine. Depending on the ambient temperature and the operating state of the stack, the water is emitted from the tailpipe as either liquid droplets or water vapor (steam). This water is generally very clean, though it is not considered potable because it can pick up trace contaminants from the air intake or the internal components of the exhaust system.

The fuel cell pulls in large amounts of ambient air to meet oxygen requirements. The air exiting the tailpipe is essentially the same air that went in, with a significantly lower oxygen content and a higher concentration of nitrogen.

Most fuel cells targeted to work in heavy-duty transportation are of the proton exchange membrane (or polymer electrolyte membrane or PEM) variety and operate at temperatures between about 140°F to 200°F [25]. Fuel cells are a technology originally developed in the aerospace industry that have evolved over time. Today’s PEM fuel cells operate by passing hydrogen gas over a catalyst that splits the proton from the electron. The proton passes through the membrane, and the electrons are then used to power electrical devices. Once the electrons arrive on the other side of the membrane, they combine with the protons along with oxygen from the atmosphere to form water. 1. **Steam Methane Reforming:** Currently, most hydrogen fuel is produced via SMR, which strips hydrogen from natural gas using high-temperature steam. Hydrogen produced by this method is identified in the industry as grey hydrogen. This process is energy-intensive and releases about 20 lbs. of $\text{CO}_2$ for every kilogram of hydrogen produced. Since 1 kg of hydrogen has roughly the same energy content as a gallon of diesel (which emits ~22 lbs. $\text{CO}_2$), SMR-based hydrogen offers negligible GHG benefits without carbon capture [33].
2. **Electrolysis:** Producing green hydrogen via electrolysis (splitting water with renewable electricity) is carbon-neutral but energy inefficient. The round-trip process — converting electricity to hydrogen, compressing it, transporting it, and converting it back to electricity in a fuel cell — results in significant energy losses compared to direct battery use.

**The Energy Penalty of State Change:** The physical state of the fuel imposes a heavy energy tax. Compressing hydrogen to 10,000 psi (700 bar) for onboard storage consumes about 15% of the fuel's energy content. Liquefying it to -423°F (-253°C) for higher density storage consumes approximately 35% of the energy. These parasitic loads are major contributors to the WTT emissions catalog [30].

A kilogram of hydrogen has the highest energy density per unit mass of any known fuel, which is often referred to as its specific energy or gravimetric energy density. Hydrogen represents a gravimetric energy density of approximately 33,000 Wh/kg. By comparison, today’s modern lithium-Ion batteries have a comparable mass energy density of 200 Wh/kg, with industry efforts striving toward approximately 400 Wh/kg in a future solid state battery design [31]. In transportation, one kilogram of $\text{H}_2$ contains about the same energy potential as one gallon of diesel fuel.

Hydrogen compression and transportation is significantly more difficult and expensive than for diesel fuel. Today’s pipelines are mostly incapable of transporting hydrogen over distances without significant leakage. In addition, hydrogen also can cause a condition called hydrogen embrittlement in steel and aluminum components, making these materials much more likely to fail from stresses that they normally could withstand. Therefore, any materials used in the transportation or storage of hydrogen gas must be thoughtfully designed.

Hydrogen, in either liquid or gaseous form, is typically transported via trailers. In either form, the amount that can be transported on a traditional sized trailer provides much less energy than a comparable sized trailer hauling diesel fuel.

Hydrogen is typically stored in gaseous form on a vehicle at about 10K psi — roughly three times the pressure of typical CNG storage system. The amount of area volume required to store enough hydrogen at that pressure to complete a normal day’s work is many times higher than the chassis room needed for storing diesel fuel.

Another option for hydrogen storage on the vehicle is in liquid form. This can be used for bulk transport or storage on the vehicle for normal operation. To achieve this, special highly insulated tanks are necessary to hold the liquid at a very low temperature for the duration of storage. Liquid hydrogen must be kept below -253°C (-423°F) at standard atmospheric pressures, or it will evaporate to gaseous form.

## 7.1 Review of the Internal Combustion Engine

The commercial transportation sector finds itself at an important intersection, driven by the dual pressures of operational efficiency and increasingly stringent environmental regulations. As global logistics networks strive to reduce their carbon footprint, the internal combustion engine is undergoing a period of rapid evolution. * **The Intake Stroke: Induction of Pure Air:** The cycle begins with the intake stroke, during which the piston moves from top dead center (TDC) to bottom dead center (BDC). As the intake valves open, the downward motion of the piston creates a vacuum (in naturally aspirated engines) or receives pressurized air (in turbocharged engines) [41]. Turbocharging improves the effective compression ratio and improves volumetric efficiency. Crucially, unlike gasoline engines, the intake charge of a diesel engine consists only of air, with no fuel present during this stage [41]. This allows the engine to operate unthrottled, reducing pumping losses and contributing to the diesel's superior part-load efficiency.
* **The Compression Stroke: Generating the Heat of Ignition:** Following the intake stroke, the intake valves close and the piston moves from BDC back to TDC. The trapped air is compressed into a tiny fraction of its original volume, typically one-sixteenth to one-twentieth (representing the compression ratio). This rapid compression causes the air temperature to rise precipitously, reaching levels between 1,000°F and 1,300°F [41]. The pressure at the end of this stroke can reach 35 to 50 bar (~ 500 to 725 PSI) even before combustion begins [42].
* **The Power Stroke: Injection & Spontaneous Combustion:** As the piston nears TDC, the fuel injection system delivers a precise quantity of diesel fuel directly into the superheated air. Because the air temperature is well above the autoignition point of the fuel (approximately 410°F to 536°F depending on cetane rating), the fuel ignites spontaneously [43]. The resulting rapid expansion of gases creates a high-pressure explosion that drives the piston back down toward BDC, performing the mechanical work that turns the crankshaft [41]. This process is characterized by a diffusion flame, where the rate of combustion is controlled by the rate at which fuel and air mix [44].
* **The Exhaust Stroke: Expelling Combustion Byproducts:** Finally, as the piston reaches BDC and begins its second upward journey, the exhaust valves open. The piston forces the spent combustion gases out of the cylinder and into the exhaust manifold [41]. This completes the 720 degrees of crankshaft rotation required for one full power cycle [42].

### 7.1.3 Input Dynamics: Air & Fuel in a Turbocharged System

In a modern 4-stroke engine, the inputs of air and fuel are managed by highly sophisticated subsystems designed to maximize power density and minimize emissions.

* **Advanced Turbocharging & Air Management:** Turbocharging is essential for the performance of heavy-duty diesel engines. A turbocharger uses the kinetic energy of the exhaust gases to spin a turbine, which is connected via a shaft to a compressor. This compressor forces more air into the cylinders than would be possible through atmospheric pressure alone [41]. Modern engines may use wastegate turbo technology to limit maximum exhaust pressure which in turn controls the intake air pressure, or a variable geometry turbocharger (VGT). A VGT features a sliding nozzle ring that can vary the exhaust flow area, allowing the turbo to act like a small unit at low engine speeds (for rapid boost) and a large unit at high speeds (for maximum flow and reduced backpressure) [35]. Next generation VGTs offer up to 5% higher efficiency through optimized wheel options and shroud designs [38]. To increase air density further, the pressurized air is passed through a charge air cooler (CAC) to reduce its temperature before it enters the intake manifold [45].
* **High-Pressure Common Rail (HPCR) Injection:** The fuel input is managed by the HPCR system, which decouples the pressure generation from the engine speed. A high-pressure pump (such as the Cummins XPI system) maintains a constant reservoir of fuel in a "rail" at pressures up to 35,000 psi or more [35]. Electronic injectors use solenoids to open and close with microsecond precision, allowing for multiple injection events per combustion cycle.
  * *Pilot Injection:* A small amount of fuel is injected just before the main event to preheat the chamber and reduce combustion noise.
  * *Main Injection:* Provides the primary energy release for the power stroke.
  * *Post Injection:* Used to manage exhaust temperatures or provide a fuel source for active regeneration of the particulate filter [46].

### 7.1.4 Chemical Energy Conversion & Emission Formation

Diesel fuel is a dense mixture of hydrocarbons, typically characterized by an average molecular formula of $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{23}$ or $\text{C}_{13.8}\text{H}_{24}$. The conversion of this chemical energy into mechanical power occurs through an exothermic oxidation reaction [47].

* **The Combustion Reaction:** In an ideal scenario, the combustion of diesel fuel would be complete, yielding only carbon dioxide, water, and nitrogen.

$$4\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{23} + 71\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 48\text{CO}_2 + 46\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

In terms of the total energy liberated, this reaction releases approximately 56,000 kJ per mole of fuel [48]. However, because diesel engines operate with an overall lean air-fuel ratio (often exceeding 25:1 to avoid smoke), the exhaust also contains significant amounts of unused oxygen and atmospheric nitrogen [49].

* **The Mechanism of Pollutant Formation:** The “dirty” nature of historical diesel exhaust is the result of three primary chemical phenomena occurring in the cylinder.
  * *Thermal $\text{NO}_x$ (Zeldovich Mechanism):* $\text{NO}_x$ is formed when atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen react under conditions of extreme heat (above 1,800 Kelvin) [50]. The high compression ratios

### 7.1.5 Exhaust Emission Treatment & Aftertreatment Systems

To transform raw engine-out emissions into harmless tailpipe output, modern diesel engines employ a complex, multi-stage aftertreatment stack. This system is a chemical factory on the chassis that operates in concert with the engine's electronic control module (ECM).

* **Exhaust Gas Recirculation (EGR):** EGR is a diesel engine-level strategy used to inhibit $\text{NO}_x$ formation. A portion of exhaust gas is cooled and recirculated back into the intake manifold. Because the returning exhaust gas has reduced $\text{O}_2$ saturation, it lowers the concentration of oxygen in the cylinder and increases the heat capacity of the charge, thereby lowering the peak combustion temperature below the threshold where the Zeldovich mechanism becomes most active. First proposed by the Russian physicist Yakov Borisovich Zel’dovich in 1946, the Zeldovich reaction pathway accounts for nitrogen and oxygen in the air combining at high temperatures to form nitric oxide.
* **The Diesel Oxidation Catalyst (DOC):** The first component in the aftertreatment stack is the DOC. It contains a ceramic or metallic substrate coated with precious metals like platinum and palladium. The DOC promotes the oxidation of gaseous pollutants.

$$2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2$$
$$[\text{HC}] + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Another critical function of the DOC in modern systems is also to oxidize NO into $\text{NO}_2$, which is more reactive and helps in downstream processes.

* **The Diesel Particulate Filter (DFP) [sic]:** The DPF is a wall-flow filter that captures the physical soot particles. The exhaust gas is forced through porous ceramic walls, leaving the soot trapped in the channels [52]. Periodically, the DPF must undergo regeneration to prevent clogging. This can be passive, using the $\text{NO}_2$ from the DOC to oxidize soot at normal operating temperatures, or active, where the ECM injects fuel into the exhaust to raise temperatures above 1,000°F to burn the soot into a small amount of ash. Ash is mostly a result of lubricating oil that makes its way into the combustion chamber, thus, the industry push for low ash formulation lubricating oils. Ash cannot be oxidized by the DPF.
* **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR) & Ammonia Slip Catalyst:** The SCR system targets the remaining $\text{NO}_x$. It requires the injection of DEF, which is a urea-water solution. In the hot exhaust stream, urea decomposes into ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$).

### 7.2.1 Spark Ignition vs. Compression Ignition

The most significant difference between spark ignition and compression ignition lies in how the fuel is ignited. While the diesel relies on the squeeze of high compression, the X15N is a spark-ignited engine [54].

* **Cylinder Head & Ignition System:** The X15N features a natural gas-specific cylinder head with ports for spark plugs rather than high-pressure diesel injectors. The ignition system has changed from capacitive to inductive, providing a high-energy spark that extends plug life and improves combustion stability [55].
* **Compression Ratio & Thermal Management:** Natural gas has a much higher-octane rating than gasoline (120+) but does not auto-ignite as easily as diesel fuel [43]. Auto ignite temperatures for NG are higher than diesel, but these temperatures will be reached at lower compression ratios than diesel. However, NG can be ignited because of hot spots in the combustion chamber before the spark is introduced, resulting in pre-ignition. Therefore, the compression ratio (with turbocharging and air throttle control) must be controlled to lower levels than diesel. The lower compression/expansion ratio results in lower thermal efficiency. To prevent knock (pre-ignition) while maintaining efficiency, the X15N operates at a lower compression ratio than its diesel counterparts — typically around 11:1 to 12:1 [56]. The cylinder head is sculpted for better thermal distribution to manage the high exhaust temperatures characteristic of stoichiometric gas combustion [56].
* **Induction and Throttling:** Unlike the unthrottled diesel, the stoichiometric X15N requires a throttle valve to precisely meter the air-fuel mixture [57]. This ensures the engine maintains a perfect stoichiometric ratio, with exactly enough air to burn the fuel with no excess of either [58].

* The X15N combusts methane, which is a simpler, single-carbon molecule compared to the complex chains in diesel fuel [47].

### 7.3.1 Fundamentals of Heavy-Duty Electric Vehicle Architecture

The core challenge in engineering a Class 8 BEV lies in reconciling the immense energy density requirements of long-haul freight with the gravimetric and volumetric constraints of current battery technology. A standard diesel truck stores approximately 3,700 kWh of chemical energy in two 100-gallon tanks, weighing under 2,000 lbs. To replicate even a fraction of this range electrically requires battery packs weighing 8,000 to 12,000 lbs. This massive weight penalty necessitates completely rethinking vehicle architecture.

**Chassis & Structural Topology:** The structural backbone of typical Class 8 trucks remains the ladder frame — two parallel C-channel steel rails that run the length of the vehicle. However, for a clean sheet design of new BEV architecture the use of the space between and around these rails shifts drastically. New chassis layouts are emerging which have optimized vehicle design around the battery packs rather than adapting the packs to fit to the chassis.

**Adapted vs. Native Architectures:** The industry is currently divided into two distinct design schools when it comes to architecture.

1. **Adapted Legacy Architecture:** Exemplified by the Volvo VNR Electric and Freightliner eCascadia, this approach retains the existing diesel chassis and cab structures. The internal combustion engine is removed, and the transmission tunnel and frame rail spaces are repurposed for high-voltage (HV) cabling, power distribution units (PDUs), and pneumatic systems [74]. The advantage of this approach is supply chain continuity and manufacturing ease. However, this often compromises aerodynamic efficiency and packaging optimization, as the cab shape is dictated by the cooling requirements of a non-existent diesel engine.
2. **Clean-Sheet (Native) Architecture:** Exemplified by the Tesla Semi and Windrose E700, this approach designs the chassis specifically for electric propulsion. The absence of a bulky diesel engine and transmission allows the cabin to be pushed forward and tapered, significantly reducing the drag coefficient. The chassis rails often are integrated with the battery subframe to increase torsional rigidity and lower the vehicle’s center of gravity [75].

**Battery Packaging & Safety Structures:** In adapted architectures, batteries are typically mounted in saddle configurations on the exterior of the frame rails, occupying the space previously reserved for fuel tanks and aftertreatment systems. This necessitates robust side-impact protection systems. Volvo, for instance, has integrated a specialized side-impact barrier into the battery mounting system to protect the modules during lateral collisions [74].

In contrast, native architectures often use the space between the frame rails more aggressively or integrate the battery pack as a structural member. The Tesla Semi, for example, situates its 850 to 900 kWh pack low in the chassis, between the wheels, acting as a structural spine that enhances handling stability and rollover resistance [76]. As shown in Figure 25, the Windrose BEV goes further and creates a box frame around the batteries.

### 7.3.2 Aerodynamics & Energy Consumption

In heavy-duty trucking, aerodynamic drag is the dominant resistive force at highway speeds, typically accounting for more than 50% of energy consumption [78]. The power required to overcome drag is proportional to the cube of velocity, making aerodynamic efficiency critical for maximizing range.

* **Legacy Aerodynamics:** Traditional trucks have drag coefficient values in the range of 0.5 to more than 0.8. Even with electric retrofits (grille shutters, side skirts), their fundamental shape is limited by the need for a large hood [79]. Currently, aerodynamically optimized tractors pulling aerodynamically configured trailers have drag coefficients between 0.4 to 0.5. As detailed in NACFE’s 2024 report *SuperTruck 2: Empowering Future Trucking*, the DOE Super-Trucks demonstrated drag coefficients below 0.4 [80].
* **Next-Generation Efficiency:** The Tesla Semi is estimated to achieve a drag coefficient of 0.36, while the Windrose E700 claims an industry-leading 0.2755 [81]. While specific aerodynamic numbers may be debated, modern tractors are significantly better than those from a decade ago. This is achieved through a variety of factors.
  * *Unibody Cab Construction:* Smoothing the transition between cab and trailer.

### 7.3.3 Electric Propulsion Systems

The propulsion system of a Class 8 BEV replaces the diesel engine and multi-gear transmission with electric machines and simplified gearing. All propulsion motors are alternating current (AC) design and batteries store energy in a direct current (DC) design architecture. Therefore, DC-to-AC conversion and control is necessary, and vice versa for regenerative braking.

**Motor Topology: Permanent Magnet vs. Induction:** The choice of electric motor technology dictates efficiency, cost, and operational characteristics.

**Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSM):** This type of motor is used extensively by Freightliner, Tesla, and Windrose, Freightliner [sic].
* **Principle:** The rotor contains permanent magnets (usually Neodymium-Iron-Boron). The stator generates a rotating magnetic field, and the rotor locks into sync with this field.
* **Advantages:** High power density and superior efficiency (95% to 97%), especially at low speeds and high torque loads, making them ideal for starting heavy loads [83].
* **Carbon-Sleeved Rotors (Tesla Innovation):** High-performance PMSMs face a structural limit — centrifugal force. At high RPMs, the magnets can detach from the rotor. Tesla wraps the rotor in a carbon fiber sleeve. This sleeve puts the magnets in compression, allowing the motor to spin to 20,000+ RPM without failure. This high-speed capability allows for a smaller, lighter motor to produce immense power through gearing [84].

**AC Induction Motors (ACIM):** This AC motor configuration is used by Tesla on its rearward drive axle, and can be found on trucks from other BEV manufacturers outside of Run on Less, such as Orange EV, BYD, etc.
* **Principle:** The rotor uses conductive bars (aluminum or copper). The stator's field induces a current in the rotor, creating an opposing magnetic field. The rotor always spins slightly slower than the field (slip).
* **Application:** While less efficient than PMSM, induction motors have zero drag torque when not powered (the rotor is not magnetic when the stator is off). This makes them suited for secondary drive axles that are only needed for peak acceleration, as they can coast freely without a clutch [85].

### 7.3.4 Drivetrain Layouts: Central Drive vs. eAxle

The mechanical transmission of power to the wheels is evolving.

**Central Drive (Volvo VNR - Early Generations):** Early BEV trucks mounted a large electric motor in the center of the chassis, connected to a standard driveshaft and rear differential.
* **Pros:** Uses existing axles and suspension; easy to integrate a standard multi-speed transmission (like the Volvo I-Shift) [74].
* **Cons:** Occupies valuable space between frame rails that could be used for batteries; higher mechanical losses through the driveshaft [86].

**The Integrated eAxle (Freightliner, Tesla, Volvo Next-Generation, Windrose):** The Detroit eAxle (Freightliner) and Windrose systems integrate the electric motor, transmission, and differential into a single unit located directly on the axle [87]. The Tesla Semi uses a tri-motor powertrain. One motor operates continuously for highway efficiency, while the other two motors, paired to a separate axle, activate for acceleration and climbing.
* **Pros:** Eliminates the driveshaft and central motor mounting, freeing up chassis space for batteries. Reduces weight and mechanical parasitic losses [88].

### 7.3.5 Transmission & Gearing Strategies

Unlike passenger BEVs, heavy-duty trucks usually require multiple gears to handle the massive variance in load (30,000 lbs. empty vs. 82,000 lbs. loaded).

* **2-Speed Transmissions:** Used by Volvo (I-Shift) and Freightliner (within the Detroit eAxle). Low gear provides the torque multiplication needed for grade startability, while high gear allows for efficient highway cruising at lower motor RPMs [74].
* **Single-Speed with Tri-Motor Clutching:** Tesla avoids a multi-speed transmission by using three motors.
  * *Efficiency Axle:* One motor is permanently geared for highway cruising.
  * *Torque Axle:* Two additional motors are geared for acceleration and grades. Crucially, these motors feature a mechanical clutch mechanism that physically disconnects them from the wheels when not needed. This eliminates the "cogging" and drag losses of spinning unused PMSM motors, maximizing range [90].

### 7.3.6 High-Voltage Energy Storage & Management

The Energy Storage System (ESS) is the heaviest and most expensive component of the battery electric vehicle.

**Battery Chemistry and Design:** Trucks operating in Run on Less – Messy Middle featured a battery chemistry design that included NMC and LFP, common to production vehicles in today’s transportation industry. Other chemistry designs such as solid state, semi solid state, sodium ion, etc., are emerging as performance improvement initiatives in future designs, but are not yet in production.

**NMC (Nickel Manganese Cobalt):** Used by Volvo, Freightliner, and likely Tesla.
* **Characteristics:** High energy density (~250 Wh/kg), standard 3.7V nominal per cell.
* **Implication:** Essential for long-haul trucks where minimizing battery weight is critical to maximizing payload capacity. However, NMC chemistries are more volatile and require rigorous thermal management to prevent thermal runaway.

**LFP (Lithium Iron Phosphate):** Offered by Windrose as an option [91].
* **Characteristics:** Lower energy density (~160 Wh/kg), 3.2V nominal, but significantly higher cycle life (2,000 to 3,000+ cycles) and thermal stability.

**High Voltage Architecture: 400V/600V vs. 800V/1000V**

The voltage level determines the efficiency of power transfer and charging speed.

* **400V/600V Systems:** The Freightliner eCascadia and Volvo VNR Electric use nominal 400V/600V architectures. This allows the use of standard industrial components but limits charging speeds (maximum ~250 to 270 kW) and requires heavier copper cabling to handle high currents [93].
* **800V and 1000V Systems:** The Windrose E700 (800V) and Tesla Semi (1000V) adopt higher voltages.

**Benefits:** There are several benefits to a high voltage architecture.
* *Reduced Resistive Losses:* Joule heating is drastically reduced.
* *Megawatt Charging:* Higher voltage is the key enabler for the Megawatt Charging System (MCS). The Tesla Semi can recover 400 miles of range in 30 minutes (charging at ~1 MW), which would be thermally impossible at 400V as a result of the massive current required [94].

**Thermal Management Systems (BTMS):** Maintaining the battery between 25°C and 35°C for both vehicle operation and charging is non-negotiable for performance and safety.

* **Liquid Cooling:** All trucks analyzed use liquid cooling loops (glycol-water) circulating through plates sandwiched between cells or modules.
* **Active Heating:** In cold climates, the system must heat the battery to accept a charge (lithium plating occurs if charged while freezing). This is achieved via resistive (PTC) heaters or by scavenging waste heat from the drivetrain [95].
* **Windrose & Tesla Cooling:** The high charging rates of these vehicles (800kW+) generate immense heat. Their cooling systems are oversized compared to legacy adaptations, likely employing active refrigeration (chillers) during charging events rather than just passive radiators.
* **In Development:** Some vehicles are moving toward managing both battery temperature and the passenger cabin through the same system, along with cooling integration between the drive motors and power electronics. The target temperatures for these vehicle components are different, so the cooling systems will need to accommodate the individual component requirements.

### 7.3.7 Auxiliary Systems & Vehicle Control

Electrifying the drivetrain necessitates the electrification of all accessory systems previously driven by the diesel engine belt. Much of the truck’s electrical system may still operate on low voltage 12V or 24V circuitry, such as interior and exterior lighting, the dash, etc. However, beyond this, specialized auxiliary systems are required.

**Electric Air Compressors (EAC):** Class 8 trucks rely on compressed air for friction brakes and air suspension.
* **Mechanism:** A dedicated high-voltage electric motor drives a scroll or piston compressor. Unlike a diesel compressor which runs constantly (unloaded), the EAC operates on-demand, filling tanks only when pressure drops.

* **Redundancy:** This is a safety-critical system. Failure leads to the deployment of spring brakes (emergency stop). Therefore, these units are robust, often oil-cooled, and monitored by the Vehicle Control Unit (VCU).

**Steering Option:** The trucks in the Run used either electric power steering (EPS) or steer-by-wire technology.
* **Electro-Hydraulic (EHPS):** Used in Volvo and Freightliner trucks, an electric motor drives a conventional hydraulic pump. This retains the mechanical linkage, and steering feel familiar to drivers.
* **Steer-by-Wire (SBW):** The Windrose E700 features a steer-by-wire system. There is no mechanical column connecting the steering wheel to the rack. Sensors capture driver input, and electric actuators turn the wheels.
  * *Advantages:* Eliminates vibration transfer to the driver, allows for variable steering ratios, and enables seamless integration with autonomous driving software (Chassis-by-Wire concept) [96].
  * *Safety:* Requires triple-redundant power and data paths to ensure fail-safe operation [97].

**Regenerative Braking Integration:** The integration of regenerative braking with pneumatic friction brakes is complex.
* **Blended Braking:** The brake pedal does not directly actuate the air valves. instead, it sends a signal to the VCU. The VCU requests negative torque from the motors (regen). If the requested deceleration exceeds the motor's limit (or if the battery is full), the VCU proportionally engages the pneumatic brakes.
* **Efficiency:** This one-pedal capability captures up to 15% to 20% of kinetic energy on flat routes and significantly more on descents, drastically reducing brake pad wear and particulate emissions [98].

**Heating, Ventilation, Air Conditioning (HVAC) Systems:** In the design of a legacy diesel-powered Class 8 tractor, thermal management was primarily a task of heat rejection and scavenging. The massive thermal inefficiency of the diesel engine, while detrimental to fuel economy, provided an abundant and nearly free source of waste heat for cabin climate control and defrosting. BEVs are inherently more efficient, but this efficiency creates a significant challenge. The HVAC system is no longer a peripheral, energized by wasted thermal energy, but rather a core component of the vehicle’s energy management strategy, directly impacting the driving range, battery life, and TCO.

* **The Traditional Approach:** Today, HVAC vehicle systems range from a traditional approach where BTMS is a closed-loop liquid system that actively regulates the temperature of the traction battery, e-axle, and drive control unit, through interlinked circuits — a liquid cooling circuit, a refrigerant circuit, and an air circuit. Separately cabin heating, cooling, and defrost are designed to work alongside the BTMS.
* **Emerging HVAC Technologies:** Beyond this, the Tesla Semi represents a different approach and the most aggressive integration of thermal systems in the Class 8 market. Rather than using discrete loops for the battery, drivetrain, and cabin, Tesla employs a unified thermal architecture centered on the Octovalve and Supermanifold [99]. This system is designed to treat the entire vehicle as a thermal ecosystem where heat is shuffled to wherever it is most needed at any given moment.

### 7.3.9 Environmental Impact & Emissions Analysis

### 7.3.10 Summary of Battery Electric Findings

The transition to Class 8 BEVs is driven by the convergence of high-voltage power electronics, structural packaging innovation, and aerodynamic optimization. The divergence in engineering approaches — between the Volvo and Freightliner and the native architectures of Tesla and Windrose — represents a market in transition. Adapted trucks offer a lower barrier to entry and easier serviceability for existing fleets, using proven 400V systems and standard transmissions. However, the future of long-haul trucking lies with native 800V/1000V architectures. The ability to integrate 700 to 1000 kWh batteries, charge at megawatt rates, and operate with extreme aerodynamic efficiency, is collectively essential to achieving parity with diesel range and payload.

Technologically, the permanent magnet motor with carbon-sleeved rotors, coupled with mechanical disconnect clutches and steer-by-wire systems, represents the new state-of-the-art. These innovations not only maximize energy efficiency but also prepare the heavy-duty sector for the next phase of its evolution: autonomous operation.

The next frontier for heavy-duty BEVs may lie in the mainstream integration of ultra-high-density cell chemistries, such as solid-state or silicon-anode variants, which promise to radically alter the payload-to-weight ratio. As megawatt-level charging networks scale, thermal management systems will evolve from passive cooling loops into predictive, AI-driven architectures capable of pre-conditioning the pack based on real-time topography, cargo weight, and fleet telemetry.

Finally, while the technology in the latest generation of trucks is highly impressive, the heavy-duty electric trucking sector is in a phase of nascent product maturity. Consider these three realities that illustrate the early stage of the industry’s transition.

1. **High-Volume Serial Production Has Just Began:** The first wave of commercial heavy trucks consisted largely of hand-assembled pilot fleets. Large-scale, high-volume dedicated factory lines for Class 8 BEVs — such as Tesla entering high-volume production at Giga Nevada — are a brand-new milestone.
2. **The Charging Infrastructure Gap:** Traditional commercial charging grids were designed for passenger cars or light logistics vans. The proliferation of electrification within the heavy-duty sector is dependent on the emerging deployment of MCS, meaning cross-country long-haul corridors are still in their infancy compared to localized hub-and-spoke depot routes.
3. **Massive Runway for Weight Reduction:** The initial engineering hurdle for electric semis was proving they could haul 82,000 lbs. across hundreds of miles. Optimization has only just begun. Major design revisions — like shifting to a 48-volt low-voltage ancillary system architecture and eliminating hydraulic steering loops — are shedding thousands of pounds of dead weight to recover valuable payload capacity.

Because heavy-duty BEVs are near the very beginning of their product life cycle, the pace of engineering refinement over the next decade is expected to vastly outstrip the incremental gains left to be realized by a century of past diesel development.

### 7.4.2 The Fuel Cell System: Electrochemical Power Generation

The core of the XCIENT's propulsion capabilities is its hydrogen fuel cell system. This device is not an engine in the traditional thermodynamic sense; it is an electrochemical energy converter that transforms the chemical potential energy of hydrogen directly into electrical energy without combustion.

**Dual-Stack Architecture & Power Output:** The XCIENT is powered by a 180-kW fuel cell system, which is mechanically comprised of two identical 90-kW fuel cell stacks operating in parallel [113].
* **Redundancy & Reliability:** The dual-stack approach offers a degree of redundancy. If one stack were to experience a fault, the system potentially could limp home on the remaining stack.
* **Modularity:** This modular design allows Hyundai to scale the technology. A single 90-kW stack might power a medium-duty truck, while the paired configuration meets the high-power demands of a Class 8 tractor.

### 7.4.3 PEM Technology

The stacks use PEM technology, which is the industry standard for automotive applications because of its high-power density and relatively low operating temperature (allowing for quick start-up).

**Principle of Operation:** The fundamental operation occurs at the membrane electrode assembly (MEA) within each cell of the stack.
* **Anode Reaction (Hydrogen Oxidation):** Hydrogen gas flows from the tanks into the anode side of the stack. A platinum-based catalyst facilitates splitting the hydrogen molecule into protons ($\text{H}^+$) and electrons ($\text{e}^-$).

$$2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{H}^+ + 4\text{e}^-$$

* **Proton Transport:** The PEM is a selectively permeable polymer electrolyte. It allows the positively charged protons to migrate through the membrane to the cathode side. Critically, the membrane is electrically insulating, blocking the electrons.
* **Electron Flow (Current):** Forced to find an alternative path, the electrons travel through an external circuit. This flow of electrons constitutes the DC electricity that powers the truck's motor and charges the battery.
* **Cathode Reaction (Oxygen Reduction):** On the cathode side, oxygen from the ambient air meets the protons (from the membrane) and the electrons (returning from the circuit). The catalyst facilitates their recombination to form water.

$$\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

### 7.4.4 Balance of Plant: Supporting Systems

The fuel cell stack cannot operate in isolation. It requires a complex array of support systems known as the Balance of Plant (BoP).

**Air Processing System (APS):** The stack requires a massive and continuous supply of oxygen.
* **Compression:** A high-voltage centrifugal air compressor forces ambient air into the cathode channels. The pressure and flow rate must be precisely controlled to match the current demand of the driver.
* **Humidification:** The PEM relies on water content to conduct protons. If the membrane dries out, conductivity drops and permanent damage can occur. The APS includes a membrane humidifier that transfers moisture from the wet exhaust air to the dry incoming air, maintaining optimal hydration levels [114].

**Thermal Management System:** The fuel cell reaction is roughly 50% to 60% efficient, meaning nearly half of the energy content of the hydrogen is converted to heat.
* **Cooling Loop:** A dedicated cooling loop circulates deionized water (or a specialized low-conductivity coolant) through channels in the bipolar plates of the stack. This coolant must be non-conductive to prevent short-circuiting the high-voltage cells.
* **Heat Rejection:** Heat must be exclusively rejected through large heat exchangers on the vehicle. By comparison, internal combustion engines reject approximately 50% of the waste heat through the exhaust gas. The rest is managed through the radiator and charge air cooler. Given this, the FCEV heated coolant is pumped to the large frontal radiator, where the heat is rejected to the atmosphere. The system must maintain the stack temperature strictly between 60°C and 80°C. Exceeding this range can degrade the membrane; running too cool reduces efficiency [115].

* 700 Bar vs. 350 Bar: Earlier generations of heavy-duty FCEVs often used 350 bar storage. The shift to 700 bar in the North American XCIENT allows for a near-doubling of energy density. * Protective Shell: An outer layer of fiberglass provides impact resistance and protection against abrasion from road debris.

Safety Mechanisms: Handling gas at 10,000 psi requires robust safety engineering.
* Thermally Activated Pressure Relief Devices (TPRDs): In the event of a fire, the pressure inside the tanks would rise dangerously. TPRDs are heat-sensitive valves that melt at a specific temperature, venting the hydrogen gas upward and away from the vehicle to prevent a tank rupture or explosion.
* Solenoid Valves: Each tank features an electronic solenoid valve that defaults to the closed position. These valves only open when the vehicle is powered on and the system checks are passed. In a collision, inertia sensors trigger these valves to slam shut, isolating the hydrogen in the tanks [118].
* Leak Detection: Hydrogen sensors are distributed throughout the storage module, the fuel cell enclosure, and the cabin. If a leak is detected, the system warns the driver and can automatically shut down the fuel flow.

## 7.4.6 Hybrid Powertrain Architecture & Energy Management

The XCIENT is technically a hybrid electric vehicle. It combines the steady-state power generation of the fuel cell with the dynamic power buffering of a high-voltage battery.

**The High-Voltage Battery System:** The battery serves as a power buffer. Fuel cell stacks prefer to operate at a steady output; they do not respond instantly to rapid throttle inputs because of the lag time in air compressor spool-up and gas transport. The battery provides the immediate current for acceleration and absorbs the high-current spikes from regenerative braking. The high-voltage battery system is configured by a Lithium-ion Polymer 72kWh battery bank mounted along the chassis frame, in three individual packs of 24kWh each.

**Electric Traction Motor:** The motive force is provided by a single high-output electric motor.

**Fossil Diesel (ULSD):** Albert Transport, MVT, Schneider (in NV/AZ), and Frito-Lay (Start/Stop) used ULSD.
* **Origin:** Extracted from geologic reservoirs (crude oil), refined via fractional distillation.
* **Carbon Intensity:** The baseline CI for North American diesel is approximately 100.5 gCO2e/MJ (grams of CO2 equivalent per megajoule). This carbon is new to the atmosphere, having been sequestered for millions of years.
* **Pros/Cons:** It is ubiquitous, energy-dense, and relatively cheap (in non-regulated markets). However, this fuel type is the primary driver of the sector's GHG footprint.

**Renewable Diesel (RD/HVO):** In California, Schneider used renewable diesel.
* **Origin:** Produced from renewable feedstocks such as tallow (animal fat), used cooking oil, and vegetable oils.
* **Process:** The feedstocks undergo hydrotreating, a process like petroleum refining where hydrogen is used to remove oxygen in the fuel substrate [134].
* **Chemistry:** The result is a paraffinic hydrocarbon that is chemically identical to fossil diesel. It has no oxygen content, high cetane, and excellent cold-flow properties. It is a drop-in fuel requiring no engine modifications.
* **Carbon Intensity:** Depending on the feedstock, RD offers a 60% to 80% reduction in lifecycle GHG emissions compared to ULSD. Tallow-based RD can have a CI score as low as 30 gCO2e/MJ [135].

**Biodiesel (FAME/B99/B100):** Frito-Lay operations are based on biodiesel.

* **Carbon Intensity:** Biodiesel is biogenic. The CO2 released during combustion was recently absorbed by the feedstock plant. The lifecycle reduction is typically 74% to 85%.
* **The Optimus Factor:** By enabling the use of B99 (rather than the industry standard B20 blend — 20%FAME/80% fossil diesel), Frito-Lay maximizes the carbon reduction potential of the fuel.

## 8.3.1 RNG: The Mass Balance, Book-and-Claim System

The mass balance book-and-claim energy delivery system avoids the carbon-intensive and inefficient process of trucking RNG or building dedicated pipelines for RNG, thereby maximizing the environmental benefit and economic viability of renewable fuel projects. It is based on an understanding that RNG (biomethane) is chemically identical to fossil natural gas and cannot be physically distinguished once injected into the common pipeline grid [153].

The book-and-claim accounting method, supported by a mass balance approach, allows the environmental benefits of RNG to be sold and claimed by transportation customers without requiring a direct physical pipeline connection to the RNG production facility. The mass balance approach is a critical tool for scaling sustainable technologies such as RNG. It provides a credible way to report on environmental performance and carbon intensity without requiring the massive infrastructure costs of building an entirely separate supply chain. ### Mass Balance (Physical Layer)

One component of the mass balance approach is the physical layer or the production layer of methane gas.
* RNG is produced at a facility (e.g., a landfill or dairy farm) and injected into the existing natural gas utility grid.
* Once injected, the RNG molecules mix indistinguishably with conventional fossil gas. The grid acts as a mass balance system where the volume of gas entering must equal the volume leaving, but the specific molecules travel to the nearest demand point (e.g., a home or local business) rather than traveling thousands of miles to the specific vehicle fueling station.
* The physical gas is used locally, displacing fossil gas in that region.

### Book and Claim (Accounting Layer)

A second and equally important component of a mass balance system is the accounting layer which supports recognition of the benefit behind RNG but does so without the need for separate distribution infrastructure.
* **Book:** The RNG producer books the specific volume of renewable gas injected into the grid and its associated environmental attributes (e.g., carbon intensity reduction) into a registry (such as M-RETS). These attributes are decoupled from the physical molecules.
* **Claim:** A transportation customer (e.g., a fleet operator) at a different location fueling with natural gas claims these environmental attributes by purchasing the RNG credits.

## 8.4.5 Fueling Ecosystems: Origins & Carbon Intensity

A battery-electric truck is only as clean as the electrons that charge it. In Run on Less – Messy Middle, the fuel for these vehicles was electricity drawn from distinct regional grids, each with a unique generation mix and CI. To accurately assess the environmental impact, we must analyze the upstream emissions associated with each fleet's energy procurement.

The electricity powering 4Gen’s Volvo VNRs is derived from the Southern California Edison (SCE) grid, recognized as one of the cleanest in the US. Driven by California's aggressive Renewable Portfolio Standard (RPS), SCE’s generation mix has shifted decisively away from fossil fuels.
* **Generation Mix:** As of the 2023/2024 reporting period, SCE’s base power mix delivered approximately 33% to 35% eligible renewable energy, primarily solar and wind. When combined with large hydroelectric resources and nuclear generation, more than 50% of the delivered energy was carbon-free [164].
* **Carbon Intensity:** For 2024, the California Air Resources Board (CARB) designated the average CI for California grid electricity at 80.55 gCO2e/MJ. When converted to electrical units, SCE's specific emission factor is approximately 183 g CO2e/kWh. This relatively low CI allows 4Gen to slash upstream emissions significantly, particularly if charging is managed to coincide with the midday solar peak, further reducing the marginal emissions rate [165] [166].

## 9.5 Comparative Analysis: Navigating the Messy Middle

As fleet operators evaluate their options for decarbonization, they are faced with a matrix of choices: clean diesel, RNG, BEV, and FCEV. Each has a distinct health impact profile when analyzed from a lifecycle perspective.

**The RNG Advantage**
RNG represents a unique opportunity to decouple heavy-duty trucking from the challenges of diesel. RNG is chemically identical to fossil methane but is derived from biogenic sources like dairy manure, landfills, and wastewater treatment plants.

From a criteria pollutant standpoint, modern natural gas engines are inherently cleaner than diesel. They use spark ignition and stoichiometric combustion, which produces significantly less $\text{NO}_\text{x}$ than the compression-ignition diesel cycle — often 90% below current EPA standards. Furthermore, because they do not rely on high-pressure injection of liquid fuel, they produce virtually zero PM.

Analysis has quantified the benefit of replacing the oldest 130,000 diesel trucks in the 31 target counties with RNG models [217]. The results were profound:

* **Avoided Mortality:** More than 100 premature deaths avoided annually.
* **Morbidity Benefits:** 230 fewer hospital admissions and 660 fewer asthma onsets per year.
* **Economic Savings:** Approximately $2 billion in annual healthcare savings.

Independent findings confirm RNG achieves approximately 88% of the public health benefits of full electrification. This is a critical insight for the Messy Middle. While BEVs might represent a destination goal of zero tailpipe emissions, RNG offers nearly all the local air quality benefits today through access to existing natural gas infrastructure and commercially available vehicles, without the massive capital cost and delays of building a megawatt-charging grid. Conversely, it would be oversimplification to suggest growth in RNG capacity and distribution does not also come with significant capital cost or delay in market availability.

**The BEV Promise & Challenge**
The battery-electric truck is the gold standard for local health. With zero tailpipe emissions, a BEV eliminates street-level $\text{NO}_\text{x}$, PM, and VOCs entirely. In a 31-county study, replacing the legacy diesel fleet with BEVs yielded the highest theoretical health savings, valued at approximately $34.4 billion over a 15-year period.

However, the well-to-wheel reality adds nuance. Run on Less BEV fleets demonstrate while the trucks are clean, the grid which fueled their charging activity varies in upstream emission output.

For example:
* Electric - Rialto, CA Logistics charged on the Southern California Edison grid (low carbon intensity), delivered a massive net emission reduction.
* Electric - Phoenix, AZ Logistics operating across AZ, NM, and TX, drew power from grids with higher coal and gas mixtures. While still cleaner than diesel, their upstream $\text{SO}_\text{x}$ and PM footprint was non-zero.

Furthermore, BEVs still produce non-exhaust emissions. The sheer weight of the battery packs (often adding 8,000+ lbs. to the chassis) can increase tire and road wear, generating coarse PM. However, the use of regenerative braking drastically reduces brake dust emissions, likely resulting in a net neutral or positive impact on non-exhaust PM compared to diesel.

**FCEVs — Hydrogen & the Future**
The two FCEVs piloted in Run on Less – Messy Middle offer a middle ground. Like BEVs, they have zero tailpipe emissions (emitting only water vapor). Their health impact is entirely determined by the production method of the hydrogen.

* If hydrogen is made from natural gas without carbon capture, the upstream criteria pollutants (from the SMR plants) and GHG emissions are significant, offering little advantage over diesel on a lifecycle basis.
* If hydrogen is produced via renewables, FCEVs offer the same zero-emission health profile as BEVs but with the range and fueling speed benefit of diesel.

# 10 KEY FINDINGS

The study team identified key findings impacting the transition of the North American heavy-duty trucking industry.

1. **Fuel/Energy Origin Outweigh Engine/Powertrain Technology:** The carbon footprint of a truck is determined more by the origin of the fuel molecule than the metal of the engine block. For example, RNG derived from dairy waste has the potential to make a vehicle carbon-negative, regardless of the internal combustion engine's inherent inefficiency. A BEV can only be as clean as the grid that charges it. BEV operational carbon reductions can range from 62% to more than 99% depending on whether the truck is charged on a fossil-heavy grid or a clean, nuclear/hydro-backed grid, like Northern California. California’s grid puts BEVs on par with RNG and biodiesel today.
2. **Alternative Fuels Achieve Diesel-Like Performance:** The era of yesterday’s underpowered alternative energy truck is over. The latest 15-liter natural gas engines successfully powered 140,000-lb. loads in Canadian conditions and maintained highway speeds with triple trailers across US highways, proving they can handle the most demanding heavy-haul applications. This expanded powertrain capability brings a new reality to decarbonizing heavy haul and long distant route segments. Renewable natural gas, renewable diesel, and biodiesel are positioned to significantly reduce emissions within these segments.
3. **Decarbonization as a Public Health Need:** The health impact from freight emissions are large and unevenly distributed. Fossil fuel emissions (diesel and coal) cost the US economy between $100 billion and $1 trillion annually in health damages, according to the US Congress’ Joint Economic Committee. Reducing emissions in populated freight corridors leads to significant reductions in negative health outcomes and healthcare cost expenditures. A transition of the oldest diesel trucks in major cities to any modern alternative (RNG, BEV, or FCEV) would yield immediate, high-value health returns.
4. **The Driver Impact:** Drivers have as significant an impact on fuel economy and emissions reductions as do the truck technologies. Well-driven trucks on predictable, well-known routes where operations are optimized to avoid congestion and delays can achieve significantly better performance with all fuel types.
5. **Fleets Should Include Emissions Considerations in their Decisions:** As fleets look at the many options available when considering their next truck purchases, it is important to include emissions considerations along with operational and total cost of ownership in decision making.

# APPENDIX – EXPLANATION OF CALCULATIONS IN SECTION 9

This appendix contains explanations for calculations uses in Section 9: Emissions Summary — Cross-Fleet Comparison.

* Well-to-wheel emissions were calculated using Argonne National Lab’s GREET model.
* Fuel inputs were changed from default values to reflect the region where most of each truck’s activity occurred.
* Natural gas production’s region was assumed to be the same as the delivery region, such as Southwest-produced fuel being provided to California and Southeast-produced fuel being provided to Texas.
* Hydrogen was assumed to be created via steam methane reforming of natural gas produced in the region of the vehicle’s operation.
* Biodiesel was assumed to be sourced from soybeans, and renewable diesel was assumed to be sourced from tallow.
* Because the Kleysen truck operated in Canada and GREET is a US-focused model, it is assumed to have upstream fuel characteristics comparable to the US Rocky Mountain region. The Schneider truck was assumed to use 50% RD and 50% traditional fossil diesel. Because it refueled in multiple locations, the precise blend the truck used during the Run is not known, so 50% was chosen as a benchmark.
* Electricity was assumed to reflect the California grid for trucks primarily operating in the state, while the other trucks used the emissions profiles associated with the larger grid covering the region. For example, Texas Regional Entity covers much of southern Texas, and Western Electricity Coordinating Council represents most of the western US. Emissions factors are sourced from EPA and EIA data given in g/kWh, which does not reflect the hourly carbon intensity differences in electricity generation.
* Emissions associated with vehicle manufacturing and fuel delivery infrastructure (chargers, wells, fuel pumps, power plants, pipelines, etc.) were not considered in this analysis. However, the energy used to refine and transport fuels using that infrastructure was included.
