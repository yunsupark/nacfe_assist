**Mike Roeth:** In 2009, the Department of Energy announced and invested in four programs to improve the efficiency of Class 8 heavy-duty tractor trailers. And four teams took off and took that challenge.

> **Slide/Figure — Department of Energy logo**
> Type: graphic/logo
> Notes: DEPARTMENT OF ENERGY UNITED STATES OF AMERICA

<!-- timestamp: 00:40 -->
**Mike Roeth:** One was led by Cummins with Peterbilt, another with Navistar, Daimler and Volvo. And they only delivered incredible vehicles. We've seen that in the press. Those vehicles performed and operated in real-world testing conditions through the duty cycle assumed by the engineers at those companies, and they showed efficiency in the 11 to 13-mile-per-gallon range.

<!-- timestamp: 00:59 -->
**Mike Roeth:** First of all, weight and payload matter. The DOE SuperTruck programs had a 65,000-pound expectation or design limit, and so the trucks were designed for that heavy load. And the trucks operating in Run On Less are at a lower level of of weight generally.

<!-- timestamp: 01:15 -->
**Mike Roeth:** Also the SuperTruck programs were designed with a pretty difficult duty cycle for their MPG demonstrations, which challenged the trucks and the drivers to get to the high levels. Some of the technologies incorporated in the SuperTrucks don't exist in these current trucks.

<!-- timestamp: 01:30 -->
**Mike Roeth:** So some of the items like waste heat recovery and some of the other electrification technologies that were put in the demonstrators from the DOE SuperTruck program are not showing current payback for fleets or for manufacturers to bring them to production.

<!-- timestamp: 01:44 -->
**Mike Roeth:** So this level of Run On Less performance of currently available technologies is very impressive for what they're doing with the equipment that the OEMs have given them. And kudos to the OEMs for so quickly bringing the SuperTruck designs into their current production.
