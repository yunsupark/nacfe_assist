# Introduction

As communities across the globe work to clean up local air pollution, protect public health, and address climate change, EVs have emerged as a solution. Over the past decade, EV technology has improved in both cost and performance and EVs have become increasingly popular among consumers. This is especially true for the light-duty vehicle market, which, in the United States, achieved its best-ever quarter on record for EV sales in the fourth quarter of 2021, reaching a 6% share of new sales.²

While cars account for most carbon emissions from transportation, medium- and heavy-duty vehicles (MHDVs), predominantly trucks, are a close second. MHDVs collectively emit over 561 million metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) — nearly one-quarter of all transportation sector emissions, even though they account for only 10% of vehicles on the road.³ The problem is growing; emissions from trucks have nearly doubled in the past 30 years, increasing as the industry expands to accommodate a growing economy and an increase in e-commerce.⁴

Trucks, which are the vast majority of MHDVs, are also responsible for a significant amount of air pollution such as nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter. These air pollutants disproportionately affect communities located along major highway corridors and near industrial zones like ports and warehouses, which tend to be among the most economically disadvantaged in the country.⁵

Although the external costs created by diesel trucks are significant, the trucking industry is vital to the US economy. The industry moves over 70% of goods in the country and employs nearly 9 million workers.⁶ Preserving the economic benefits associated with trucking while eliminating these negative externalities is critical.

Fortunately, development in EV technology has led to breakthroughs in electric medium- and heavy-duty vehicle (e-MHDV) production. Today, while supply is still relatively low, there are dozens of battery-electric truck models available in the United States and Canada alone, and that number is increasing rapidly to meet demand.⁷ Across the country, fleets have already voluntarily committed to deploying over 140,000 electric vehicles.⁸

Although this trend is enabled by the improvements in technology and capabilities of electric trucks, regulation is also encouraging e-MHDV adoption. In 2020, California adopted the ACT regulation, which requires truck builders to sell an increasing percentage of electric trucks in the state.⁹ Subsequently, 14 additional states — Connecticut, Colorado, Hawaii, Maine, Maryland, Massachusetts, New Jersey, New York, North Carolina, Oregon, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont, and Washington — and the District of Columbia signed a joint memorandum of understanding (MOU) to follow California’s path. In that MOU, all 16 signers committed to work collaboratively to advance and accelerate the market for e-MHDVs, with the goal of ensuring “that 100% of all new medium- and heavy-duty vehicle sales be zero-emissions vehicles by 2050, with an interim target of 30% zero-emissions vehicle sales by 2030.”¹⁰

<!-- page: 7 -->
To complement this supply push with market signals around demand, California is also considering adopting an Advanced Clean Fleets (ACF) regulation. That rule would require specific fleets operating trucks in the state to transition to zero-emissions vehicles more quickly than ACT regulations call for. ACF goals include, where feasible, 100% zero-emissions drayage trucks by 2035, 100% off-road zero-emissions vehicles (ZEVs) and equipment by 2035, and 100% MHDV ZEVs by 2045 for drayage fleets, public agency fleets, federal government fleets, and other high priority fleets that gross more than $50 million in annual revenue or operate more than 50 MHDVs.¹¹ Additionally, the government of Canada aims to achieve 35% zero-emissions vehicle sales by 2030 for MHDVs, with plans to require a subset of MHDV categories to be 100% ZEV by 2040.¹²

While e-MHDV trucks are a promising technology, they are not yet viable for all use cases. For example, long-haul trucking electrification remains unattainable with existing e-MHDV models and charging infrastructure. However, other market segments like urban and regional trucking can be electrified immediately. For these types of trucks, action and investment need not wait. Over 2,000 e-MHDVs are currently operating in the United States, most of which are buses. About 500 of these e-MHDVs are vans, box trucks, and tractors used for freight movement.¹³

Sharing lessons learned and success from these early adopter fleets will help build confidence in the wider market. Some of these early e-MHDV adopters participated in RMI and NACFE’s Run on Less–Electric (RoL–E) demonstration. RoL–E was the latest edition of NACFE’s long-running, biennial showcase of technology developments in freight efficiency. In September 2021, 13 fleets participated in the RoL–E project, which exclusively featured battery-electric vehicles. The vehicles participating in RoL–E included electric vans, walk-in vans, box trucks, tractors, and terminal tractors driving an assortment of duty cycles.¹⁴ Insights gained from early-adopter fleets are valuable for understanding how the market will be able to transform and what the associated pain points are.

Although the growing push for zero-emissions trucks is encouraging and the capabilities of electric trucks are sufficient for hauling freight in many applications, some important questions remain, specifically regarding charging. Addressing these questions will be critical to sustaining trucking electrification’s growing momentum. For example:

* How quickly can and will commercial trucking fleets electrify?
* What sort of charging infrastructure will be required?
* How will truck charging affect the electric grid, and what investments will utilities need to make to enable truck charging?

While we can’t predict the future, we try to answer these questions using data. RMI, NACFE, and Geotab have teamed up to produce much needed insights into which segments of the trucking market can be electrified first and what charging infrastructure will be required to enable that transition.¹⁵

# Analytical Approach

We know qualitatively that many trucks travel relatively short daily distances and return to a home base at night. In theory, existing battery-electric trucks should be able to meet the daily range needs of those vehicles, and home-base charging should be largely sufficient to meet their energy needs. However, given the speed at which the transition to electric trucks is happening, it is critical to move beyond qualitative assessments. Fortunately, many trucks are outfitted with telematics devices that collect detailed information on how they behave. That data can deliver robust insights into the real-world potential for truck electrification. In partnership with Geotab, a leading provider of telematics in North America, we have developed a methodology to deliver such insights.

We use aggregated and anonymized telematics data from Geotab to understand the behavior of MHDV trucks. Although the data provided by Geotab for this analysis is exclusively from ICE trucks, the observed driving patterns of these vehicles can be used to determine which trucks could be replaced by EVs the soonest based on existing EV technology and charging infrastructure. Furthermore, we use the same data to estimate the charging needs of the EV replacements and identify the potential emissions and cost impacts associated with electrifying these MHDVs.

For this analysis, we have defined an electrifiable vehicle as one that returns to a home base after fewer than 300 miles of travel in 95% of instances. These two key conditions — limited travel distance and a return to a fixed base — are intended to capture the two main constraints on real-world operation of electric trucks: limited vehicle range and the lack of a public or shared charging network for trucks. We considered trucks that traveled electrifiable distances on the vast majority of their trips because, in the real world, truck activity varies from day to day and meeting the average needs of an operator is not enough. To be adopted, electric trucks need to be able to perform not just in average scenarios, but also on more demanding days when longer distances are required.

Currently available e-MHDV models are not able to travel more than 300 miles between charging sessions (although 500 mile-range vehicles have been promised by some manufacturers). Furthermore, interviews with fleets currently using electric trucks and direct experience from NACFE’s RoL–E demonstration suggest that for the next several years at least, commercial trucks will not be able to rely on public or shared charging.

This analysis assumes that an electric truck must be able to replace an existing diesel or gasoline vehicle by performing the same duty cycle. In reality, operators are likely to customize their operations to the capabilities of EVs. However, in this analysis we assume that operators will only adopt EVs when they can perform the typical activities of a competing ICE vehicle.

## Data Used

In this report, we focus on California and New York as case studies in electric truck deployments and charging; however, the models used are broadly applicable in North America. The analysis in this report is based on aggregated and anonymized from medium- (gross vehicle weight rating [GVWR] classes 3–6) and heavy-duty (GVWR classes 7–8) commercial vehicles on Geotab’s telematics platform. Geotab, a global leader in IoT and connected transportation, is committed to helping its customers navigate the EV transition, as evidenced by its EV Suitability Assessment (EVSA) tool and partnership on this analysis.¹⁶

The subset of Geotab data from California and New York includes approximately 10% of medium- and heavy-duty vehicles domiciled in these states operating between January and December 2019 and was therefore not affected by the COVID-19 pandemic.ⁱ Vehicle domiciles are based on geographical zones in which the vehicles spend most of their parked time and are not based on specific addresses or fleet facilities, as that information is protected. In order to share the telematics data with permission from fleets, the data has been anonymized via aggregation. In other words, data from individual vehicles is not available, but summary statistics from groups of vehicles are.

Data observed includes aggregated information about when vehicles were stopped at domicile or away from domicile, distance traveled between stops, speed, and time spent moving or stopped. The data is aggregated by Geotab at two time intervals — hourly (i.e., distributions of operational statistics for each hour of the day) and daily (distributions of operational statistics per day). Additional details about the Geotab data can be found in Appendix A.

## Modeling Framework

To estimate the charging needs and impacts of trucking electrification, we developed a multistep modeling framework (Exhibit 1). First, we identify the subset of vehicles that can be most easily electrified in the near term. As mentioned, we consider vehicles that spend most of their time traveling fewer than 300 miles between domicile stops to be electrifiable. We do not consider total cost of ownership (TCO) or payload of vehicles, although these details should be considered in a future analysis. Including payload could have implications on the definition of electrifiability (300 miles may be too long for heavy loads) and efficiency, which is needed for load calculations. Additionally, we do not consider factors that may affect range, such as weather and road grade. We use our definition of electrifiable (i.e., traveling fewer than 300 miles between domicile stops) to easily parse the Geotab data into two groups of vehicles: electrifiable and other.

Although weight may be a limiting factor for MHDV electrification because electric trucks weigh more than ICE trucks, we did not exclude any vehicles from the electrifiable subset based on weight limitations because we did not have access to payload data.

Further, there is a 2,000-pound federal weight exemption for EVs to compensate for the heavier powertrain components, and California has also increased its limits. (New York currently offers the 2,000-pound weight exemption to natural gas vehicles but not to EVs.¹⁷)

Finally, there is limited data on the topic but anecdotal evidence suggests that most freight vehicles fill their cargo space before reaching maximum weight, though this will not be true for all fleets. Those with

### Fleet Electrification Timeline

We analyzed two scenarios for fleet electrification: one with an average vehicle replacement age of 15 years, and a second with an average vehicle replacement age of 10 years. For both scenarios, we assume that states would meet ACT sales targets through 2035,¹⁸ and that MHDV vehicle populations would grow proportional to US MHDV population growth estimates from the US Energy Information Administration’s Annual Energy Outlook.¹⁹

### Vehicle Emissions Analysis

To estimate CO₂e emissions from charging electric trucks, we assume that vehicles would distribute their charging demand uniformly throughout their time at domicile.ⁱⁱ We calculate the expected emissions from charging by multiplying the proportion of demand occurring each hour of the day by the projected emissions on the grid at that hour. We use long-run marginal emissions from the standard scenario of National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s Cambium model to estimate hourly emissions of vehicle charging in California and New York in future years.²⁰

To calculate the expected grid emissions from charging per mile of driving, we assume that on average medium-duty trucks (MDTs) consume on average 1.3 kilowatt-hours per mile and heavy-duty trucks (HDTs) consume 2.5 kilowatt-hours per mile, which is consistent with the conservative end of California Air Resources Board’s estimates.²¹ We also incorporate a 30% reduction in efficiency for vehicles in New York during winter months to account for reduced EV efficiency due to cold weather.

### Charging Needs Assessment

To estimate the charging energy required to meet the duty cycles of electrifiable MHDVs, we calculate total expected energy demand per day and use average daily time at domicile to determine the length of the available charging window. Rather than size charging infrastructure for an average vehicle day, we assume that fleet owners size charging infrastructure with some margin for longer days. We therefore estimate total daily energy demand using the 75th percentile of daily distance driven.

To account for charging ramp rates, we also assume that chargers operate at an average of 70% nameplate capacity. For simplicity, we assume that each vehicle has a dedicated charger. It is likely that costs could be further reduced by sharing fewer high-powered chargers among multiple vehicles. Such cost-minimization approaches are the subject of ongoing research at RMI.

# Electrification from Advanced Clean Trucks Regulation

Currently, the pace of truck electrification is largely dictated by regulatory mandates — the maximum pace of truck electrification is the same as the minimum pace required by regulators. Although electric vehicles are better from both climate and public health perspectives, vehicle and infrastructure costs, as well as fleet hesitancy toward new technology, pose real challenges. Nevertheless, more e-MHDVs are becoming available and ACT regulation will require truck manufacturers to sell an increasing percentage of zero-emissions vehicles in California; by 2035, 40% to 75% of trucks sold, depending on class, must be zero-emissions.²³ Fourteen other states, including New York, plus the District of Columbia, have signed an MOU agreeing to adopt the ACT rule as part of their commitment to strive for 100% zero-emissions medium- and heavy-duty vehicle sales no later than 2050, with an interim goal of 30% zero-emissions sales by 2030.²⁴

We explore two ACT scenarios, one in which MHDV vehicles are retired after a 15-year life (shown in light blue) and one in which MHDV vehicles are retired after a 10-year life (shown in dark blue). These two scenarios indicate that, in addition to new vehicle sales targets such as those proposed in ACT, vehicle life spans are also a significant factor affecting the overall makeup of the vehicle population. Accelerated vehicle turnover could occur if vehicle TCO is significantly reduced through electrification.

# Energy Needs

Trucking electrification is only possible if vehicle charging is available. Even for trucks that return to base to charge, charging trucks is not as easy as simply plugging them into an outlet. Not only will these trucks require dedicated charging infrastructure, but in some instances electric grid upgrades will also be needed.ⁱⁱⁱ As a result, utilities, policymakers, and fleets are all trying to understand how much energy each truck will require, what time of day the trucks will be recharged, and what sort of infrastructure build-out will be necessary. To help answer some of these questions, we estimate daily energy consumption per vehicle.

Based on our analysis, the electrifiable trucks in our dataset would require on average 50 to 400 kilowatt-hours per day — comparable to the electricity used by 2 to 14 homes.²⁵ This amount of energy is generally less than or equivalent to one full charge of the truck’s battery, based on electric truck models currently on the market.²⁶

# Charging Patterns, Infrastructure, and Costs

When it comes to charging electric trucks, the time when electricity is needed and the power at which the electricity is delivered are far more important than the quantity of energy required. Fleets may prefer to have access to the fastest charging possible to “fill up” their trucks’ batteries quickly, more in line with the experience of filling up a diesel tank. However, if the vehicle charges overnight or during long parking periods, it may be more convenient to charge slowly on a dedicated charger so that trucks don’t need to be swapped through the night.

Furthermore, the energy demand spikes that a high-speed charging strategy would induce are problematic for utilities. Rather than fast recharge times, utilities prioritize consistent, predictable loads. Utilities will be able to shape charging behavior through rate making, but we sought to better understand how flexible that charging behavior really could be. Where is the middle ground between fleets being able to quickly recharge their trucks and utilities avoiding grid infrastructure upgrades? How much flexibility could there be while ensuring electric trucks are a viable option from both cost and operational perspectives? Through our analysis, we seek to inform the conversation taking place across the country about what electric truck charging will look like and how best to support it.

Although each fleet depot will develop its own charging plan to maximize individual vehicle uptime while minimizing costs, we present a few generic cases to help illustrate the dynamics at play. We model truck charging for the following three scenarios to examine the relationship among load curves, infrastructure investment, emissions reductions, and electricity bills:

* **Anytime** – Vehicles spread their charging evenly across all time at the depot. Essentially, if the truck is at the depot, we assume it is plugged in and charging.
* **Overnight** – Trucks charge only during the lowest demand periods for the grid, when electricity tends to be cheapest. We assume they charge only from 10 p.m. to 8 a.m. when at the depot.
* **Daytime** – Trucks charge when the grid has high renewables penetration — when solar resources are generating the most power. We assume trucks charge only from 9 a.m. to 2 p.m. when at the depot.

The anytime charging scenario allows for the largest possible charging window, minimizing the needed charging power. Overnight charging is an attractive charging option for vehicles that are largely at their domiciles overnight and in areas where there is excess cheap power at night, but this strategy becomes increasingly unattractive when cheap solar is abundant. Daytime charging often results in the lowest grid emissions and may also result in low costs in grids that have a lot of solar energy.

The electrifiable trucks are largely parked at their depots overnight (Exhibit 7). Although this is not a representative schedule for any individual vehicle, the trends indicate that trucks are generally domiciled overnight and driving or stopped away from the domicile during the day. The amount of time spent stopped at the domicile over the course of the day, combined with the energy a truck expends during driving, dictates when and how quickly vehicles need to charge and what types of chargers trucks will require to abide by our three charging scenarios.

# Charger Capacity and Costs

One of the biggest costs associated with charging is the up-front cost to purchase the charging hardware and have it installed. Cost varies greatly by charger power level. For example, a 19.2 kW Level 2 (L2) charger may cost $20,000 to procure and install, while a 150 kW direct current fast charger (DCFC) may cost $135,000 or more. Although these high-powered chargers enable faster charging, they can also lead to additional costs for both fleets and utilities.

Demand charges are fees on an electricity bill that are based on the maximum amount of power drawn from the grid during a billing period. Using high-powered chargers can dramatically increase demand charges. Additionally, installing high-powered chargers may require utilities to run new wires to the depot or even install new transformers, both expensive propositions. Depending on the utility, either fleets or the utility’s customers would have to pay for those upgrades.²⁹ In both cases, lower-powered chargers — when feasible — end up being cheaper for utilities and fleets.

Based on our analysis, the anytime scenario required the lowest-powered chargers, followed by overnight, and then daytime. In the anytime scenario, MDTs would generally be able to charge sufficiently using 16.6 kW chargers while HDTs would require 50 kW chargers. For comparison, the daytime scenario would require seven to nine times those power levels, with MDTs using 150 kW chargers and HDTs needing 350 kW chargers.

Utilities have the opportunity to create cost structures to incentivize charging during times when renewable generation is high. # Key Findings

Overall, we know that larger vehicle classes can begin to electrify using commercially available vehicles. This electrification results in dramatically lower carbon emissions per mile compared with diesel using the electric grids of California and New York to recharge. Nevertheless, projected increased freight demand over the next decades means that even with electrification that follows ACT regulation, overall freight emissions will not likely decrease significantly compared with today. Considering this, trucking electrification is critically important to ensure that trucking-related emissions do not increase in the future. Electrification is possible, but fleets will need support from a large group of players, and utilities will need to ensure that electric truck charging can occur at a reasonable price.

Based on these results, a few takeaways are clear:

* **With existing technology, many trucks can be electrified and continue to be used in a similar manner as ICE trucks.** For trucks domiciled in California and New York, we found that 65% of MDTs and 49% of HDTs are electrifiable today. As electric truck ranges increase and public or shared truck-charging infrastructure becomes available, even more of the ICE trucks on the road today will be electrifiable.
* **Electric trucks, regardless of when they charge, offer significant greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions reductions per mile compared with diesel vehicles.** Considering the quantity of emissions associated with freight movement in North America, the potential reductions resulting from electrification provide an unmissable opportunity.
* **Given demand charges and high infrastructure costs, policymakers, regulators, and utilities should encourage fleets to charge at lower power levels over longer periods of time.** Charging at lower power levels outside of peak grid demand times is the best strategy for minimizing both capital and operational expenses related to charging.
* **Utilities will need to provide rate structures that allow MHDV trucks to electrify economically.** Using big trucking datasets, such as the one discussed in this report, can help utilities understand the large-scale impacts of their rate structures on fleet electrification.
* **Fleets will require support.** Managing electric trucks represents a steep learning curve, particularly regarding optimal charging strategies. Utilities should participate in truck electrification by offering fleet customers free or low-cost advisory services to co-develop charging strategies that minimize overall charging costs, which will benefit the grid and fleets.

# Next Steps

We are working collaboratively with the US Department of Energy to undertake similar analyses for each of the remaining 14 states and District of Columbia that have signed the ACT MOU. We invite fleets, utilities, policymakers, regulators, charging providers, and other stakeholders with an interest in this work to contact us.

Furthermore, we intend to conduct additional detailed work regarding charging costs. In addition to the up-front capital expense for chargers, fleets must also consider the cost of the electricity they will use, and we plan to explore the impact of time-of-use rates, demand charges, low-carbon fuel standards, and more on the overall charging costs associated with trucking electrification.

We also plan to investigate the financial implications of on-site storage, renewable energy resources (like solar power), and/or microgrids that may help fleets maximize GHG reductions and minimize demand charges from the grid.
