# 1 INTRODUCTION

This Confidence Report forms part of the continued work of the North American Council for Freight Efficiency
(NACFE), highlighting the potential of fuel efficiency technologies and practices in over-the-road (OTR) goods
movement. Prior Confidence Reports and initial findings on nearly 86 available technologies can be found at
www.nacfe.org.

According to the American Transportation Research Institute (ATRI), fuel is one of the largest operating
expenses for trucking fleets, accounting for roughly 20% to 25% of total operating costs, second only to driver
wages and benefits [1]. A substantial portion of this fuel is consumed while trucks are stationary, as long-haul
drivers often idle engines for cabin climate control and to power devices like microwaves, TVs, and CPAP
machines during rest periods [2]. A typical Class 8 truck idles at around 0.8 gallons of diesel per hour (Figure 1),
resulting in 1,000 to 1,800 gallons of diesel burned annually per vehicle, depending on climate, duty cycle, and
usage patterns [3]. With diesel prices averaging $3.81 per gallon as of July 2025 [4], this equates to $3,500 to
more than $6,000 annually in idle-related fuel costs per truck (Figure 2).

Prolonged idling also accelerates engine wear, requires more frequent oil changes, and raises maintenance
costs, particularly due to strain on diesel particulate filters (DPFs) and aftertreatment systems [5].
Environmentally, each gallon of diesel emits approximately 22.4 lbs. (10.1 kg) of carbon dioxide (CO₂), meaning
that eliminating excessive idling can avoid up to 15 metric tons of CO₂ emissions per truck yearly [6].

Both the economic and environmental stakes of idling are high. At an average diesel price of $4 per gallon, a
single long-haul truck might waste $4,000 to $5,000 worth of fuel each year if it idles overnight. Multiply that
across an entire fleet, and the costs are staggering. Environmentally, eliminating unnecessary idling is one of the
quickest ways to cut emissions. Each idling truck emits CO₂, NOx, and particulate matter (PM) that contribute to
air pollution. Many jurisdictions have responded with anti-idling regulations, typically limiting idling to 5 to 10
minutes when a truck is not in traffic. For example, California has some of the strictest anti-idling rules, with the

California Air Resources Board (CARB) enforcing a 5-minute limit and additional requirements for trucks
operating in the state [7]. Canada’s major provinces likewise have anti-idling laws in cities and incentives
through programs like Natural Resources Canada’s (NRCan) energy initiatives.

For fleet operators, the challenge lies in finding practical ways to reduce idle time without compromising driver
comfort, safety, or operational efficiency (Figure 3). Drivers require heating or air conditioning during mandated
rest periods and increasingly depend on onboard electrical appliances (lighting, microwaves, refrigerators,
televisions, CPAP breathing machines, laptops, etc.) that can drain batteries. Therefore, any idle reduction
strategy must provide an alternative heating, cooling, and auxiliary power source. Over the years, various
technologies and practices have been developed to meet this need.

The following sections of this report offer a historical perspective and then explore the technologies available
today — from simple techniques to high-tech solutions — along with their adoption and effectiveness. The
report examines strategies for enhancing fuel efficiency and minimizing idling, highlighting established and
innovative technologies, best practices, and policy advancements that provide considerable savings and
environmental benefits for proactive fleets.

Fuel costs have remained volatile, with prices reaching $5.81 per gallon in June 2022 [4]. ATRI continues to track
fuel as the second-largest expense for fleets, behind only driver costs [8]. Although fuel costs have since
moderated, national averages for diesel still hover around $3.81 per gallon as of July 28, 2025. Fuel continues to
account for roughly 25% of a fleet’s total operating costs, underscoring its significant impact on profitability, as
shown in Figure 4. The industry seeks solutions that reduce fuel dependency because diesel prices remain
subject to global market fluctuations and regulatory factors. When fuel costs rise, payback periods on fuel-
saving technologies shorten; conversely, when prices drop, payback analyses should be revisited to reflect the
changing economic conditions.

Investing in established technologies and practices to enhance fuel efficiency for trucks and fleets is not merely
an option; it is a transformative strategy for the industry. Envision achieving the same level of business while
significantly reducing fuel expenditures. The benefits of adopting these innovations become exceedingly
apparent in a landscape where fuel prices fluctuate unpredictably and constitute a substantial portion of a
fleet’s operational budget. By prioritizing fuel efficiency, companies protect their profit margins and establish
themselves as leaders in an increasingly competitive marketplace. Fleets should not allow volatility to dictate
their financial outcomes. They should embrace sustainability and efficiency to secure a more prosperous and
resilient future.

# 2 SCOPE

This report only considers idle-reduction technologies that are currently available on the market for over-the-
road applications. It focuses on the key reasons for idling regarding the benefits fleets seek to obtain from any
idle-reduction technology they might adopt, including:
1. Cost and fuel savings
2. Extended engine/battery life and reduced maintenance
3. Driver comfort and safety — heating/cooling and running hotel loads (TV, refrigerator, microwave, gaming
console, CPAP, fan, laptop, electric blanket, cell phone charger, etc.)
4. Emission reductions
5. Noise reduction

In addition, this report focuses on solo drivers and does not address team drivers, as the percentage of team
drivers is relatively low.

# 3 NACFE’S MISSION

NACFE’s overriding principle in reporting on technologies is to provide an unbiased perspective. NACFE
recognizes that it also has vested interests and an agenda. NACFE’s mission is to improve the efficiency of North
American goods movement. NACFE pursues this goal in two ways: By improving the quality of the information
flow and by highlighting successful adoption of technologies. NACFE’s Confidence Reports assist in information
flow, while the Run on Less demonstrations and Fleet Fuel Studies highlight adoption success and provide
benchmarks for fleets.

NACFE also recognizes that emerging and future technologies need an unbiased review to help fleets consider
them and for manufacturers to learn and adapt their products and solutions to better meet fleets’ needs. To
that end, NACFE started publishing Guidance Reports on emerging and future technologies. As of mid-2025,
NACFE has published 11 Guidance Reports on electric and hydrogen fuel cell trucks.

# 4 REPORT METHODOLOGY

NACFE’s research for this report included interviewing key people with firsthand knowledge of idle-reduction

idle reduction. The report includes a list of references to assist readers interested in pursuing more details.

# 5 BACKGROUND ON IDLE REDUCTION

Before we get into idle technologies and analysis, let’s first look at what factors cause trucks to idle, and why
fleets are interested in minimizing idle time.

## 5.1 Why Idle?

Restarting a diesel engine in cold temperatures is much easier if the engine oil is already warm (and thus less
viscous), and idling also helps prevent cold-weather diesel fuel gelling. The need for climate control within the
cab — heating and cooling — also persists, given that many long-haul truck drivers spend substantial off-duty
time parked in areas without access to shore power or other anti-idling technologies. While idling still can
provide ambient noise and gentle vibration conducive to rest, the industry has increasingly turned to auxiliary
power units (APUs), fuel-operated heaters, and electric auxiliary power units (eAPUs) to reduce idling time and
fuel consumption. In previous work, NACFE referred to these as battery HVAC units, but fleets now are more
commonly using the term eAPUs.

Over the past 20 years, the demand for onboard electronics has surged as drivers increasingly use smartphones,
tablets, laptops, and even small appliances during rest breaks. This rise in personal electronic devices has
increased battery loads and idling requirements on many trucks. Recent public health data continues to show
that truck drivers have higher obesity rates compared to the general workforce. Estimates indicate that more
than 70% of long-haul drivers meet the criteria for obesity (BMI ≥ 30), with a growing number classified as
morbidly obese (BMI ≥ 40). In contrast, about 42% of all US adults are obese, and roughly 9% are morbidly obese
[10]. Several factors contribute to these high rates among drivers:
* Sedentary work conditions (sitting for 8 to 11 hours daily),
* Limited access to healthy food options on the road,
* Irregular sleep schedules,
* Few opportunities for regular exercise, and
* Time pressures that encourage convenience foods.

As driver health has become a bigger priority for fleets, more trucks are being outfitted with refrigerators for
storing fresh food (see Figure 8), CPAP machines to manage sleep apnea, and APUs or eAPUs to power these
health-supporting systems without running the engine [11]. Both contribute to additional power demand.

The introduction of sleeper berths in the trucking industry meant that truck drivers needed a safe, controlled
indoor climate while their trucks were parked in all weather conditions. Some sleeper tractor drivers use idling
to create white noise and a gentle vibration as they try to rest in the middle of a truck stop parking lot, with
vehicles pulling in and out around them all night.

This overall demand for batteries to power electronics is perhaps the most compelling reason for idling,
engaging the alternator, and keeping a truck’s batteries charged to a level sufficient for restarting the vehicle.

Also inherent to the trucking industry is idling in queues, such as waiting for a dock to open or an inspection,
especially at border crossings. Idling at docks or making deliveries is especially problematic for day cab
operators. See Section 9.2 for more information on idle reduction and day cabs. Finally, a few unique instances
within the trucking industry create a need for continuous idling to supply needs, such as in-transport heat to
warm liquid bulk trailers. However, these applications are the exception and not the rule.

This study categorizes idling behavior into eight specific instances. Refer to Section 2 for information on the scope
of this report.

<!-- page: 27 -->
* Cooling and heating the cab/sleeper,
* Maintaining battery charge,
* Powering electronic devices for driver comfort/convenience,
* Keeping the engine warm for improved start-up operations,
* Preventing diesel fuel from gelling,
* Waiting for a dock spot,
* Vocational application requirements, and
* Engine white noise and vibration.

## 5.2 A Brief History of Idle Reduction Techniques & Technologies

What follows is a brief look at the history and evolution of idle reduction technologies fleets have used over the
years.
* **1940s Engine Block Heaters:** One of the earliest idle-reduction devices was the electric engine block
heater (introduced in the 1940s), designed to keep engine coolant and oil warm in cold weather so that
trucks could be started without lengthy idling. By plugging into shore power at a truck stop or terminal,
a block heater allows the main engine to remain off overnight yet start reliably on cold mornings. Figure
10 features a 1940s era truck.

* **1950s Fuel-Operated Heaters:** In 1952, Webasto developed fuel-fired heaters that operate
independently of the vehicle’s engine. Initially used in cars and buses, these heaters were adopted in
trucks. They burn a small amount of diesel fuel to provide heat to the cab (and/or to the engine
coolant), using only about one-eighth of the fuel per hour that an idling main engine would consume.
These bunk heaters became especially popular in cold regions, keeping drivers warm without running
the truck’s main engine. By the late 20th century, fuel-operated heaters were a standard truck option in
Europe and Canada, and eventually saw increasing use by US fleets [13]. Figure 11 shows an early
version of a heater.

* **1970s Oil Crisis and Rising Awareness:** Rising fuel costs and fuel shortages highlighted the issue of
excessive idling, prompting efforts to reduce unnecessary engine running. Two major "oil shocks"
occurred: the first in late 1973 during the oil embargo and the second in 1979 during the Iranian
Revolution, leading to significant oil price increases. Before the 1973 embargo, oil traded at around $3
per barrel. After the 1973 to 1974 embargo, the price of oil roughly quadrupled to around $12 per barrel
by 1974, doubling the price of gasoline. During the 1979 to 1980 Iranian crisis, prices surged from
roughly $14 per barrel to mid-$30. By 1980, oil briefly approached $40 per barrel in nominal terms [14].

* **1970s to 1980s Early APUs and Engine Controls:** Diesel-powered auxiliary power units (APUs) —
essentially small secondary engines or generators mounted on trucks to provide electrical power and

<!-- page: 29 -->
heating and cooling — have existed in various forms since at least the mid-20th century, as they were
used in military and aviation applications during WWII. It’s unclear when the first APU was fitted to a
highway truck, but owner-operators living in their rigs have long used add-on generators or “pony
engines” for comfort power. In the 1980s, another significant development was the advent of electronic
engine controls. As diesel engines became electronically governed, it became possible to program idle
shutdown timers and advanced idle parameters. Truck manufacturers introduced features to
automatically cut off the engine after a preset idle duration and to allow for optimized “fast idle” speeds
(e.g., high idle RPM to run the alternator efficiently) only when needed. These early idle management
settings laid the groundwork for more sophisticated idle-control logic later [15].
* **1990s Growth of APUs and Stricter Emissions Standards:** APUs, originally developed in WWI, became
increasingly popular for providing heating, cooling, and electrical power to sleeper cabs. Although these
early APUs were often expensive and bulky, design advancements made them more practical by the mid
to late 1990s. The 1990 Clean Air Act Amendments sparked further interest in reducing emissions,
including those from idling. Some fleets began experimenting with automatic engine shutoff systems
based on timers or temperature to limit extended idling [16]. Volvo introduced shore power connections
as a factory option with the VN model 1996.
* **Early 2000s Rapid Expansion and Truck Stop Electrification:** Companies like IdleAire, which was founded
in 2000, began to install electrification systems at truck stops, allowing drivers to connect to power for
HVAC and accessories instead of idling. These systems ranged from basic shore power connections to
advanced “above-cab” units that provided HVAC, internet, and other amenities as seen in Figure 13. In
2004, the Environmental Protection Agency’s (EPA) SmartWay Program was launched to encourage
fleets to adopt fuel-saving and emission-reduction technologies, including idle-reduction solutions.
Advances in diesel APU design and battery technology improved reliability. The Bergstrom Nite, an
eAPU, was introduced in 2006, providing a solution that didn’t depend on diesel fuel while delivering
cool air to the sleeper. Many states and municipalities enacted or enforced idle-reduction regulations,
increasing market demand. In 2008, CARB introduced “Clean Idle” stickers, indicating that a particular
engine system met the CARB requirements for extended idling. This initiative has since expanded to
include a separate sticker that shows a diesel APU has a Diesel Particulate Filter (DPF) and complies with
more stringent requirements for California.

* **2010s Next-Generation Systems and Efficiency Gains:** Lighter, higher-capacity batteries and advanced
inverter technologies made it increasingly feasible to power climate controls and hotel loads without
diesel fuel. These systems often paired with solar panels mounted on truck roofs or trailers, which

provided supplemental charging to maintain battery levels during daytime operation or layovers. Figure
14 shows examples of solar panels on a tractor and a trailer. The use of solar power extended the
runtime of eAPU systems, helped maintain battery state-of-charge for engine starts, and reduced
reliance on engine idling. More precise and programmable engine start/stop controllers became
common, automatically starting the engine only when battery voltage dropped or cabin temperature
thresholds were reached, and shutting it down again once conditions were met. Telematics integration
enabled fleets to monitor idle time in real-time, giving dispatchers and drivers tools to identify and
reduce excessive idling. Additionally, data analytics platforms emerged that refined idle-reduction
strategies and informed driver incentive programs [17].

* **2020s and Beyond Recent Developments:** Improved lithium-ion battery packs, which have higher
energy densities, faster recharging times, and lower weights than earlier technologies, have emerged as
a commercially viable option. This advancement has enabled nearly silent “parked HVAC” solutions that
can operate for extended periods. Companies like Thermo King, Carrier, and Phillips & Temro have
introduced fully electric APUs that use these high-capacity lithium-ion batteries to power heating,
cooling, and in-cab devices without running the truck’s engine. Freightliner’s ParkSmart, seen in Figure
15, features a dedicated, battery-based HVAC system integrated directly into the cab from the factory.
Volvo’s I-Park Cool provides an electric, cab-based cooling system designed specifically for idling in hot
weather. Kenworth has enhanced its battery-based climate control packages by offering more powerful
lithium-ion batteries and quicker charging solutions. Several companies have developed solar panels
designed for the tops of truck cabs and trailers. These solar panels charge onboard batteries throughout
the day, helping to power HVAC systems, refrigeration units, and other devices without running the
engine. Geotab and Omnitracs have improved idle-monitoring features, providing fleet managers with
real-time insights into idling events [19]. Dashboards and alerts encourage drivers to follow best
practices, while some fleets incorporate gamification or incentive programs to reduce idle time.
Cummins has refined its connected engine management systems to include more intelligent start/stop
features. These systems automatically shut down the engine after reaching certain idle thresholds or
when specific environmental conditions are met.

## 5.3 Why Minimize Idling?

Over the past two decades, reducing truck idling has become standard practice in the industry. The most
significant reasons for reducing idle time are to decrease fuel consumption; for economic reasons; to achieve
operating goals to maximize throughput therefore minimizing engine wear; and because of mandates that set

### 5.3.4 Extended Engine/Battery Life & Reduced Maintenance

Excessive idling leads to incomplete combustion and residue buildup, particularly on components like piston
rings and the turbocharger, contributing to premature wear. Fewer hours on the engine result in longer intervals
between oil changes and less frequent engine rebuilds. Many engine manufacturers estimate that every hour of
idling equals roughly 25 to 30 miles of driving wear on the engine [23]. Some fleets that have installed automatic
engine shutdown systems (which turn the engine off after a set idle period) have reported 10% to 15% longer
intervals between major engine overhauls [24].

While the alternator does supply power during idling — especially when running HVAC, lighting, and in-cab
electronics — its load is not necessarily high relative to its design capacity. However, alternator output is
reduced at idle RPMs, which may limit its ability to keep up with heavy accessory demands. In such cases,
batteries can be discharged gradually, prompting the system to cycle and recharge more frequently. Electrical
system overheating during extended idling is often linked to high ambient temperatures and limited airflow
around under-hood components, rather than alternator strain alone.

Most diesel engines utilize DPFs to comply with emissions regulations. Idling at low temperatures can hinder
DPF regeneration, resulting in clogging, frequent regenerations, and increased downtime. Reducing idle time
helps maintain the DPF's health for longer, decreasing DPF cleaning costs (ranging from $600 to $2,000 per
service, depending on severity). SCR systems also are susceptible to prolonged idling. By minimizing idle time,
fleets can lower the risk of accelerated degradation and reduce associated maintenance costs.

While data confirming a direct correlation between idle reduction methods and extended DPF and SCR lifecycles
is still being gathered and remains inconclusive, it is worth noting that modern engine controls have significantly
improved aftertreatment system performance. DPF management strategies now support effective regeneration
during stationary idle, and most SCR systems are largely unaffected by extended idling under typical use, with
earlier DEF delivery issues now addressed by engine OEMs.

In older or poorly tuned engines, excessive idling could contribute to water buildup or, in rare cases, fuel dilution
in engine oil. However, these risks are largely mitigated in modern engines with advanced engine controls.
Today, idling has a minimal effect on oil quality under normal operating conditions, though it still contributes to
overall engine hour accumulation and maintenance intervals. Some fleets have reported a 10% to 20% decrease
in annual lubricant costs by implementing strict idle-reduction policies, attributed to fewer oil changes and
extended filter life [25].

## 5.4 Emission & Noise Reductions

Traditional diesel engines burn roughly 0.8 gallons of fuel per hour while idling and reducing idling can
significantly decrease fuel consumption and emissions. Reducing idle time by a few hours a day can reduce
several tons of CO₂ annually per vehicle. The DOE estimates that a Class 8 truck idling an average of 1,800 hours
per year would use approximately 1,500 gallons of diesel (assuming ~0.8 gallons/hour). Burning one gallon of
diesel produces about 22.4 lbs. (10.2 kg) of CO₂, equating to roughly 33,600 lbs. or 15.2 metric tons of CO₂ per
truck annually — emissions that could be avoided by eliminating idle time [23].

While overall emissions during idling are lower as a result of reduced fuel consumption, certain pollutants
behave differently at low engine loads. For example, nitrogen oxides (NOx) emissions are relatively low at idle
because of cooler combustion temperatures. Still, PM and unburned hydrocarbons (VOCs) can be higher relative
to fuel burned, as incomplete combustion is more likely. These pollutants are linked to respiratory and
cardiovascular issues, and VOCs can contribute to ground-level ozone (smog) formation in sunlight.

According to the EPA, idle reduction can lower NOx emissions, though the extent varies widely by duty cycle and
engine technology. In specific high-idle applications — such as long-duration parking with limited aftertreatment
effectiveness — idle time may contribute a disproportionately high share of total NOx output. However, in
typical over-the-road duty cycles, most NOx is generated under load rather than at idle [27].

Reducing or eliminating idling can significantly lower noise levels in and around trucks, enhancing driver comfort
and minimizing noise pollution in nearby communities. A Class 8 diesel truck idling typically produces about 70
to 80 decibels (dB) of noise measured at 5’ to 7’ (1.5 to 2 meters) from the engine’s source. See Figure 21.

## 8.1 Auxiliary Power Units

An auxiliary power unit (APU) is a secondary engine/generator installed on the truck (often mounted on the side rail or back of the cab) that can provide HVAC and electrical power while the main engine is off. Diesel APUs have a small diesel engine (usually 2-cylinder or 3-cylinder) that consumes fuel at a much lower rate than the large truck engine, typically around 0.2 gallons of diesel per hour under moderate load, which is about one-quarter of what an idling truck engine would burn. This engine drives a combination of:
* an alternator or generator to produce electricity (for lights, microwave, TV, etc., and to charge the truck’s batteries),
* a compressor for air conditioning (cooling the cab),
* a coolant heater or small heater core for warmth — both to heat the sleeper for occupant comfort and, in many systems, to keep the engine or fuel warm to ensure easier cold-weather starts.

Diesel APUs generally consume between 0.1 and 0.5 gallons of fuel per hour, depending on their design, the ambient temperature, the sleeper’s insulation, the resulting HVAC load, and the generated AC power. They use various brands of two- or three-cylinder water-cooled engines running on diesel fuel from the truck’s fuel tank, with engine power ranging from 7 to 18 HP.

There are two basic types of diesel APUs depending on how electrical power is supplied to the cab. One type features a 4kW to 6kW generator providing AC power directly to the cab, while a 12V DC alternator delivers power to the truck’s electrical system to charge the batteries. In addition, some diesel APUs are closed systems (closed loop) — they are not plumbed into the truck engine’s cooling system — while others are open systems (open loop) connected to it.

Diesel APUs also vary based on the supplier, the cab configuration, and the placement of heat exchangers. Some units integrate with the truck’s existing air ducting, while others require supplemental ducting. #### 8.1.1.1 Functionality

Cooling capacity varies from 12,000 to 20,000 Btu. Heat can be supplied through heat strips, coils, or a heat pump. Typically, cab heating is provided by a fuel-operated air heater integrated into the APU, with heating capacity spanning 7,500 to 18,000 Btu. #### 8.1.1.4 Generator vs. Inverter Models

Diesel APUs with generators provide 120V AC power directly to the cab and do not rely on the truck’s batteries for this power. They can support maximum hotel loads for power inside and outside the cab without purchasing and installing an inverter. A disadvantage of the generator-based diesel APU system is that the diesel APU engine and generator must be run to access any AC power needs.

Inverter-based diesel APUs rely on the diesel APU’s alternator to provide power to the DC truck batteries, which is converted to AC power through an inverter located in the sleeper. As a result, the engines of inverter-based diesel APUs only run when the system’s batteries require recharging, except for HVAC requirements. Given the hotel power demands, the truck batteries might need more frequent replacement because of the increased charging and discharging cycles these systems incur. On a positive note, this diesel APU configuration is generally lighter and cheaper to acquire than a generator-based system, usually consuming less fuel and requiring slightly less maintenance.

Diesel APUs have seen incremental improvements in the last 5 to 10 years. Manufacturers have worked to make diesel APUs smaller, quieter, and cleaner. #### 8.1.1.7 Adoption Rate

The adoption of APUs in long-haul fleets grew significantly in the late 2000s and 2010s. By the late 2010s, many US long-haul trucks had diesel APUs (estimates vary, but some industry surveys put it at 30% to 40% of sleeper trucks). eAPUs, being newer, had a smaller share but were gaining interest, especially among fleets concerned about emissions or weight. Many large fleets (and most owner-operators who live in their trucks) invest in APUs for the comfort and autonomy they provide drivers. In Canada, diesel APUs have been common among owner-operators, whereas larger Canadian fleets often lean toward using bunk heaters plus occasional idling (because of the high cost of an APU). However, as anti-idling regulations tighten (and some provinces consider mandating zero-emission idle solutions), interest in eAPUs is rising.

#### 8.1.1.9 Challenges of Diesel APUs

While there are many benefits to using diesel APUs, as with any technology solution, there also are challenges.
* **Up-front price and payback risk:** Installed costs of $8,500 to $13,000 are substantial; savings disappear if diesel prices fall or the truck idles for less than roughly 1,000 hours per year [49].
* **Weight and space penalty:** A complete diesel APU, condenser, and bunk evaporator add 400 lbs. to 550 lbs. and take up frame rail real estate, often requiring drivers to rely on the 550 lbs. federal/state weight exemption to avoid payload losses.
* **Extra maintenance:** The APU’s engine needs oil, filters, and valve adjustments every 500 to 2,000 operating hours, in addition to periodic fuel system and HVAC service, which adds labor and parts that the truck would otherwise avoid [50].
* **Residual fuel use, emissions and noise:** Even at 0.25 gal. per hour, diesel APUs still burn fuel, emit $CO_2$, $NO_x$, PM, and produce approximately 60 dBA of sound outside the cab — issues that electric or fuel cell APUs sidestep [50].
* **Regulatory complexity:** California already requires a Level 3 DPF or routing APU exhaust through the truck’s DPF. CARB is reviewing whether to further tighten hours of use and health limits, so compliance costs could increase [51].
* **Installation and service network constraints:** Upfitting can be tricky on short wheelbase tractors or those with fairings, and fleets operating far from OEM dealers may face delays in parts or warranty service.

#### 8.1.2.1 Functionality

Cooling capacity for the units varies from 4,600 to 10,000 Btu/hour. Cooling is influenced by a variety of factors, including the size of the sleeper/cab, amount of insulation in the vehicle, size of windows, sun loading (when the sun itself heats up the interior of the cab), exterior paint color, as well as the ambient temperature in which the vehicle is operating. Most eAPU system manufacturers say their units can provide eight to 10 hours of cooling capacity. Real-world results vary depending on ambient temperature, whether the driver pre-cooled the vehicle, and whether the eAPU system also is used to power electronic devices in the sleeper/cab while the truck’s engine is off.

In everyday operation with the engine running, an eAPU’s high-capacity AGM or lithium-ion battery pack charges from the engine’s alternator. Some fleets enhance this by adding 300- to 600-watt thin-film solar panels, which harvest supplemental energy and store it in the battery bank for later use. However, one of the most frequent complaints among eAPU users is inadequate battery charging during drive time. This is often the result of insufficient alternator output — most systems require at least a 250-amp alternator, and many manufacturers recommend 270- to 320-amp units to ensure full recharge by the end of a shift. It’s important to note that generating this energy still requires fuel. The good news is that when the truck moves freight, the engine operates more efficiently, making alternator-driven charging far more economical than idling to power hotel loads.

With the engine turned off, in hotel mode, the energy stored in the batteries can power air conditioning, hotel loads, and, in some configurations, heating systems. The in-cab inverter/charger powers a 12- or 120-V circuit, plus a sealed electric compressor and evaporator (7,000 to 9,500 Btu per hour typically). The system can maintain sleeper climate and hotel loads (microwave, CPAP, laptop, etc.) for eight to 10 hours, depending on the configuration.

A plug-in port or reefer-alternator interface for eAPUs keeps batteries at 100% charge during terminal dwell time or refrigerated operation. This provides continuous runtime without starting the tractor.

#### 8.1.2.2 Components

The basic components of an eAPU include deep-cycle batteries (usually four AGM batteries), a high-performance alternator, a DC-to-AC inverter, air conditioning components and a controller/thermostat.

Most eAPUs systems are specified with optional heating from fuel-operated heaters.

The air conditioning units can be split or self-contained. With a split system, the condenser is located outside the sleeper/cab, while the evaporator/compressor is inside. All components are located in a storage compartment or under the bunk in a self-contained unit. An additional battery box must be installed to house the auxiliary batteries needed to operate the eAPU.

There are multiple options for the electrical setup of the eAPU. Sometimes, all the batteries in the truck are interconnected, while in other instances, the eAPU batteries are kept separate from the primary batteries to allow engine restarts when necessary. Each system includes a low-voltage warning to notify the operator if the battery charge level becomes too low. There are different ways to integrate inverter-chargers into the vehicle's electrical system. Collaborating closely with equipment suppliers is recommended to meet fleet requirements optimally.

#### 8.1.2.3 Benefits of Electric APUs

Here are the key benefits of installing an eAPU on a truck to limit idling and provide driver comfort.
* **Potentially significant fuel savings:** By replacing the tractor’s idling 15-liter engine with an eAPU, fleets can save up to 2,500 gallons of diesel per truck per year, depending on duty cycle, climate, and usage patterns. More typical savings range from 1,200 to 1,800 gallons annually for long-haul trucks with consistent rest periods.
* **Lower emissions and anti-idling compliance:** Zero emissions are produced while running, which helps fleets meet numerous state and city regulations and corporate ESG objectives.
* **Reduced engine wear and maintenance:** Shutting down the main engine during idle hours reduces oil change frequency, extends DPF life, and avoids maintaining a second diesel (as with a diesel APU).
* **Quieter, healthier rest for drivers:** With no combustion engine running, eAPUs deliver climate control in near silence, reducing vibration and cab noise to enhance sleep quality and driver wellness.
* **Stronger driver-comfort value proposition:** Modern eAPUs power in-cab refrigerators, CPAP machines, laptops, and game consoles for up to 10 hours (AGM) or 15+ hours (lithium) [55].
* **Quicker payback and positive TCO:** When diesel is approximately $4 per gallon, the fuel alone pays for a $10,000 to $12,000 installed eAPU in 18 to 30 months. Incentives or factory-ordered options can shorten that to under two years.
* **Future-proofed, flexible power platform:** eAPUs integrate with 300- to 600-watt solar roof panels and shore power plugs. Lithium packs can later be repurposed or upgraded, making the system adaptable as battery prices fall [54].

#### 8.1.2.4 Challenges of Electric APUs

While there are many benefits to using eAPUs, as with any technology solution, there are challenges. These challenges don’t negate the clear fuel, emissions, and noise-reduction benefits of eAPUs. Still, they mean fleets must carefully match battery chemistry, climate profile, duty cycle, and support strategy before committing to an electric-only idle-reduction path.
* **Limited runtime in extreme heat:** Drivers in hot regions report that four-battery eAPUs fail to cool the sleeper during a full 10-hour rest. Many fleets idle or use workarounds on hot nights when temperatures are above 90°F [50].
* **Cold weather battery limitations:** At around 32°F, AGM batteries can lose up to half their capacity, reducing runtime. Lithium iron phosphate ($LiFePO_4$) batteries retain capacity better in the cold but need limited charge current to prevent damage, often requiring heating or cab mounting for winter. Most modern lithium systems have onboard thermal protection and management systems to mitigate these risks automatically [55].
* **Battery replacement and lifecycle cost:** AGM systems need battery “change-outs” every 24 to 36 months, resulting in recurring costs that reduce fuel savings. Lithium packs last longer but raise the initial purchase price [55].
* **Weight and payload impact:** A four-battery AGM setup under the bunk usually adds about 345 to 400 lbs. to the tractor, which can reduce payload capacity on weight-sensitive routes. $LiFePO_4$ systems provide the same or greater usable capacity at a significantly lower weight — often reducing the added mass by 40% to 60% — although they come with a higher initial cost [50].
* **High upfront investment:** Despite recent price drops, fleets spend $9,000 to $16,000 per tractor on lithium eAPUs. Despite the favorable payback window, OEMs and suppliers note that sticker shock is the biggest hurdle to adoption.

## 8.2 Fuel-operated Heaters

A fuel-operated heater is a small diesel-fired heater that burns fuel from the truck’s tank to produce heat without running the main engine. There are two main types:

* **Cab/Bunk Heaters** heat the air in the cab or sleeper compartment. Often mounted under the bunk or on the floor, they consume diesel to blow hot air and keep the driver warm.
* **Engine Block/Coolant Heaters** circulate and heat the engine coolant (antifreeze) to maintain the engine block's warmth. This facilitates cold starts and radiates heat into the cab through the stock heater core.

Modern fuel-operated heaters often perform both functions — heating engine coolant and maintaining an air-heating loop for the bunk. They typically run on 12V power and consume a very small amount of fuel (around 0.1 gallons per hour or less, depending on output). Directly heating the cab or coolant is much more fuel-efficient than idling the main engine, using roughly one-eighth the fuel and generating as little as one-twentieth the emissions for the same heating task. These heaters also warm the cab faster than relying on engine heat.

### 8.2.1 How the Technology Works

A fuel-operated heater is a compact metal box that generates safe, low-cost heat without running the truck’s engine. A glow plug ignites a small diesel flame in a sealed combustion chamber, a fuel pump dispenses fuel drops, while a quiet fan provides fresh air and pushes exhaust through a short tailpipe. A heat exchanger captures heat and sends it into the sleeper or coolant loop. An electronic control unit, with temperature, flame, and voltage sensors, maintains the set temperature and safely shuts down the heater if needed. The kit includes plug-and-play wiring, simple brackets, and small ducts for installation under the bunk or on the frame, allowing drivers to enjoy steady heat all night on minimal fuel. ### 8.2.3 Benefits of Fuel-operated Heaters

Here are the key benefits of installing a fuel-operated heater on a truck to limit idling and provide driver comfort.
* **Significant fuel-cost savings:** A fuel-operated heater burns about 0.03 to 0.06 gal. per hour, compared to more than 0.6 gal. for an idling engine, reducing winter idle fuel by approximately 85% to 90%.
* **Lower emissions:** Reducing idling significantly decreases $CO_2$, $NO_x$, and PM.
* **Reduced engine wear and maintenance expense:** Eliminating long idle periods extends oil life, reduces soot loading in DPFs, and generally lengthens engine life, resulting in lower maintenance costs.
* **Improved driver comfort and quieter rest breaks:** Fuel-operated heaters keep sleepers warm without engine noise or vibration, helping drivers rest better and meet hours of service requirements.
* **Regulatory and incentive advantages:** Fuel-operated heaters are an EPA SmartWay-verified idle-reduction technology that meets state anti-idle rules and makes fleets eligible for Diesel Emissions Reduction Act (DERA) grants or federal excise-tax exemptions. As of 2025, DERA is still active, although its funding levels vary by administration and year. The EPA continues to award grants through DERA, often coordinated with states or regional air quality agencies. While the Inflation Reduction Act and Clean Ports Program now get more visibility, DERA is still listed on EPA’s clean diesel program site [67].
* **Fast payback:** Published fleet case studies show that fleets recover their heater installation costs in under two years, often in just a few months, thanks to fuel and maintenance savings [68].
* **Lightweight, compact, and easy to retrofit:** Typical units weigh less than 15 lbs., mount under the bunk or on the frame, and generally install in 3 to 4 hours, minimizing downtime and preserving the frame rail space for other purposes.

### 8.2.4 Challenges of Fuel-operated Heaters

While there are many benefits to using fuel-operated heaters, as with any technology solution, there are challenges.
* **Provide heat only — no cooling or AC power:** Drivers still need a separate solution for summer air conditioning and hotel loads.
* **Draw down the starter batteries during extended rest periods:** The heater’s 12-volt fan and control unit consume a few amps. Over 10 hours, this can weaken the batteries, especially in older systems and trucks.
* **Create small but real exhaust emissions:** Although much lower than an idling engine, fuel-operated heaters still consume diesel and emit $CO_2$, $NO_x$, and PM, which may concern some sustainability-minded shippers.

## 8.3 Fuel-operated Coolant Heater

Mounted in the frame rail or under the hood, the compact unit uses an electric pump and glow-plug ignition to burn a few ounces of diesel per hour, raising coolant temperature to roughly 160°F to 180°F (70°C to 80°C) within 30 to 60 minutes. ### 8.4.1 Smart Shutdown or Auto-Start/Stop Technologies

They connect the engine control module to a network of sensors that monitor coolant temperature, battery state of charge, and sleeper cabin thermostat settings. When those values remain within preset limits, the software shuts down the engine and briefly restarts it to restore conditions before shutting down again. The system will cycle:
* When the coolant temperatures drop below approximately 60°F, the system cycles to ensure smoother engine restarts.
* When the battery falls below approximately 12.2 V, the system cycles to maintain an adequate charge for engine restarts.
* When the sleeper requires heating or cooling during driver rest periods [76].

### 8.4.2 Predictive Idle Shutdown Systems

Predictive idle shutdown takes the familiar auto-start/stop idea further by layering AI-driven analytics on top of telematics data. If the algorithm forecasts a genuine idle event, it powers down the engine pre-emptively. Then it restarts only when sensors or the duty cycle indicate that heat, cooling, or charging is needed.

Early adopters of predictive idling tools report double-digit fuel use reductions during parked periods. ### 8.4.3 Neutral Idle

Neutral idle systems automatically switch an automatic transmission to neutral when the truck stops, then re-engages as the driver accelerates. See an example in Figure 49. This reduces the torque converter load that typically affects the engine while the vehicle is stationary but still “in gear." Consequently, the engine only needs to drive its essential accessories, such as the alternator, A/C compressor, and steering pump. As a result, idle load decreases, fuel consumption decreases, and tailpipe emissions drop — all without shutting off the engine.

The transmission control unit shifts to neutral once the wheel speed reaches zero. When the driver releases the brake or presses the throttle, it re-engages drive automatically. Engine accessories remain fully operational, allowing HVAC and hydraulics to function normally. The only noticeable difference is a slight, automatic delay on takeoff — no driver action is required. Several OEMs offer this feature standard or can enable it via a software flash on modern automatic or automated manual transmissions, typically without additional hardware.

### 8.4.5 Adaptive Power Management Systems

Adaptive power management systems treat the truck’s hotel loads, batteries, alternator, and any eAPU as a single, self-optimizing micro-grid. A supervisory controller measures real-time battery state of charge, HVAC draw, accessory loads, and predicted dwell time, then decides where the next watt of energy should come from or go to. When the truck is running, the algorithm opportunistically overcharges the lithium or AGM eAPU battery pack, throttling the alternator to minimize parasitic drag. Once the truck is parked, it meters power back out, automatically switching to shore power or solar when available and shutting down the main engine unless the battery pack’s reserve reaches a user-defined threshold floor [80].

Many large fleets use automatic idle management, particularly if they have not invested in an APU. This solution is cost-effective because, in many instances, the capability is already present on the truck (just needs programming) or requires only a minor upgrade. NACFE identifies electronic engine idle parameters and automatic-start/stop controls as features with short payback periods and high confidence among fleets. This approach costs very little — it may even be standard on new trucks. Fleets operating in milder climates or having shorter rest periods particularly appreciate this option. However, some drivers object to the engine starting up during their sleep (because of noise and vibration), so fleets need to manage driver acceptance. Overall, auto-start/stop is common in newer trucks and often is combined with other strategies (like a bunk heater) for better results.

### 8.4.7 Challenges of Intelligent Engine Management Systems

While there are many benefits to using intelligent engine management systems, as with any technology solution, there are challenges. Auto-start/stop is one type of intelligent engine management system — a broader category that includes smart shutdown logic, predictive idle shutdown, neutral idle, and contextual idle management. Some of the challenges, according to the US DOE Idle Reduction Research [83]:
* **Up-front cost and integration complexity:** Fleets often struggle to justify capital costs. Implementation can be complicated because each truck must be calibrated for battery size, HVAC load, and duty cycle before savings appear.
* **Higher starter and battery duty cycles:** Automatic-start/stop systems cycle the engine more frequently during rest breaks, increasing wear on starters, alternators, and batteries. To handle the demand, OEMs often specify heavy-duty AGM or Enhanced Flooded Battery (EFB) types, along with reinforced starter motors, which increases upfront cost and maintenance complexity.
* **Driver acceptance and workarounds:** Drivers complain that the auto-shutdown activates in the cab or paperwork bay. They exchange tips on forums to override or turn off the timer, undermining expected savings and creating compliance issues for the fleet managers. Additionally, frequent engine restarts can disturb driver sleep, particularly during 34-hour resets, making comfort and operational settings an important consideration.
* **Risk of shutdowns during PTO or critical loads:** Misreading PTO status or underestimating sleeper HVAC demand can cause engine cutouts during lift gate use or standby loads, posing safety and cargo quality risks. Therefore, fleets use intelligent engine management systems with eAPUs or diesel heaters instead of auto-start/stop alone.
* **Software updates, telematics, diagnostics:** Fleets must keep trucks connected and firmware updated for over-the-air management. Faulty sensor data or outdated calibrations can lock the system, making it necessary for a fleet to visit the dealer for parameter resets, increasing downtime costs.
* **Regulatory uncertainties:** These systems aid compliance with anti-idling ordinances, but some jurisdictions fine drivers for engine cycling. Fleets should verify state rules before enabling auto-start/stop software.

### 8.4.9 Recommendations & Best Practices

Here are some considerations when deciding whether an intelligent engine management system makes sense for part or all of your fleet.
* **Start with a data-driven baseline:** Pull three to six months of telematics reports to identify “true” idle hotspots (locations, shifts, temperatures). This helps identify needs, avoid blanket settings, and target assets that will have a payback of under two years.
* **Tune electronic engine parameters first:** Before adding hardware, program shutdown timers, low-rpm limits, and start/stop rules in the ECU you must first tune the electronic engine parameters. Fleets that optimize parameters gain 5% to 8% fuel economy without extra devices.
* **Size batteries and starters for the duty cycle:** Specify heavy-duty AGM/EFB batteries or lithium eAPU packs and high-cycle starters to prevent premature failures from frequent restarts, particularly for sleeper tractors that cycle 40 to 60 times nightly.
* **Pair the intelligent engine management systems with an eAPU or thermal storage:** Automatic-start/stop is optimal during low hotel loads. Pair it with an eAPU, fuel heater, or thermal storage to restart the engine only in extreme temperatures, not just for comfort.
* **Segment control logic by context:** Use geofences, PTO signals, and trailer data to disable shutdown during liftgate or reefer operation but enforce it at distribution yards and rest areas. Use “situational idle control.”

in most outdoor temperatures.

### 8.8.1.2 Challenges of Electronic Engine Idle Parameters

While there are many benefits to setting electronic engine parameters, as with any technology solution, there
are challenges.
* Security concerns: Unauthorized parameter changes can compromise vehicle settings and, in some
cases, lock out management access, requiring complete ECM reprogramming or module replacement.
Even with password protections, technicians should not adjust settings without management approval.
* Interrelations between parameters: Altering one parameter often requires adjusting other parameters.
All parameters must be customized to the overall truck specification.
* Diversity of terminology: Each engine manufacturer uses its own vocabulary and brand names for
programmable parameters, and the functions themselves may vary between OEMs or even across
engine models from the same OEM.
* Driver acceptance: It can be difficult to gain buy-in from drivers who may feel the fleet is trying to
restrict their driving style.

### 8.8.4 Optimizing A Truck’s Cooling Capabilities

Regardless of the system, drivers can help optimize the system’s capabilities by following some general rules.
These measures can keep engine temperatures comfortable, extend component life, and trim 2% to 4% of total

following, according to TMC Recommended Practices [107].
* Schedule regular heat exchanger cleaning.
* Follow a service schedule and test pressure annually. Flush coolant per OEM intervals. Inspect hoses and
clamps every 25,000 miles. Clear debris from the radiator. Charge the air cooler with low-pressure air
and water.
* Use the proper coolant mix and pressure cap to ensure boil-over margin and prevent corrosion.

There are several other areas to explore regarding idle reduction. This section focuses on the residual value of

### 9.1 Residual Value

As the NACFE team interviewed fleets during the research process, they asked if fleets could receive any
substantial additional trade-in value for a vehicle because it was equipped with idle-reduction technologies. The
only technologies mentioned that might get extra value on the secondary market were diesel APUs or eAPUs.
The answers varied from receiving no additional trade-in value to a relatively small add-on.

Fleet managers mentioned that potential buyers of the used vehicles generally had a great deal of uncertainty
about how well the systems had been maintained, so they were unwilling to pay much of a premium for these
additional systems, given that they might need significant repairs soon after the second owner purchased the
truck.

To make purchase decisions based on payback, it is safe to assume that diesel APUs or eAPUs will have no
residual value. Diesel-fired air heaters and auto-start/stop systems will unlikely generate additional premiums at
vehicle resale.

### 9.3 Evaluating Idle-Reduction Technologies

Fleets must evaluate the ROI of all their investments, including idle-reduction technologies. Not all advantages
of idle-reduction technologies are categorized as hard costs; some are classified as soft costs and can still
motivate investment in idle-reduction technologies.

Here are some financial and other factors to consider when evaluating idle reduction technologies:
* Fuel savings
* Driver comfort (sleep quality, convenience, productivity, and safety), which impacts driver recruitment
and retention
* Reduced emissions and increased environmental sustainability
* Reduced truck and engine wear, especially to main engine accessories (fan, alternator, air compressor,
belts, HVAC system, electrical components) as a result of reduced engine hours and longer component
life resulting from lower vibration
* Depending on the frequency and cost of the fleet’s jump starts, some idle-reduction systems will reduce
jump starts, as both APUs and automatic-start/stop systems can monitor and charge batteries
* Potentially avoiding fines for violating idle regulations
* Reduced noise pollution inside and outside the cab
* Reduction of cold starts with some idle-reduction technologies

Costs of idle-reduction technologies include:
* Initial purchase price
* Installation labor costs if not factory-installed
* Additional costs for service contracts or interest if financed
* Maintenance costs
* Driver and technician training costs

The fleet’s analysis must also include an understanding of current idle percentages and the associated costs
incurred by those instances of idling to correctly assess the amount of savings they should reasonably expect
from a particular idle-reduction technology.

* Train drivers to use these tools optimally (like setting temperature parameters to reasonable levels).

This combination could achieve most idle-reduction benefits at about one-third the cost of an APU. Conversely,
a fleet that desires maximum comfort and minimum fuel usage might invest in both a diesel APU and a bunk
heater — using the heater on milder cold nights (so the APU doesn’t even need to run) and the APU for hot
weather, thereby also saving APU engine hours.

The business case for idle reduction is strong for most long-haul trucks. Even the priciest solution (diesel APU)
tends to pay for itself within the truck’s lifespan, especially with high fuel prices. For medium-duty vehicles, the
calculations might be slightly different (lower fuel usage means a longer payback), but often the motivation
there includes maintenance savings and regulatory compliance in addition to fuel.

## 10 BEST PRACTICES & RECOMMENDATIONS FOR IMPLEMENTING AN IDLE-REDUCTION STRATEGY

Fleets should take a holistic approach to deploying idle-reduction strategies. The most effective programs
usually combine operational policies, driver engagement, and technology. Below are best practices and
recommendations, from basic steps to advanced measures, and advice on combining technologies for maximum
effectiveness.

### 10.1 Baseline Best Practices

This section offers a look at some of the baseline best practices that fleets can use to reduce idle emissions.

1. **Establish an idle-reduction policy:** Communicate expectations to drivers — e.g., “No idling more than 5
minutes when parked, unless necessary for comfort or safety.” This policy should be realistic
(acknowledging that in extreme weather, some idle or APU use is needed). Still, fleet managers should
emphasize the importance of shutting down the engine whenever possible. Fleet management must
demonstrate its commitment to this policy for both cost and environmental reasons.
2. **Use engine parameters aggressively:** Program the truck’s ECM settings to enforce the policy. For
instance, set the automatic idle shutdown timer to 5 minutes (with appropriate overrides for cold/hot
conditions if the truck is equipped). Also, set any available smart idle features. Many trucks allow you to
set ambient temperature thresholds. For example, outside of 25°F to 75°F, the truck will not allow
continuous idling unless it’s for PTO use or other approved purposes [119]. These electronic controls
come at no extra cost and act as a backstop. Be sure to educate drivers on how these parameters work
so they aren’t caught off guard by the engine shutting off. They should understand that the truck is
assisting them in complying and saving fuel.
3. **Train and incentivize drivers:** Integrate idle reduction into driver training and orientation. Clarify the
reasons (fuel savings lead to a more profitable company that can pay drivers more, environmental
stewardship, etc.) and the methods (demonstrate using bunk heaters, APU controls, etc.). Many drivers
default to idling out of habit or the belief that “it’s the company’s fuel, not mine.” Offering an idle bonus
or fuel efficiency bonus can align interests. For example, share some saved fuel costs with drivers who
consistently maintain low idle time. Positive reinforcement tends to be more effective than punitive
measures. Fleets also display driver scorecards (idle percentage, MPG) to encourage competition.
Drivers are arguably the most crucial factor in managing idle time successfully.
4. **Provide alternatives for comfort during downtime:** Ensure drivers have what they need to feel
comfortable without idling. This could be as simple as encouraging them to use truck stop facilities
(lounges, rest areas) during waits instead of sitting in the truck or providing small in-cab fans, battery-powered bunk coolers, or even hotel rooms in extreme cases. A fleet might adopt a policy like “if the
heat index is above X and the truck has no APU, we will reimburse the cost of a motel room.” A motel
room for a night might be cheaper than 10 hours of high-idle, and it keeps the driver happy. While not a
technology per se, this kind of support is part of a baseline idle-reduction strategy, acknowledging that
sometimes shutting the engine off means finding comfort elsewhere.
5. **Monitor and report:** Use the available data (telematics, engine download, fuel logs) to monitor idle
time. Establish a KPI (like “idle % of engine hours” or “idle hours per driver per week”) and track it.
When you bring attention to it, it tends to improve. Identify high-idling units or shifts and investigate the
reasons for the idling. Is a particular route causing excessive waiting time? Does a specific driver require

### 10.2 Advanced Best Practices & Technology Deployments

What follows are some advanced best practices and technology deployments fleets can use as part of their idle-reduction strategy.

1. **Invest in Appropriate Idle Reduction Equipment:** Based on your fleet profile (routes, climate, operation
type), choose the technology or combination of technologies that yields the best ROI and driver
acceptance. There is no one-size-fits-all answer. A fleet operating mainly in Texas might prioritize an
eAPU for cooling. In contrast, a Canadian fleet might ensure every truck has a diesel heater and stick
with engine start/stop for A/C. Evaluate the options discussed in this report by calculating fuel costs and
idle hours to assess payback. The DOE’s Idle Reduction Savings Worksheet (from Argonne) can assist in
estimating savings for each technology [120]. Generally:
   * If your trucks have sleepers and spend many nights on the road, investing in either diesel APUs
   or eAPUs (with heaters) is almost essential for achieving significant idle reduction. Without it,
   drivers will have to idle or be uncomfortable, which isn’t sustainable. Fleets in this category
   should seriously consider factory-ordering these systems for new trucks or retrofitting select
   trucks that do the most idling.
   * If your operation is more day cab or slip-seat (no overnight idling), you might focus on auto
   shutoff and operator training rather than an APU. Additionally, consider investing in diesel-fired
   coolant heaters for trucks that must remain outside during winter (e.g., oilfield trucks waiting at
   sites). This prevents them from idling to keep the engine warm.
   * Consider hybrid PTO units or anti-idle retrofits for vocational trucks that idle to run equipment.
   Idle reduction kits are available for utility trucks (e.g., anti-idle systems that provide power for
   lights and small tools). Each vocational use case (cement mixers, dump trucks, etc.) may require
   specific solutions. For example, electrified concrete mixer barrel drives are being piloted so the
   truck doesn’t have to idle at the job site.
2. **Combine Complementary Technologies:** Often, the best approach is to combine two or more solutions
to efficiently cover all bases. See Figure 70.
   * Heater + Diesel APU: A diesel bunk heater manages cold nights while an APU (possibly with
   engine auto-start/stop backup) handles hot nights. Each system performs its best function —
   heating with fuel and cooling with electricity — making this combination popular among many
   fleets for achieving year-round comfort with minimal idling. One analysis suggests this pairing
   can reduce annual idle fuel use by 80% or more compared to having no devices [119].
   * Diesel APU + Shore Power: If you have APUs, also make trucks shore-power capable. That way,
   when parked at a home terminal or an equipped stop, the driver can use an outlet to run the
   APU’s functions (many diesel APUs have electric heating elements or shore power kits) instead
   of consuming even a small amount of diesel. Save the APU engine hours for when no outlet is
   available.
   * Diesel APU + Heater: It may sound redundant, but some fleets install an APU and an additional
   bunk heater. Why? Because the heater can operate on very little fuel on a chilly night that
   requires only heat, avoiding the approximately 0.2 gal/hour that the APU engine would
   consume. Essentially, use the heater only when heat is needed, and use the APU when A/C or
   full power is required. This practice can extend the life of the APU by avoiding its operation in
   winter (except perhaps to charge batteries).
   * Auto-start/stop + eAPU: This is a natural pairing. The auto-start/stop ensures the battery
   system never leaves the driver stranded, and it can be recharged as needed. It’s common in

3. **Maintenance and Upkeep:** Once idle-reduction equipment is installed, maintain it properly. A broken
APU will lead to the driver idling the main engine, negating the investment. The same applies to eAPUs.
If the batteries are old and won’t hold a charge, the driver has no choice but to run the engine.
Incorporate APU servicing into regular PM schedules (e.g., service the APU at every oil change interval).
For eAPUs, load test and replace batteries on schedule, perhaps every 2 to 3 years, or upgrade to
lithium-ion batteries if viable. Clean the HVAC filters in bunk units and keep engine alternators and
charging systems in top shape. Since they are key to recharging any electric idle-reduction solution, a
weak alternator or a loose belt can undermine your idle-reduction strategy. Protect your investment by
ensuring the tools provided to drivers are always functional. Fleets that succeed in lowering idling time
often foster a culture of quickly fixing any bunk heater or APU issues that drivers report.
4. **Monitor Results and Adjust:** Use data to analyze improvements in fuel consumption and idle hours. For
instance, after installing heaters and inverters, you may notice a reduction in idle fuel consumption by
X%. Calculate the financial savings and share this internally to emphasize the program’s significance. If
particular trucks continue to exhibit high idle times, investigate the cause. It could be that a specific lane
lacks access to plug-ins, leading to driver discomfort, which might warrant a different solution, such as
an APU. Keep refining your strategy. As technology progresses, remain updated on new solutions. For
example, consider upgrading some systems if a new battery chemistry provides longer cooling times.

### 10.3 Special Recommendations for Medium-Duty & Other Fleets

For non-sleeper fleets (like city delivery, utility, law enforcement), idle reduction best practices focus more on
operational changes and right-sizing.
* Encourage drivers to shut off their engines during deliveries. Many new vans do this automatically.
* Use auxiliary batteries or anti-idle devices for equipment (e.g., an electrician’s truck can have an inverter
to run tools instead of idling the van).
* Some municipalities have successfully installed automatic shutdown devices on city vehicles that turn
off the engine after a few minutes of parked idling (with manual override for emergencies). These
devices can be considered for large municipal fleets.
* Explore alternate-fueled vehicles. For example, where feasible, deploy hybrid or electric vehicles that
inherently solve idling (a BEV has no engine to idle; power draw at “idle” comes from the battery). Many
medium-duty segments (buses, delivery vans) are electrifying quickly, and one major benefit is the
elimination of fuel waste at idle.

### 10.4 Driver Comfort & Engagement

A recurring theme is ensuring any idle-reduction strategy keeps the driver comfortable. Uncomfortable drivers
may either refuse to comply with idle-reduction policies or be less productive, and there's a risk of losing them
to competitors. Therefore, when implementing idle reduction strategies, gathering driver feedback is essential.
For example, if you install an eAPU and drivers report that it doesn’t keep the space cool enough for a complete
10-hour break in the summer, consider adding a small battery-operated fan, reminding them to park in the
shade when possible, or adjusting the auto-start/stop to activate a bit sooner.

Simple measures can make a difference, such as providing windshield sunshades for drivers to use while parked
in the sun to minimize heat buildup. Some fleets incorporate into their idle-reduction policies that drivers should
park strategically, like backing the tractor so that the sun hits the trailer rather than the cab during the
afternoon, when feasible. Sharing these practical tips can demonstrate to drivers that the fleet is genuinely
trying to assist them, rather than merely trying to cut costs.

The best practice is to treat idle reduction as an ongoing program that involves specifying the right equipment,
setting smart policies, and actively managing and supporting drivers. Fleets that do so have documented idle
reductions to very low levels (single-digit percentages of engine hours in some cases). Those savings go straight
to the bottom line and also yield cleaner air.

## 11.2 Engine-Off Climate Control via Electrification

Truck manufacturers are moving toward electric-driven HVAC compressors in vehicles, partly to accommodate
hybrid and electric trucks. Traditionally, in a diesel truck, the A/C compressor is engine-driven by a belt.
However, future designs may use electrically driven compressors and pumps. Even a conventional truck could
operate its HVAC system using batteries or an external source without idling. Some aftermarket systems already
mimic this functionality, but we will see it integrated more thoroughly. Heat pump technology may be
implemented in sleeper cabs, which provides both cooling and heating electrically. This would dramatically
reduce the need for an engine or fuel heater by efficiently utilizing ambient heat. If OEMs develop trucks with
48-volt mild hybrid systems, for example, they could power heat-pump HVAC units for extended engine-off
periods.

## 11.3 Zero-Emission APUs

Research is underway into fuel cell APUs that generate electricity from hydrogen or reformate fuels, producing
only water vapor as exhaust. However, these systems are not yet commercially viable, and they typically only
make sense on trucks already powered by hydrogen fuel cells. Due to cost, infrastructure, and integration
hurdles, fuel cell APUs remain a longer-term concept rather than a near-term idle-reduction solution. The
military has experimented with diesel fuel reformer fuel cells for vehicle silent watch power. On the civilian side,
companies might develop a hydrogen fuel cell APU that trucking fleets could adopt as hydrogen refueling
stations become available (likely tied to the broader push for hydrogen trucks). This could provide long-haul
truckers with a mini power plant on board for A/C and heat, with water as the only byproduct. The timeline for
this is uncertain, but we may see pilot installations within 5 to 10 years. If hydrogen infrastructure lags, another
alternative is propane, CNG, or LNG APUs, which some companies have tried. These produce lower emissions
than diesel and might meet future zero-idle emission requirements in certain areas.

## 11.4 Integration with Electric Trucks

By 2030, a notable share of new trucks, particularly medium-duty and some heavy-duty models, will be BEVs
similar to the model shown in Figure 70. These trucks inherently eliminate engine idling, but they still need to
manage energy for HVAC while parked. We will likely see knowledge transfer. The thermal management systems
in electric trucks (which have large battery packs) might employ clever strategies like thermal storage or heat
pumps.

Additionally, electric semi-trucks could use their main battery to power cab amenities. For long-haul electric
trucks, manufacturers must ensure that about 5% to 10% of the battery is allocated for hotel loads to cover
overnight air conditioning and heating without jeopardizing driving range. The presence of mega-batteries on
board will make idle reduction straightforward (use the existing battery). It introduces the need to manage
energy so the truck can operate carefully. Fleet adoption of electric trucks will naturally solve the problem of
idling emissions when they are deployed. However, during the transition period (the next 10 years), diesel trucks
will still dominate long-haul, so conventional idle reduction remains crucial.

## 11.9 Data-Driven Optimization

In the IoT and big data age, trucks might use predictive algorithms to optimize idle reduction. For example, a
smart system could use weather forecasts and the driver’s schedule to plan energy use: “Tonight will be very
cold, so prioritize using shore power at the next stop to preserve diesel and preheat the engine” or “You have a
long break midday in summer, park in shade and activate the battery cooler earlier to avoid thermal soak.”
Telematics could even coordinate with truck stops to reserve an electrified parking spot if the system knows the
driver will need one. These are speculative, but the pieces (weather APIs, telematics, GPS) exist to make idle
reduction more intelligent and automated, further minimizing idling without human intervention.

The next 5 to 10 years should bring cleaner and more efficient idle-reduction solutions. NACFE anticipates a shift
toward electrification — whether through improved batteries in trucks, external power sources, or fuel cells —
which will reduce dependence on burning diesel for idling needs. Advanced battery or fuel cell systems might
gradually supplant traditional APUs, especially if regulators demand zero emissions. The focus also will expand
from merely saving fuel to contributing to decarbonization goals.

Idle reduction is low-hanging fruit in cutting emissions, so it will attract attention. Fleets that stay ahead of the
curve by adopting new idle-reduction innovations will save money, ensure compliance with future regulations,
and meet sustainability expectations from customers and communities.

## 12 CONCLUSIONS

Idle reduction in trucking has advanced significantly from its early days and is now a proven strategy for fleets to
reduce fuel consumption, costs, and emissions. Fleet managers can develop an idle-reduction plan that meets
their needs by understanding operational habits and available technologies.

The key takeaways are:
* There is a wide array of solutions. Use a combination that addresses heating, cooling, and power needs.
* Economic paybacks are generally favorable, especially as fuel prices rise and carbon-related costs —
such as emissions surcharges, low-emission zone fees, or regulatory penalties — become more
common.
* Driver comfort and training are pivotal. Get buy-in from drivers.
* Continued innovation will make idle reduction even easier and more effective in the near future, likely
tied to the broader electrification trend.

Eliminating unnecessary idling lowers operating cost, extends equipment life, and reduces a fleet’s
environmental impact. Fleets embracing these technologies and practices position themselves for immediate
benefits and long-term success in a more sustainable freight transportation industry.
