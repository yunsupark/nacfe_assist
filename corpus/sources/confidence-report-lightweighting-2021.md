## 5.6 Wheels and Tires

There are two areas to reduce weight in the wheels and tires, and each is seeing growth in the adoption
of lightweighting technologies. First is the aluminum wheel, which was introduced more than 40 years
ago, and is the single most effective product for saving weight on both a tractor and a trailer [8, 10].
Industry leaders estimate that 50% of the market already is using aluminum wheels. (See Figure 15.) Both
steel and aluminum wheel producers have successfully reduced the weight in recent years — 72 lbs. down
to 65 lbs. in steel [11], and 45 lbs. down to 38 lbs. — both materials cutting 7 lbs./wheel. At as much as 18
wheels per tractor trailer, this improvement cannot be overstated. [8, 10, 11]

A conversion from 22.5” steel to aluminum wheels means a reduction of 54 lbs. on the steer axle and 108
lbs. on each tandem, for a total of 270 lbs. off the tractor. The steel wheel is expected to be delivered at
64 lbs. for aftermarket and trailer applications by Q3 2021.

Aluminum wheels are a great example of a lightweight product that delivers so much more value than just
weight savings. Many OEMs and fleets, even those who are not sensitive to weight, shared that aluminum
wheels look great, are easy to maintain, and will never rust. Even if covered with aero covers, the material
you can see still looks good. A good-looking wheel translates to a happy driver, higher resale value, and
reduced maintenance. A large dry van fleet specs aluminum wheels on the outer tandems, with steel
inside, clearly taking advantage of the appearance of aluminum.

The second option is to switch to wide-base tires, which can eliminate one entire wheel and tire assembly
per wheel-end — four wheels and four tires per tractor. This conversion delivers nearly 1,300 lbs. weight

The reduction in tires and material from wide-base tires also equates to a simpler operation. One tire
instead of two means fewer tires to inventory. It also can make casing management and purchasing easier.
Servicing one tire instead of two is much easier and could reduce labor costs. The drawback of wide-base
tires is that, with just one tire on the wheel-end, the truck could very well be out of service if the tire is
damaged. A service call and the resulting delay in operations are serious concerns, and a contributing
factor to the reluctance to embrace wide-base tires as the standard. Wide-base wheels are available in
both steel and aluminum; most fleets run with aluminum wheels to maximize the weight savings. A recent
NACFE Confidence Report on low rolling resistance tires outlines the benefits and challenges of taking
advantage of wide-base tires. [50]

Behind the wheels are the mud flaps, and there is weight reduction here too. Solid rubber flaps weigh 40
lbs. per pair whereas a conversion to solid plastic is half that at 20 lbs. per pair. Going a step further,
vented plastic mud flaps are four times lighter at just 5 lbs. per pair [11].

## 5.7 Frame and Chassis

The frame is the backbone of the vehicle. It’s the primary load-carrying structure between the wheels and
the cab and the trailer. Frame design is considered a core competency of OEM engineers. There are many
attachment points, and the integrity of the frame has a large effect on vehicle dynamics. Critical features

---

<!-- page: 41 -->

of frame design include stiffness, strength, and fatigue strength as the frame is subject to twisting and
shock loads from one end to the other.

The baseline Class 8 frame is a steel construction, usually using steel with a strength of 120 ksi (827 Mpa).
Any changes to the frame — whether they involve materials, wall thickness, structural shape, or
modifications to crossmembers — result in a change to stiffness and the way the frame resonates to
vibration. Design changes are first simulated under static loads, which are well understood. The dynamic
simulation, however, has many components with many attachment points, some bolted and some
compliant. The sheer number of variables leads to unreliable test results according to a leading frame
provider. As a truck OEM looks at improvements to the frame design, the engineering team will test the
new frame in a laboratory, after the computer simulations have suggested acceptable results.

The machine for testing in a laboratory is a large frame chassis dynamometer, or “dyno,” and is capable
of testing the whole chassis and its subsystems that include the frame and suspension system, even down
to individual components. The dyno is programmed using real-world data from vehicle testing in the field.
After success on the dyno, which is often an iterative process of test, redesign, and test again, the OEM
will assemble a complete truck for testing on a private course, and eventually for testing on the highway.

While there are many frame section configurations, from C-channels to box sections to flat plate, the real
lightweighting innovation in frame design for heavy-duty trucks will be had through material conversion.
There are a number of innovations in the frame. One is the use of high strength steel, from 120 to 160ksi
(1,100 Mpa), allowing for gauge thickness reduction and a weight savings of 200 lbs. on the tractor [12].
See Section 11 for more innovations coming in frame and crossmember design.

Lightweight aluminum crossmembers are growing in popularity, and a number of OEMs and fleets
mentioned them in the recent survey. From the front to the rear of the frame, there are as many as nine
cross supports, though not all are candidates for reduced weight materials and designs. At one OEM, an
intermediate member saves 13 lbs., a forward suspension member saves 37 lbs., a bogie member saves
34 lbs., and a rear crossmember saves 21 lbs. All told that equals 105 lbs. of savings, though some fleets
and OEMs quoted more than 150 lbs. savings potential with lightweight crossmembers. The OEM team
will review a specification of aluminum crossmembers and will make a judgment call whether or not the
reduced strength member is suitable in a specific frame location. Concerns vary from twisting to fatigue
to stiffness.

In the recent study, more specific information was gathered relative to roll forming and casting. Novelis
has collaborated with Daimler Trucks North America to produce a package of aluminum alloy
crossmembers [13]. These are a replacement for existing steel components and promote weight
reduction. This new generation of high-strength, high-formability aluminum alloys allow for stamping to
take place within the existing tooling, which was originally designed for steel parts. Design will vary based
on location of the crossmember. (See Figure 17.)

The cast A356-T6 crossmember was an improvement over a fabricated extruded aluminum member, in
both cost and weight savings [14].

## 5.8 Cab

There are multiple aspects to consider when lightweighting a cab. Size is chief among them, as day cabs
weigh from 700 to 1000 lbs. less than tractors with short sleepers, and short sleepers weigh 500 to 600
lbs. less than larger ones. One OEM said it got great reviews on a 52” sleeper on a tractor weighing around
15,000 lbs.

Another 100 to 200 lbs. in weight savings are attainable from the cab by spec’ing mid- or flat-roof sleepers
compared to a hi-rise sleeper. All of these alternatives offer upfront purchase price savings along with
lower weights, but driver satisfaction and residual values need to be considered when “decontenting” a
typical long-haul sleeper tractor.

A large for-hire carrier said they’ve “bought some smaller sleepers, about a foot to 18” shorter. That saves
weight not only in the cab but the chassis. Wheelbase is shorter as well.” But another weight-sensitive
fleet from Category 1 was quick to say they do not use shorter sleepers to save weight. After all, the truck
is the driver’s “home away from home,” and a comfortable sleeper is key to retaining good drivers. One
OEM’s “premier interior package” adds 153 lbs., though in some cases the passenger seat can be removed
to save 35 lbs. Many fleets, however, also cautioned that the more space the driver is provided, the more
stuff they bring along with them, and stuff adds weight.

At one OEM, 60% of the cab’s content is aluminum construction, while others work to make their all-steel
cabs as light as possible. With the many innovations in steel, there is significant potential to reduce weight
while maintaining a common supply chain. Plastic also is finding its niche on the Class 8 truck, largely in
nonstructural parts like bumper fascia’s, fairings, and aerodynamic covers. One such plastic, called
dicyclopentadiene (DCPD) resin, is produced by Romeo Rim and has a very low density of just 1.03 g/cc,
and can be used to produce parts as large as 11.15 square meters and 300 mm depth [15].

---

<!-- page: 43 -->

On many cabs the grilles are injection molded plastic with chrome plating, and roof fairings are injection
molded plastic. A plastic roof fairing is about 25 lbs. lighter than sheet-molded composite (SMC) [16].

In addition to being lightweight, plastics are durable and produce a beautiful A-Surface. An injection
molded hood is coming in a couple of years and will offer a 30% reduction in weight. Aluminum is both
lightweight and structural yet can be significantly more expensive than steel. In the light-duty pickup truck
market today, 43% of the hoods are aluminum and none are plastic, so perhaps those successes will carry
over to the heavy-duty truck market. But on the OEM side, material conversion can come with a cost that
may be too high to justify, given the low volumes of the Class 8 truck market. A large injection mold tool
for making those roof fairings can cost over $1 million, and an aluminum body panel-stamping tool can be
$1.5 million.

Out in front, the metal grilles have been replaced by chrome plated molded plastic and the steel bumpers
can be replaced by a clad structure with thicker aluminum inside, and thinner stainless steel on the
outside, delivering 112 lbs. of weight reduction.

## 5.9 5th Wheels

The 5th wheel is available in multiple configurations from steel to aluminum top plates and fixed to various
levels of sliding adjustment. The study team’s interviews frequently noted that the fixed 5th wheel is 50
to 150 lbs. lower in weight than a sliding adjustable wheel, yet the slider would bring better resale value,
so it’s common to spec the heavier and more expensive slider.

Most tandem axles are rated to carry a maximum of 40,000 lbs., so the 5th wheel should be specified to
a similar amount. One 5th wheel is rated at 50,000 lbs. and weighs 202 lbs., but the best seller today is
rated to 55,000 lbs. and weighs 226 lbs. Over-spec’ing the 5th wheel load capacity has weight and cost
implications. Presumably, the reason for the over-spec’ing is because the vehicle manufacturer has no
control over the weight of the trailer that will be attached in the field. One OEM shared this as the reason
for over-spec’ing on the tandem axle rating as well.

The 5th wheel mounting configuration is another consideration. Inboard mounting can save 100 lbs. vs.
outboard mounting. Aluminum components can save weight with aluminum top plates savings around
100 lbs. and aluminum angle mounts an additional 35 lbs.

The most prominent example of lightweighting the 5th wheel is the forged aluminum top plate that
delivers 100 lbs. of weight savings [17]. For a fuel hauler, this 100 lbs. is equivalent to 15.6 gallons of fuel,
and that adds up. (See Figure 18.) Aluminum 5th wheels may not be the best choice for applications that
are hooked and unhooked often, as it’s common for the kingpin to hit off-center, causing damage to the
throat or the ramps. As we travel the highways, we see aluminum fifth wheels on the tanker trucks, yet
because they are so expensive compared to stamped and cast steel models [18], the overall take rate is
very low.

## 6 Electric Vehicles and Weight

NACFE interviews with fleets, OEMs and the supply base in the Class 8 market focused on three
segments: dry van, refrigerated and bulk haulers. Because the trucking industry is a large contributor to
CO~2~ emissions, cleaner powertrains are in development that will use electricity or hydrogen to propel
the vehicle. During the interviews, the NACFE team had a number of questions centered on these
alternative powertrains ranging from expectations of weight, plans to work with electric or hydrogen
powertrains, vehicle integration challenges around packaging, weight, and chassis engineering.
Summarizing the comments, most respondents believe that battery electric vehicles (BEV) would be
heavier than diesels, and that an additional 2,000 lbs. allowed by state regulators would not be
sufficient to cover the increase in the weight of the electric powertrain. NACFE suggests that most fleets
are running closer to 67,000 lbs., and could consider BEV’s; but the most weight-sensitive fleets would
not be likely candidates for BEVs unless other forces, such as legislation, drive it. There are proactive

---

<!-- page: 46 -->

beverage haulers running short distances, i.e. “last mile,” at around 60,000 lbs. and there is a business
case to electrify this segment of the fleet market.

Of the tractor OEMs interviewed, the electric tractor weights were 2,500 to 5,000 lbs. heavier than the
comparable diesels. The concern about weight is not so much about allowable road limits, rather it is
inefficient and expensive to carry thousands of extra pounds. When asked if 200 lbs. could be taken off
the vehicle, would they add more batteries (if it was viable), or keep the weight off, fleets said they would
add more batteries to increase the range. Many fleets question the battery range estimates for their duty
cycles, through traffic jams, and in hot or cold weather; they will not really know until they procure and
test vehicles in-house.

Also, when the study team asked OEMs if they select the lightweighting options; the answer was, “yes, all
of them.” When asked if this is affordable, the response is that the battery pack dominates the additional
weight and vehicle cost, and lightweighting is low cost in comparison. At current estimates, electric vehicle
cost today is twice that of diesel tractors for the same duty cycle.

When considering purchasing electric trucks, fleets mentioned that sustainability is important, but the
new trucks must not overly hinder the ability of the trucks to perform. Items such as weight and range
cannot be too much different from today. This is a call to those working on new powertrains — know your
target customer, their duty cycle, and operations. The concern of short range in BEVs led one large dry
van fleet representative to say that they absolutely do not want to hire more drivers and buy more
equipment to do the same task as they do today. Short-range BEVs will be dedicated to short-range
operations with long overnight charging until the technology proves the range and durability to extend to
more duty cycles.

In the automotive electric vehicle (EV) market, there is extensive crash testing on the vehicle with the
battery pack in place. One such test is the Euro NCAP side pole in which the vehicle is moving at 32 km/h
and slams into a stationary pole, right into the driver’s door [19]. The assessed damage on an EV is looking
for battery damage because an electrical short in the battery connection can lead to a potential fire. The
EV battery boxes are integrated within the vehicle body structure and are protected with materials like
aluminum extrusions or roll-formed sheet metal. According to the Aluminum Extruders Council, the
weight of the battery pack, in automotive applications, is approximately 27% of the weight of the total
battery pack [20].

In a webinar by A2MAC1, a benchmarking firm, across nine electric vehicles, the distance between the
outer-most sheet metal and the nearest battery cell is an average of 250 mm (9.8”) [21]. The prototype
Class 8 BEVs on display at the 2019 ACT Expo, with battery packs hanging over the frame rails like saddle
bags, had much less of a gap between the sheet metal cover and the packs. Granted, the market is early
in design and testing, yet the concern is that there is no known standard on battery pack durability testing,
such as a vehicle impact test, nor is there a standard on a way to protect the pack. One OEM attributed
1,000 lbs. of a BEV’s weight to the cage surrounding the saddle-mounted battery packs. Ideally the
batteries would be packaged within the frame rails and protected from passing vehicles, without
additional structure and weight, yet that does not seem feasible for today’s duty cycles and the required
volume of batteries available.

---

<!-- page: 47 -->

Some industry stakeholders asked if there was room to store tractor batteries under the trailer. There is
likely room, though many questioned the validity of such a solution from a fleet perspective knowing that
trailers far outnumber tractors, and that trailer drops are common. This system would require a dedicated
tractor-trailer combination.

There are efforts to add electric energy generation devices to the trailer, two of which are most
noteworthy — a solar unit on the roof and an e-hub. The solar roof product is intended to capture energy
and store it in a battery to run the lift gate or assist in the energy to run the refrigeration unit [22]. The
unit weight is 250 lbs. not including the battery pack, which would be sized to its application.

Another trailer energy generation device in development is mounted to the axle, as an electric hub,
generically designated as an e-hub [14]. The intent is to capture energy when driving and to capture some
regenerative braking energy to assist in slowing down the trailer; the energy is stored in a battery and is
intended to be used to energize the compressor on a reefer. The e-hub system eliminates a number of
heavy items in the reefer system: diesel fuel, fuel tank and brackets, generator, and liftgate batteries and
mounts. This leads to a total weight savings of 1,700-1,910 lbs. according to the manufacturer. The system
weight comes back on, with a net savings of 500 lbs. not including the battery pack, which would be sized
to the refrigeration requirements and the trip time. There may be additional weight to harden the battery
pack, like that mentioned above on tractors, and it is not known if there is a crash safety specification for
mounted battery packs on trailers.

Electrification in the Class 8 market is the early stages, where there are more questions than answers and
designs are not field-tested. Once fleets are ordering hundreds of BEVs, then OEMs and the supply chain
will begin to deliver efficiencies in products and systems. Then, we can expect the weight to come down
along with the development of standards for battery protection.

## 6.1 Class 8 CBEV Battery Weight

It is assumed that an electric Class 8 tractor will be pulling the same trailers as the diesel units. Removing
all the diesel-related systems from the Class 8 tractor could reduce vehicle weight by more than 7,000 lbs.
Cummins AEOS prototype was able to offset the added weight of the electric drivetrain (batteries, motors
and mountings) to achieve parity for a 100-mile range Class 7 tractor [44]. Consider an example 500-mile
range Class 8 tractor using wheel-end motors as prototyped by both Mercedes Benz and Tesla. To achieve
the additional 400 miles, battery capacity must be added, assuming space on the chassis can be found. All
other systems remain largely unchanged. This is similar to adding diesel fuel tank capacity to extend range
on a tractor; all other truck systems remain largely unchanged.

Regarding battery performance units, some clarification is needed. Power density is a scientific term used
to describe battery electrical power per unit of mass or volume. Units are typically kilowatt hour per
kilogram or kilowatt hour per liter (kWh/kg or kWh/l). The fleet focus is typically on mass, not volume of
batteries. Specific power is the inverse of that value — mass divided by power — typically expressed as
kg/kWh. In the US, colloquially, it’s common to see batteries described in units of lb/kWh. This technically
mixes measurement systems, something that your science teacher would fault you for. It is US weight (a
unit of force not mass) per SI system power and expresses a weight-to-power value. In the US, trucks carry
pounds of freight weight. Furthermore, vehicle GVWR, the gross vehicle weight rating, is expressed in
pounds of weight. Battery electrical power in kWh per pound of battery weight expresses a power-to-

---

<!-- page: 48 -->

weight ratio for comparison purposes. Expressing the inverse of that, weight-to-power, emphasizes that
battery weight is important in terms of freight capacity, so is a common simplification used in this report.

The Department of Energy’s (DOE) funded research into battery improvements is reported on annually as
documented in annual progress reports for energy storage and advanced batteries. The DOE states in its
2016 progress report, “Current battery technology performs far below its theoretical limits.” For example,
in the near term, even with existing lithium-ion technology, there is an opportunity to more than double
the battery pack energy density (from 125 Wh/kg to 250 Wh/kg) by using new high-capacity cathode
materials, higher voltage electrolytes, and high-capacity silicon or tin-based intermetallic alloys to replace
graphite anodes.

Battery weight required for a specific duty cycle is then a moving target for the future. Working through
an example, assume battery efficiency of 2 kWh/mi; then adding 400 miles range requires an estimated

## 7 Major Lightweighting Options for Trailers

The study researched most of the lightweight optional components of trailers across the three categories
of bulk haulers, refrigerated, and dry vans. Bulk tanks are available in various metals from steel, stainless
steel to aluminum. The study also addressed some of the components, namely the “running gear” of bulk
trailers, but mostly focused its efforts on 53’ dry and refrigerated trailers.

Nearly all interviewees mentioned that the trailer market is extremely cost sensitive. Illustrative of this,
one fleet reported that it runs 100% of its tractors with aluminum wheels but doesn’t have any aluminum
wheels on its trailers.

Durability is also imperative, as trailers are generally kept for longer periods of time than tractors. A
truckload reefer might keep trailers for four to seven years, a dry van fleet from six to 10 years, and private
fleets, such as food service and grocery, for 15 years or more. The long life combined with the minimal
appetite for weight reduction poses quite a challenge for new product developers to recoup the research
and development costs for lightweighting options for trailers.

The typical trailer tare weight is 15,000 lbs., yet it’s not uncommon to see dry van specs as low as a little
over 13,000 lbs., with beverage hauling requirements in the 12,500 lb. range. Two fleets in the study had
whittled this down further, to 12,180 lbs. and 11,800 lbs. Challenging the study team’s hypothesis that
one current trend in the industry is that both tractor and trailer weights are increasing, a leading trailer
manufacturer said that dry van trailers have lost 400 to 500 lbs. in tare weight in the past five to 10 years.

To reduce trailer weight, we look at the major systems — box/tank, floor, frame, suspension and wheel-
ends. The biggest opportunity may be in addressing the running gear, as lightweight brakes, hubs, and
suspensions, can deliver 600 to 700 lbs. of savings.[24].

## 7.1 Flooring and Structure

About 95% of the flooring material in a dry van trailer is laminated hardwood, with the remaining 5%
being aluminum or some form of composite. 80% of that hardwood is oak, and the rest is maple. Wood
as a material is like iron for brake drums, low cost and durable, yet also heavy, plus it absorbs moisture.
Moisture presents two problems: one is mold, and the other is that, over time, a refrigerated trailer can
become 1,000 to 2,000 lbs. heavier as the flooring and the foam insulation absorb moisture. In a 53’ trailer,
the flooring material is typically 8’ wide by 47’6” long. Steel plate is often used on the ends to provide
wear protection. The flooring is supported underneath, typically on 12” spans, so there is deflection
between the spans. Over time, fatigue will degrade the floor and the fasteners. Underneath the trailer
also faces exposure to road salts, water, and dry air.

---

<!-- page: 51 -->

Creating a laminate of wood and a resin fiber composite can reduce the weight by 360 lbs., or can be
weight neutral and increase the floor rating above 20,000 lbs. Either way the cost of the usually $2,000
wood floor would increase by about $500.

Aluminum can save 375 lbs., yet that floor has a lower rating at 15,000 to 16,000 lbs., and costs about
$1,000 more than hardwood. In refrigerated trailers, aluminum is common because it does not get heavier
with moisture, nor is there a risk of mold, and because an extruded aluminum profile provides a volume
underneath for coolant.

Aluminum crossmembers under the trailer floor are lighter by about 20 lbs. each compared to steel, with
some options delivering even more weight savings at 30 lbs. each. Using aluminum crossmembers can
adversely affect both the floor durability and the weight rating, yet applications may use some of each.
It’s common to utilize steel where it’s needed most and take advantage of low-density aluminum
elsewhere.

Lightweight van trailers may have aluminum roof bows and aluminum posts. Some refrigerated trailers
come with thinner roof sheets but that comes with concern about limb damage. Reinforcement on the
corners can be selected to protect from damage; long-haul fleets will be able to avoid this additional
weight. Aluminum extrusions are common in trailer construction, though a number of interviewees
mentioned roll-formed sheet materials because they bring more alloy options, and thinner gauges that
cut weight and are easier to assemble. Speaking of assembly, structural adhesives are gaining in popularity
for a number of reasons. The application can be robotically applied, they are leak-proof with a continuous
bead; and the outer skin lies flat and free of rivet heads, which are better in aerodynamics and aesthetics.

Fleets also can select aluminum swing doors, again saving weight but sacrificing durability. The structure
surrounding the rear doors is a good example of where high strength steel can be used to prevent dock
impact damage. If carbon steel is chosen, this can be done without a considerable weight penalty.

There is reluctance among trailer OEMs to take weight out of the coupler, the undercarriage, the rear
frame, and the landing gear, which are currently made of steel. One landing gear manufacturer offers two
lighter weight options — high strength steel with a reduction of 25 lbs., and aluminum with a reduction
of 50 lbs. However, one fleet reviewer said that it’s common for the driver to forget to lift the landing
gear, and when dragged around, it gets bent. He didn’t think an aluminum gear would be suitable.

The study team also found options for 60 lbs. of reduction from lighter weight rear doors, and of another
100 lbs. from converting steel roof bows to aluminum. Aluminum sidewall posts in a dry van can save 270
lbs. compared to steel for a mere $100, or 37 cents per pound, well below any of the various market
segment’s cost/pound requirements. Specifications for trailer sidewalls are stiffness-driven, and today
they often are made of a polymer inner with steel skins.

There is a great deal of customization available in trailer construction, some of which are listed on
manufacturers’ websites. The trailer OEMs have very experienced and capable engineering teams, so
fleets ought to engage with them to specify the trailer to meet their requirements [25, 26, 27, 28].

## 7.3 Tires, Wheels, Brakes, and Axles

Like the tractor, there is considerable weight reduction opportunity in converting steel wheels to
aluminum — roughly 27 lbs. per wheel. The lightest steel wheel is 65 lbs., soon to be 64 lbs., while the
lightest aluminum wheel is 38 lbs. In 2020, we saw increased usage of lightweight aluminum wheels in
weight-sensitive trailers as a major suspension producer reported 29% of its sales are aluminum wheels.
Overall trailer application of aluminum wheels is under 10%. Both steel and aluminum wheel producers
have successfully reduced the weight from 72 lbs. to 65 lbs. for steel wheels, and 45 lbs. down to 38 lbs.
for aluminum wheels — both materials cutting 7 lbs./wheel in recent years.

---

<!-- page: 53 -->

Also, on trailers, dual tires can be converted to wide-base tires and wheels, saving 268 lbs. per trailer using
steel wheels or 536 lbs. using aluminum [29, 43]. Certainly, the improvement in rolling resistance and
weight reduction of wide-base tires is well understood, however, many fleets deem them impractical
because they wear more quickly than duals and are not as conducive to retreading. One weight-sensitive
fleet that does use wide-base tires on most tractors does not use them on trailers because of the damage
that tends to occur at a much higher rate on trailers than on tractors. This is discussed more in NACFE’s
Low Rolling Resistance Tires Confidence Report. [50]

Trailer brakes are either drums or discs — often referred to as air disc brakes, or ADBs. The market is
gradually switching from drums to discs for a variety of reasons, primarily ease of maintenance, better
performance, and the ability to integrate safety technologies. Suspension manufacturers reported that
20-25% of the trailer brakes are ADBs, growing at a rate of 1% to 2% per year. Reviewing a suspension
manufacturer’s list of wheel-end options by drum and ADB brands, hub type (ductile iron, ADI or
aluminum), there are instances where drums are lighter and where discs are lighter. A weight advantage
depends on other variables, not just drum vs. disc. In one example, a single piston caliper cuts 50 lbs. per
trailer axle from a 16.5 x 7 brake drum using a 415 mm diameter disc and a ductile iron hub [6]. Another
20 lbs. can be cut if the hub was an ADI hub. The standard trailer disc is 430 mm diameter. [30]

The common 16.5 x 7 brake drum is 108 lbs., a lightweight one-piece iron drum is around 100 lbs., and a
steel shell drum is 90 lbs. This is reference to the thermal mass that manages the heat created by friction.
There are multiple methods to manage this heat, by absorption with a large mass, or by thermal
conductivity with material innovations, which can also deliver weight reduction.

The majority of the trailer hubs are ADI because they deliver the best combination of weight and cost [30].
In comparison to ductile iron, ADI hubs reduce axle weight by 20 lbs. for N-type, and 33 lbs. for P-type.
ADI is even lighter than aluminum, reducing axle weight by 2 lbs. for N-type and 6 lbs. for P-type. [30]

An air disc brake with an aluminum hub reduces trailer weight by 125 lbs. yet comes at a cost premium.
Many interviewees told the study team that disc brakes reduce maintenance (which is becoming a larger
issue because there is a technician shortage), longer preventive maintenance intervals, and the general
feeling that systems should be maintenance-free.

One axle manufacturer increased the diameter of a trailer axle from the industry standard five inches to
six inches, yet because this also means an increase in section stiffness, the design is 36 lbs. lighter than
the standard trailer axle [7].

## 9.1 EPA and NHTSA

On October 25, 2016, the EPA and National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) jointly
published Phase 2 of the Greenhouse Gas (GHG) Emissions and Fuel Efficiency Standards for
Medium- and Heavy-Duty Engines and Vehicles [32]. The Phase 2 rule set emission standards for
tractor-trailers, vocational vehicles, and heavy-duty pickup trucks and vans. The rule expanded
on the Phase 1 standards (published on September 15, 2011, for model years 2014 through 2018)
and introduced first-ever controls on trailers and glider vehicles. As of the fall of 2020, the trailer
portion of this rule remains in limbo. A stay on the implementation of the GHG Phase 2 rules for
trailers was issued by the U.S. Court of Appeals. This stay may have the effect of suspending the
trailer rules indefinitely.

The standards phase-in is between MY 2021 and MY 2027 for engines and vehicles and between
MY 2018 and MY 2027 for trailers and gliders. The agencies outline several benefits of the rule,
including reducing carbon dioxide (CO~2~) emissions and fuel consumption from new on-road
vehicles, reducing the costs for transporting goods, and spurring innovation in the clean energy
technology sector.

The Phase 2 rule maintains the underlying regulatory structure developed in Phase 1, however,
unlike Phase 1, the rule puts forth “technology advancing standards” (i.e., standards based “not
only on currently available technologies but also on utilization of technologies now under
development or not yet widely deployed”). These may include advancements in the engine,
transmission, driveline, aerodynamic design, lower rolling resistance tires, and extended idle
reduction technologies.[32]

The agencies estimate that the Phase 2 standards will achieve vehicle fuel savings of up to 25%
beyond Phase 1 when fully implemented and depending on the vehicle category. (See Figure 28.)
Overall, the agencies estimate it could cut GHG emissions by 1.1 billion metric tons of CO~2~ and
conserve 2 billion barrels of oil over the lifetime of the vehicles sold in the regulatory time frame.

Although the regulations apply only to original equipment manufacturers as they earn credits toward their
overall compliance goals by including weight saving options in vehicle specifications, truck buyers still have
the option of specifying non-weight saving options. But truck purchasers recognize the value of weight
saving options and they become part of the specifying conversation.

Trucks and trailers sold under the GHG rule are required to have a compliance label indicating which, if
any, fuel-saving technologies were installed on the truck when it was delivered. Those devices or
technologies must remain on the truck for its “useful life,” deemed to be 435,000 miles for Class 8 vehicles,
185,000 miles for Class 6 and 7, 105,000 miles for Class 3 to 5 and 10 years for trailers. These requirements
would prevent a user from switching to non-weight saving options sometime after the truck or trailer was
put into service if it were originally equipped with weight saving options. Truck and trailer dealers are not
considered manufacturers and therefore would be required to replace new vehicle weight saving options
with the same weight saving options even if the changeover occurs prior to the delivery to the end-user
(customer). From NACFE’s perspective, most weight savings devices, like crossmembers or hubs or plastic
aero covers are integrated and are not practical to remove.

The Truck Trailer Manufacturers Association (TTMA) filed suit against the EPA and NHTSA in December
2016 because trailers are included in GHG Phase 2 rules [33]. TTMA argued that trailers do not have
engines and therefore should not be regulated as “motor vehicles” as defined by the Clean Air Act. The
EPA, NHTSA and California Air Resources Board (CARB) did not oppose lifting the long-standing temporary
suspension which means trailers are not required to meet the regulations, however, the industry
recognizes the value of weight saving options and other components of GHG Phase 2 like aerodynamics.

CARB also adopted GHG Phase 2 trailer standards identical to the EPA GHG trailer standards except that
California standards were slated to start with the 2020 model year [34]. In December 2019, CARB

announced that it would “suspend its enforcement” of the California GHG trailer standards for a period
of at least two calendar years (2020 and 2021). California cited the uncertainty around the litigation and
the imminence of the January 2020 effective date as reasons for the suspension. California will continue
to administer the California GHG trailer regulation on a voluntary basis.

## 10 Benefits and Enablers of Lightweighting

This section summarizes the benefits available to fleets and the industry as a whole from pursuing the
adoption of lightweighting technologies, and moreover it considers certain enabling factors which are
likely to reinforce or further incentivize the lightweighting trend. There is a certain “feedback loop” in
most of these, with the enablers of lightweighting directly creating the benefits that fleets will enjoy from
pursuing it.

## 10.1 Regulations

A variety of government regulations impact the weights of vehicles, and in recent years the regulatory
environment has incentivized lightweighting. Investing in lightweighting technologies therefore offers
fleets the benefit of meeting regulations.

The gross weight limit on US interstates is 80,000 lbs. This is enforced at weigh stations, and the penalties
include fines, removal of goods that exceed the allowable weight, and now with CSA, (the FMCSA
Compliance Safety Accountability program), records of violations for the fleet and the driver.

Along with limiting the total weight of a truck, government regulations on trucking often have driven the
weight of the vehicles themselves upward. One OEM engineer was quoted as saying, “Every time the
government opens its mouth, our truck gets heavier.” One recent example of such a regulation was for
reduced stopping distance (RSD), which required trucks to be able to stop in 250’, down from the previous
355’. Implemented in 2011, this was viewed by the industry as the most extreme brake-related law passed
in 30 years. To meet the requirement, OEMs converted 7” wide brake drums to 8.625” wide drums, which
also meant introducing a wider brake shoe and liner. The resulting weight increase was approximately 20
lbs. per wheel-end, or 120 lbs. per tractor. RSD also increased the structural load on the front axle,
requiring more than a brake torque change; more systems had to be upsized to return to the prior
maintenance and product life.

Another major weight-related regulation was the diesel emission equipment systems — diesel particulate
filters (DPF) added in 2007 and diesel exhaust fluid (DEF) in 2010 added about 600 lbs. to a truck’s
engine/exhaust system. A third example is the nation’s various idle-reduction regulations, which prevent
the primary engine from running overnight for heating or cooling the cabin. In lieu of the engine, trucks
were equipped with a battery or diesel auxiliary power unit (APU) to meet these needs, and those systems
add 360 to 570 lbs. to the tractor. Granted, for these idle reduction technologies, a federal exemption
offset of 400 lbs. is available. (See Figure 29.)

The EPA recognized the opportunity offered by lightweighting when it implemented Phase 1 of the GHG

## 10.2 Increased Freight Efficiency

Weight might hardly matter at all (within the realm of physics) if not for the aforementioned regulations
mandating a maximum gross vehicle weight for trucks, so the primary incentive for reducing the tare
weight of a tractor and/or trailer is to be able to haul additional payload without going over the weight
limit. As highlighted throughout this report, this means that lightweighting is a goal centered around
freight efficiency much more than it is about fuel efficiency. Trucking is not like the light-duty vehicle
market, where a few hundred pounds converts to measurable fuel economy. Specifically, for every 10%
reduction in the weight of a passenger car, there is a 5 to 7% fuel savings. But given economies of scale,
Class 8 trucks are unable to reduce much more than a few thousand pounds, which translates to a much
smaller percentage of fuel savings.

As payloads increase with the trends outlined in Section 2 of this report, lightweighting becomes more
important. For the typical bulk hauler, freight efficiency most often translates directly into more product
being hauled in each trailer (x dollars per pound = increased revenue per load). Potential benefits to the
fleet include earning a given shipper’s business by being able to haul heavier loads per trip, thus reducing
the shipper’s cost and carbon footprint. One fleet shared that “lightweighting is revenue…I only hire
owner-operators with tractors that weigh less than 16,000 lbs.”

---

<!-- page: 62 -->

Note that freight efficiency may not directly increase revenue for the for-hire fleet operator, as most
freight rates are quoted by the lane (geographical area of operation) and by the trailer load, not
specifically by the weight of freight being hauled.

Increased freight efficiency has other secondary benefits, for example, by hauling more freight in each
power unit (tractor), and thereby reducing the total number of vehicles required to move a shipment, the
issues of the driver shortage and congestion are mitigated — two of the largest issues facing trucking
today. One interviewee stated, “If we can get 10% more payload, that’s 10% fewer trips, helping with the
driver shortage and infrastructure congestion.”

## 10.3 Improved Fuel Efficiency

Lightweighting will, in fact, positively impact the tractor’s base fuel economy. Lowering overall vehicle
weight decreases the rolling resistance of a given load on the tires. And no matter what, it takes energy
to move mass, so less mass means less energy. However, the per-truck fuel economy benefit of
lightweighting is quite low — just 0.5% to 0.6% improvement per 1,000 lbs. of weight reduction. “Mild”
investment in lightweighting, as defined in Section 6, which save 2,000 lbs., would offer a nice 1% or more
fuel savings when the truck is driving empty or lightly loaded. Those fuel savings would likely not, however,
recoup the upfront cost of the necessary lightweighting technologies within an acceptable payback
period.

## 10.4 Sustainability Goals

Companies that are aggressive with lightweighting, whether they are fleets, truck and trailer builders or
component manufacturers, may enjoy a brand or marketing advantage from their efforts. Many of the
interviewees mentioned this during the team’s data collection. One fleet said, “Lightweighting sometimes
earns you actual revenue increases via business you wouldn’t have received, but also just positions you
better with the shippers.” For the Category 1 and Category 2 routes that are grossing out for a portion of
their time, lightweighting, which allows them to run one less truck to move a given quantity of freight, will
represent a major gain in sustainability. Aggressive lightweighting which saves 4,500 lbs. off a standard
vehicle tare weight will allow just 10 trucks to move the freight that would otherwise require 11 trucks,
while mild lightweighting, which saves 2,000 lbs., could keep every 24th truck off of the road.

A single truck, at current industry averages of just under 6 MPG and 100,000 miles driven per year, is
responsible for CO~2~ emissions of nearly 380,000 lbs. annually. Additionally, keeping one truck off the road
reduces congestion, and saves the fleet a great deal of money in operating costs while still allowing them
to do the same amount of work for their shippers.

## 10.5 Driver Retention

Drivers often are paid per mile and are on the road for long periods of time, so naturally they want large
and spacious cabins with powerful engines. “Condos” and “high rises,” are the large sleeper cabs that
attract drivers and are their “home away from home.” The need to attract and retain drivers has resulted
in longer and taller sleeper compartments, the move to automated manual transmissions, well-appointed
(and heavy) seats, and advanced suspensions, along with increased driver amenities like upscale interiors,
auxiliary power units and battery HVAC systems. Other driver amenities include electronically controlled
components like power inverters for refrigerators and infotainment systems, enhanced storage, and other

---

<!-- page: 63 -->

creature comforts. Finally, with these larger sleepers, the drivers are able to bring more personal
belongings on-board. On top of the upfront cost of these systems comes a considerable weight penalty.

For the weight-sensitive fleet, this is an inescapable predicament given the current industry-wide driver
shortage. Lightweighting offers fleets the benefit of allowing them to adopt many if not all these driver
amenities, and the need to adopt them reinforces the incentive to lightweight.

## 10.6 Additional Fuel Efficiency Technologies

Just as lightweighting allows fleets to offset the increased weights associated with various technologies
required to meet regulations and retain drivers, it similarly allows them to confront rising fuel prices by
opening the door to additional fuel efficiency technologies. Common examples of such technologies
include aerodynamic devices like fuel tank skirts, air shields, cab extenders, and trailer skirts and tails, all
of which increase the weight of the vehicle. Another weight-adding class of fuel efficiency technology is
that of idle reduction technologies, like diesel APUs or battery HVACs. A truck OEM engineer who was
involved in the company’s DOE SuperTruck program said, “It’s a huge challenge to reduce tractor weight
while you have the need to add new systems for fuel economy.” [35]

Looking more closely at the aerodynamics example: in product development activities, weight is optimized
on a matrix of design considerations along with cost, packaging, fuel economy, and a host of other factors.
Regarding trailers, from an aerodynamics perspective, the long rectangular box is particularly the worst
shape to pull down the highway, and the international standard corrugated ISO containers are even
worse. Therefore, the benefits of aerodynamic packages are measurable on the fuel gauge during each
and every trip. Most aerodynamic technologies are made of injection molded plastic, but still add
hundreds of pounds to the vehicle. The skirts below a trailer add 180 to 250 lbs. while the tail skirt adds
another 120 to 180 lbs., depending on the configuration.

Another newly popular technology that improves fuel economy at the expense of adding weight is the
automated manual transmission (AMT). Though it offers fuel economy improvements of up to 5%, it is up
to 500 lbs. heavier than a manual transmission.

## 11 Challenges and Consequences of Lightweighting

This section summarizes the challenges faced by fleets and the industry as a whole which can make it
difficult to pursue the adoption of lightweighting technologies, and moreover it considers certain
consequences of lightweighting which are likely to further disincentivize such efforts.

## 11.1 Upfront cost

The primary challenge to lightweighting is the additional upfront cost of the technologies. Many options
entail switching from lower-cost steel and iron to higher-cost aluminum, for components like wheels,
hubs, frames and/or crossmembers, clutch and axles housings, air dryers, and battery boxes.

Other solutions like wide-base tires, 6x2 axles, and more also come at increased cost, due to their low
uptake and increased pricing margins at the component and truck and trailer builders. Hopefully these
solutions will come down in price as their volumes increase from niche to more mainstream. Already,
wide-base tires and 6x2 axles are only slightly more expensive than their heavier alternatives.

The most desirable lightweight options are those that both lower weight and reduce cost or are cost
neutral, such as replacing a dual or a single vertical exhaust with shorter horizontal ones, replacing a 15-
liter engine with a 13-liter one, or spec’ing smaller sleepers or fuel tanks. One fleet made an interesting
point: “Now that the newer engines are getting me better fuel economy, I can save weight by buying
smaller fuel tanks and carrying less fuel.”

Many suppliers expressed concern that there are many opportunities to utilize engineering and material
technology to reduce weight, yet there is a lack of interest from OEMs that would likely say there is a lack
of pull from a significant number of fleets. Suppliers promote weight reduction, which helps them
compete with a peer company, yet many say that there is little appetite to sell higher-priced, lightweight
solutions. The tanker truck market is an exception, yet it is only a small segment of the market.

## 11.2 Residual Value

The resale or residual value of a tractor is a significant factor in the specification of a new truck. Fleets
that resell trucks themselves may turn them over as soon as three years or 500,000 miles, others do so
closer to six years or 750,000 miles. Large fleets are turning over hundreds or thousands of trucks per
year; the ability to sell or trade-in quickly for the maximum price is imperative.

Unfortunately, some lightweight options may negatively affect driver satisfaction and/or residual value.
Examples include 13-liter engines, shorter and/or flat-top sleeper configurations, 6x2 vs. 6x4 rear axles,
de-contenting cab and sleeper interiors, and less fuel tank capacity.

For instance, in the case of the 5th wheel, a sliding wheel sells quicker than a stationary wheel, even
though the stationary wheel is lighter and costs less. Even if the first buyer doesn’t need a sliding 5th
wheel, they will purchase it, and then lock down the sliding function for the first vehicle life, with hopes
of selling that vehicle faster than a similar vehicle with a stationary 5th wheel. This challenge is not unique
to lightweighting — the lack of an established residual value creates a barrier to the adoption of many
new technologies. When anything is new, it won’t have been on the US market long enough for fleets to

---

<!-- page: 65 -->

understand how it will be received by the secondary market, so the misunderstanding or confusion leads
to inaction; confused buyers don’t buy.

This challenge can be overcome, however. One well-known lightweighting fleet reported that it sells its
vehicles after three years to used truck buyers who have come to appreciate lightweight trucks. Another
lightweight fleet works to turn it into an advantage, telling the study team that: “The trucks in our fleet
are lightweight and get excellent fuel economy and we promote both when we resell these trucks to the
second owner.” These types of residual sales relationships are critical to sustaining the use of
lightweighting and for that matter, other freight efficiency features at large truck fleets.

## 11.3 Maintenance Costs

Another challenge to the wider adoption of lightweighting technologies is that they may entail higher
operating and/or maintenance costs. For example, some aluminum components are not as robust as their
steel or iron counterparts; aluminum wheels are a notable exception due to superior coating technology
that enables far less maintenance than steel wheels. Aluminum landing gear is more easily damaged while
moving trailers than steel-based landing gear, while aluminum trailer flooring is more easily damaged by
forklifts than wood or wood-composite flooring.

Other examples include 6x2 axles, which may accelerate tire wear compared to 6x4 tandem rear axles,
and wide-base tires, which, while delivering both lower weight and higher fuel economy, may not last as
long as standard dual tires and moreover have less retread life and higher replacement costs. One final
concern mentioned by a few fleets is the potential for items such as aluminum wheels on trailers being
prone to theft.

However, the decreased weight and resulting gains in payload may more than compensate for the
additional repair or maintenance costs of some components. It is critical that the total cost of operating
specific lightweight components be investigated for each fleet’s duty cycle and operating environment.

## 11.4 Redundant Product Testing

A long validation path for lightweighting features was described by many interviewees as a barrier to
adoption for these components. Due to material and or design changes, the supply chain generally
requires testing to validate that the new component meets the durability requirements of the fleet and
OEM. One interviewee said, “One hurdle from the product developer perspective is the time to market,
or time to the ROI. They develop and test the product to gain OEM interest. The OEM tests it again. Then
the fleet tests it again. Is there a faster way?”

This was a primary finding of some work completed by NACFE in 2013 in its study Barriers to Technology
Adoption. [51] That study found that there was significant redundancy in the product development efforts
for all fuel-efficiency technologies. This issue seems to be present for all new products but stood out as a
bigger challenge for lightweighting components.

Respondents queried for this Confidence Report concurred, sharing that lightweighting features do, in
fact, enter the marketplace via a variety of paths. OEMs even said that product development was as
difficult, adding that it’s a challenge to get on the priority lists at the fleets to get new products evaluated.
Some fleets will pluck innovative technologies from the aftermarket because it’s faster than the OEM

---

<!-- page: 66 -->

route, yet others appreciate the due diligence at the OEM and find that it is the best path for new product
introduction. At times, fleets that adopt features through aftermarket channels end up pulling the product
back into the development cycle at tractor and trailer builders.

A common response from OEMs and fleets points to the lack of transparency in the test data. At times
only a subset of the data is shared, showing only its advantages yet lacking the balance or complete picture
for a technology. Product developers typically scream about the benefits and whisper the drawbacks,
which encourages the OEM and fleets to test the product in-house. The ideal scenario is one where the
product developer, the OEM, and the fleet are testing to the same specifications, and the data is shared
in raw form. Even then, many fleets want to test in all four seasons, to ensure that a product performs in
a variety of weather conditions.

Charlie Janis, a product innovator from Century Inc., says, “No one wants to be first, yet they are lined up
around the block to be second.” A fleet manager confirmed this by saying that new production
introductions are tough because the initial testing never goes well. Everyone needs to be patient. Getting
the early adopters to participate is a significant challenge for product developers, especially those who
are new to the market.

In the fleet interviews, the study team heard success stories where a small fleet worked closely with an
OEM, particularly in trailers, and together they cut significant weight. One example was 2,000 lbs. cut
from a flatbed trailer. The fleet and the OEM evaluated several trailers on test rigs and a few in the field,
then met to fix the issues and now tens of these reduced-weight trailers are in the field.

## 11.5 Ability to Take Advantage

When considering the return on investment of the additional costs of lightweighting features, the fleet
needs to know how much additional revenue and/or lower operating costs those features will allow them
to generate. In North America, routes are generally disparate on a weekly basis, especially in the Category
3 general freight sector. This means that the load and route will vary significantly trip to trip and it is
unpredictable. In this sector, it is difficult to operate equipment specific to a particular set of variables.
But, as payload becomes denser, more routes will benefit from lightweighting. Or said differently, over
time, shippers and carriers will need to remove pallets from trailers in more cases due to the tractor-
trailer being over the legal weight limit. Having space in a trailer, but not being able to use it due to being
overweight leads to more trucks to the road increasing congestion, fuel use and emissions produced.
Traditionally, some fleets and owner-operators have run overweight, but with the advent of CSA, a
number of fleets shared that this practice is rarely used. Overweight fines are public information and fleets
and drivers both are motivated to operate within the weight limits. And they do so regularly.

Finally, correctly specifying components on equipment can have weight savings potential. Some
interviewees shared that the industry over-designs and specs equipment oftentimes adding unneeded
weight for the job. One interviewee said, “If there were better predictive tools on when a product needed
to be changed, you could make products lighter. Trucks are often designed for unique situations that occur
only a very little amount of the time. This adds a lot of weight to the product.”

---

<!-- page: 67 -->

Another weight-conscious fleet said, “Tractors with smaller sleepers and 13-liter engines definitely cost
and weigh less, but we have to pay higher wages to drivers to operate them. We need to factor that in
when we calculate the real ROI.”

The metric of dollar-per-pound expresses the amount of money a fleet is willing to pay for a lightweighting
technology, stemming from the amount each increment of weight savings is worth to them. However, this
seemingly simple metric actually is quite complex. Everyone uses it, from the manufacturer trying to make
a new product to the integrator buying the product and doing the integration, then marking it up and
trying to meet the vehicle manufacturer’s metric. The OEM marks it up again and works with the fleets to
place the product.

Once a product is standard equipment, like an aluminum wheel on a dry van, then no one debates its
dollar-per-pound status. Until then and for each new application, it is competing with all the other options
in the cost and weight matrix, and it is much higher than $1/lb. Aluminum wheels have been qualitatively
quoted as “half the weight and three times the cost” of steel wheels.

## 11.6 Resistance to Change

One OEM said, “Trucking is big on reliability and payback, so the uptake is always slow. There is a lot of
technology out there that could be used but is not. [There needs to be] a balance of weight and cost. We
offer lightweight axle set-ups and a variety of other lightweight systems. If a tractor cost is $110,000, you
can cut a lot of weight for $1,500, just 1% of the total. But you aren’t going to spend $500 on something
you aren’t going to use. If you own tractors and trailers, then you would do lightweighting on both,
otherwise just the tractor.”

## 12 Future Innovations

The study team asked industry stakeholders whether they saw or were aware of any technologies in the
pipeline that would enable even more lightweight designs.

The answers largely focused on advanced materials. One system design sales manager said, “It’s going to
come down to different materials. We’ve done well with steels; we’ve done great with aluminum. The
aluminum cost delta has come way down. What is the next material that can be industrialized? Titanium
is strong and light, but crazy expensive. If you go to the IAA (Commercial Vehicle Show in Hanover
Germany) you’ll see composites.”

Another respondent said, “The greatest hope is in better materials: metal matrix composites (MMCs),
better aluminums and carbon fiber trailer axle…but those are so expensive.”

## 12.1 Future Cab & Frames

The DOE SuperTruck programs are pushing the limits of trucking efficiency in engines, aerodynamics,
rolling efficiency, and lightweighting [35]. In SuperTruck II, the target tractor weight is less than 15,000
lbs., and the teams demonstrated a number of lightweighting initiatives in the cab, the frame structure,
and related components.

---

<!-- page: 68 -->

One proof of concept on the Volvo truck was an aluminum air tank integrated as a crossmember, to serve
two critical functions [36]. The same truck had a lightweight frame design that cut 35% of the baseline
frame weight.

The Peterbilt team developed a multi-material frame rail design utilizing a hybrid of aluminum and steel,
and taller sections, and it delivered 500 lbs. of weight reduction [37].

Navistar’s SuperTruck pushed into non-metallics with composite frame rail sections, crossmembers and a
composite end cap structure [38]. Navistar even proposed a composite trailer design built over a steel
skeleton.

It is not known whether the SuperTruck lightweighting technologies will be commercialized, yet as
described in this updated report, the commercial market is also working on cab and frame systems for the
next major weight reduction opportunities.

One surprise in the report update effort is the replacement of paint to cut weight. Already used in
motorsports, AERO paint replacement films are just 50 microns thick and when applied to a day cab can
cut 18 to 44 lbs., and across the 53’ trailer, 57 to 147 lbs. when replacing the base coat, color and clear
coat [39]. In addition to weight reduction, a nice benefit for start-up OEMs is the elimination of the paint
shop and the volatile organic chemicals (VOCs) produced.

Lightweight frame rails are big-ticket items that could shave hundreds of pounds off the tractor, and one
such approach is to tailor the thickness of the rail where it is advantageous. The variable thickness rail, in
the traditional C-channel, can be thin on the vertical and thick on the flanges, even varying thickness
between top and bottom flanges [40]. The projected weight savings in a 10-meter rail length is 287 lbs.,
and even more, 380 lbs., if a C-channel with a liner was converted to a variable thickness rail without a
liner.

Material and process innovations will continue to enable lighter weight and high-performance vehicles.
In steel, niobium is a grain refining element, when added in very low amounts yet it improves the strength,
formability and toughness of steel [41]. In aluminum, new alloys and processes like roll forming, friction
stir welding, castings and extrusions will find value through new products and material conversions.

during 2014 and 2015 with a radical new design of a tractor and trailer, in which the trailer body was made
of carbon fiber composite, delivering a weight savings of 4,000 lbs. (See Figure 31.)

## 13 Study Findings

As the study team completed this work, some high-level study findings emerged and are shared here in
order to help educate the industry so it can take advantage of the opportunities lightweighting presents
for Class 8 over-the-road tractor-trailers in order to improve freight efficiency.

* Through surveys conducted for this Confidence Report, the trucking industry confirmed the following
trends:
  1. Tractor, and to some degree, trailer weights have increased,
  2. Freight is becoming denser, and
  3. Shippers are loading more pallets per trailer.
* Currently, the market can be divided into three categories with respect to weight sensitivity and the
percent of time that fleets are requested to gross out.
  * **Category 1:** Trucks that currently travel at the 80,000-lbs. limit at some point along nearly
  100% of their routes. This represents a small percentage of the industry (about 2%). This
  segment consists of bulk carriers.
  * **Category 2:** Trucks that are loaded to the maximum weight (gross out) on a minority, perhaps
  10%, of their trips. This represents about 10% of the trucks on the road, mostly refrigerator
  units but also some dry van routes.
  * **Category 3:** Dry van units that rarely (2% of the time) or never travel at maximum weight,
  either because they are filled to maximum volume (cube out) before they gross out, or simply
  because their routes and cargo patterns are not conducive to traveling full. About 88% of the
  trucking industry falls into this category.
* NACFE finds that over the next five to 10 years and in light of these trends, shippers will request that
Category 2 and Category 3 trucks double the percent of time they gross out, to 20% of the time for
Category 2 and 4% of the time for Category 3.

---

<!-- page: 71 -->

* In order to meet these trends head-on and accommodate the heavier, denser freight, Category 2 and
3 fleets will have two options — add more trucks to the road, or explore lightweighting so that at least
some of their trucks will be able to carry more freight.
* Lightweighting is by far the better option. Along with improving the overall freight efficiency of a fleet,
lightweighting is a good investment because it opens the door for the adoption of additional beneficial
technologies that might otherwise make a truck unacceptably heavier than it currently is.

## 15 Recommendations

After completing its work, the study team developed a list of recommendations for the industry to use
when exploring lightweighting.

* Above all, this report recommends that Category 2 and even Category 3 fleets begin to explore
and spec lightweighting technologies in order to improve freight efficiency in the face of current
industry trend. By doing so, they may even produce economies of scale that bring down the
upfront cost of these technologies, while perhaps opening up new options for Category 1 fleets
to adopt technologies that are currently too expensive.
* Another recommendation to bring down costs is for the industry to collaborate in order to address
current redundancy in the development, testing, and product availability cycle among product
developers, tractor-trailer integrators, and fleets.
* Finally, lightweighting for freight efficiency is a critical component of fuel efficiency, as it will
facilitate the adoption of additional fuel efficiency technologies. Fleets should spec lightweighting
options together with their other efficiency choices.
