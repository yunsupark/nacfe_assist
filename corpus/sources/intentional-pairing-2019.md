# 3 EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

At the request of a major truck manufacturer, the North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) investigated the factors relevant to intentionally matching a specific tractor model to specific trailer model in logistics systems for the purpose of maximizing fuel economy, minimizing emissions and improving profitability.

NACFE researched available public data, compiling some 90 references listed in the bibliography. Sources include NACFE's own extensive reports. NACFE conducted a limited survey with 14 fleets out of 50 responding. NACFE talked with large and medium sized fleets like PepsiCo, NFI, Schneider, CFI, Nussbaum, Maverick, Bison, UPS, Werner, Hirschbach, Ryder, and others representing a significant sampling of freight industry operations. NACFE consulted with owner-operators (O/O) such as Henry Albert, and with O/O experts such as at ACT Research and Let's Truck's Kevin Rutherford.

NACFE determined that the majority of tractors pulling dry van and refrigerated 53-foot trailers operate in a drop and hook mode, keeping the tractor assets moving as much as feasible rather than waiting for trailers to be loaded or unloaded. As fleet size reduces, O/O fleets see paired tractors and trailers in operation, where the driver will wait for trailer loading or unloading. Many of these situations are linked to refrigerated trailer operations. These O/O fleets tend to be populated with older model year equipment.

NACFE concludes that while the majority of the market place currently does not intentionally pair tractor and trailer by model type, benefits would be had by fleets purchasing SuperTruck tractors and SuperTruck trailers as shown with Volvo's SuperTruck 1 demonstration vehicle in Figure 1, and the rendering of its SuperTruck 2 demonstration unit in Figure 2, as long as the individual units were compatible with existing equipment.

Pairing a SuperTruck trailer to an older model year tractor would still result in MPG benefits, as would pairing a SuperTruck tractor to less aerodynamic trailers. Benefits would be on a sliding scale, and fleet annual net MPGs would improve. Intentionally pairing by model type would provide the greatest opportunity for on-highway MPG gains, but this could only occur reliably where 100% of tractors and trailers were of the same configuration. In all other situations, benefits would vary based on a stochastic probability distribution of assets.

The market also includes fleets that only operate tractors and other fleets that only supply trailers. Arrangements can also be made between fleets to pull competitor trailers where needed. These situations further complicate reliably predicting which trailers will be available at locations for tractors to pull.

While capital asset ratios like fleet tractor to trailer are important, load routing includes other market variables like time, pricing, load availability, asset location, driver availability and capability, etc. A key measure of this variability is reflected, for example, by DAT Management's reporting of Load-to-Tractor (L/T) ratios across the market, seasons and geography. Intentional pairing would suggest a much higher number of trailers to tractors would be needed to position assets in all regions to permit reliable 100% pairing without long dwell times for drivers and tractors.

NACFE Summary of Findings

1. Intentional pairing of tractors and trailers by models in volume is technically feasible, but likely requires all assets to have independent GPS tracking and fleets need to be able to identify tractors and trailers by model (hence their characteristics) in assigning load using their load logistics systems algorithms. Intentional pairing today only occurs in isolated cases such as special purpose hauling where the trailers are customized for specific loads, promotional or event vehicles, and fleets with small numbers of tractors and trailers. Fleets pair in specific niches/examples where realities demand and economics have a good return. And while intentional pairing is feasible, it is not realistic given the realities of todays' trucking industry

2. Trailer pools or "drop and hook" is a fixture in North American goods movement and helps with asset utilization and overall goods movement efficiency. The focus is on keeping both the tractor and the driver in motion to maximize revenue miles by reducing dwell time at facilities, as documented in NACFE's report More Regional Haul: An Opportunity for Trucking? [97].

3. New technology increases the feasibility and lowers the cost and complexity of matching/pairing. The evolution of the internet-of-things means real time asset management systems can have independent access to driver information, tractor characteristics, trailer characteristics, asset status and location, loads, routes, weather, etc.

4. Fleets would consider matching more tractor-trailer by models for savings. A 5% to 10% net improvement in truck operating costs could be sufficient to interest fleets in changing operations to facilitate intentional pairing. The analysis needs to include factors such as net annual revenue miles, driver hours of service limits on mileage, customer retention, etc. in addition to trip MPG factors.

5. Large fleets will likely never have 100% of the same model year tractors or trailers because of the partial annual replacement of fleet assets. Every new model year likely introduces new technology improvements, even for SuperTruck tractors and trailers, through the natural market pressure for continuous improvement in performance and need for cost reduction.

6. Intentional pairing has potential for a number of vehicle parameters — for example, lightweight tractors paired to lightweight trailers can maximize payload in a tradeoff with service life and gradeability. Down-sped transmissions can be optimized for specific routes and loads. High power tractors can be focused on demanding mountain routes. Aerodynamically optimized units can be focused on higher speed routes where they obtain the best benefits versus non-aerodynamic ones.

There is a notion that intentionally pairing tractors and trailers by model characteristics has the potential to benefit freight transport, but only in some specific circumstances. Intentional pairing would be a fundamental shift in the majority of current operations where drop and hook prevails. However, whether implicitly or explicitly, some level of intentional pairing is already done today, such as deciding if a trailer needs to be refrigerated or dry, or deciding if a 48 foot or 53 foot is required, or if in a trailer yard, a particular empty trailer needs to be moved to another site to meet market demands.

Intentional pairing has costs in hardware, software and operations. The total cost of ownership model needs to continue to mature to more accurately represent specific fleet operations and account for net cost/benefits that include the impact of driver dwell time on net annual revenue miles. The added detail can improve confidence in the ROI projections that must compete for capital investment at each fleet.

# 4 INTRODUCTION

The SuperTruck 2 tractor and trailer demonstrates significant potential fuel efficiency improvements. Aerodynamic gains are a major contributor to that potential. However, in the commercial trucking world, aerodynamic technologies must compete in a broad spectrum of freight efficiency improvement investment opportunities.

The North American Council for Freight Efficiency, NACFE, prepared this report analyzing and suggesting factors to include in any cost/benefit analysis of deploying SuperTruck 2 aerodynamic trailers and differentiating the potential gains in real world operations.

The task of estimating benefits for trailer technology is inherently probabilistic because:
* Fleets prefer drop and hook operations pulling randomly from pools of trailers as seen in Figure 3 with a large number of trailers at a facility but few tractors. A smaller number of fleets pair tractors and trailers, as pictured in Figure 4, where each trailer has a refrigerated trailer.
* Fleets generally do not match each trip for a particular model of trailer to a specific model of tractor, so many permutations are possible as shown in Figure 5.
* Trailer-tractor ratio's average ~3:1 for dry vans, but ratios can vary from 1:1 to more than 10:1 depending on fleets, meaning the benefits of any one trailer varies differentially from tractor mileage as illustrated in Figure 6. Refrigerated vans average 1.2:1 but also can vary.
* The statistical distribution of specific trailer models and tractor models may be skewed by a variety of operational and regional factors beyond what the simplistic trailer to tractor ratio may suggest. A metric used in industry load order board tracking is Tractor to Trailer Load ratio (T/L), which varies by region and market conditions.
* Some fleets only provide trailers, and in other cases, trailers may be shared between competing fleets through agreements.
* Routing, speeds, miles, loads, conditions, driver skills, all can vary each trip.
* Fleets vary considerably, even inside a single company, multiple duty cycles may exist. National averages may not reflect each fleet's actual operation.

# 5 FREIGHT TRAILER USAGE MODELING METHODS

Freight system modeling is complex and described by the term dynamic allocation. NACFE identified two publicly available references that provide summaries of mathematical modeling methods: a) A Survey of Optimization Models for Long-Haul Freight Transport, by Crainic (et al), published in 2003 contains an extensive development of theory on modeling [65], and b) A Framework for Modeling Freight Railcar Routing Problems with a Time Window, by Samanta and Mishra, published in 2011 which contains a succinct literature review on the topic [66].

Crainic (see pages 46-48 of [65]) provides a particularly good description of the challenges in modeling the freight system that NACFE concludes is relevant to matching particular models of trailers to particular models of tractors.

> *Many operational problems, fleet management in particular, dynamically allocate limited resources to requests and tasks. For example, empty vehicles, trailers and rail cars are allocated to the appropriate terminals; motive power tractors and locomotives to services; crews to vehicles or services; loads to driver-truck combinations; empty containers from depots to customers and returning containers from customers to depots; and so on. All these problems have several common characteristics:*
> 
> *1. Some future demands are known, but most can only be forecasted, and unpredictable requests may happen.*
> 
> *2. Many requests materialize in real or quasi-real time and must be acted upon in relatively short time.*
> 
> *3. Once a resource is allocated to an activity, it is no longer available for a certain duration (whose length may be subject to variations as well).*
> 
> *4. Once a resource becomes available again, it is often in a different location than its initial one.*
> 
> *5. The value of an additional unit of a given resource at a location greatly depends on the total quantity of resources available (which are determined from previous decisions at potentially all terminals in previous periods) and the current demand.*

Crainic provides an example of a freight system in a network diagram format, with this description:

> *One may represent dynamic allocation issues by an activity graph similar to the one displayed in Figure 7. Here, the operations of a simple four-terminal system are schematically drawn for a certain length of time, which is arbitrarily divided into three periods. At each terminal, there are a number of vehicles that are available to satisfy customer requests during the current period and in future ones. Customer demands have precise characteristics, such as the origin and destination of movement, and pickup and delivery dates (with time window restrictions, eventually). At any period, a vehicle may be assigned to a customer demand in the current period and at the current location, moved to another location to satisfy a known future request, held at the current location, or moved empty to another location in preparation for future, forecasted demands.*

NACFE concludes that the nature of intentionally pairing specific models of trailers with specific models of tractors is probabilistic in nature, somewhat akin to the quantum physic's premise that one cannot know the position of a particle, one can only predict its location with probabilities. An article in Forbes explaining quantum physics summarizes this as, "All we can predict is probability [68]."

These problems are statistical and the probabilities can be estimated, but they would differ for each fleet and all its operations. The ramifications for a fleet's capital investment in new SuperTruck tractors and SuperTruck trailers is that as more enter the system, the net fuel economy would improve, eventually reaching the max fuel economy when all tractors and trailers are SuperTruck types.

The methodologies outlined in Crainic and discussed in the literature search in Samanta and Mishra provide insights on ways to model a plan to intentionally pair specific model tractors and trailers based on configuration to achieve the best performance. The methods that place a value on time can highlight where a driver waits for a trailer to arrive so that the tractor is mated to the correct model trailer. This dwell time is not moving freight, or moving at all, so has no factor on MPG, but does factor into net miles a driver could achieve under hours of service rules per day or week, so impacts paid annual vehicle miles travelled.

A requirement that a SuperTruck tractor wait for an appropriate SuperTruck trailer at a location would have a net cost to a fleet resulting in having less paid miles annually. The trade-off in improved vehicle fuel efficiency when paired would be against differences in net annual revenue miles driven.

Rules could be developed that SuperTruck tractors and SuperTruck trailers are only used on specific lanes, ensuring that the density at each end of the lanes is non-zero, but the other key factor is that there must be a load needing to be transported at those times, again, a probabilistic non-certainty.

This example is simplistic to make the point that real world prediction of trailer asset locations accounting for actual operations, market conditions, etc. is complex, with the only certainty occurring when an entire network is using specific models.

# FLEET MPG & DEVICE TRENDS

NACFE publishes an Annual Fleet Fuel Study (AFFS), which is a deep-dive investigation into the adoption of various products and practices for improving freight efficiency among 20 major North American fleets. The scope of this work encompassed Class 8 tractors (day cabs and sleepers) and trailers in regional and long-haul applications. Fleets providing data for this 2018 study include Bison Transport, Cardinal Logistics, CFI, CR England, Challenger Motor Freight, Crete, Frito-Lay, Hirschbach, Maverick, Mesilla Valley Transportation, NFI Industries, Nussbaum, Paper Transport, Prime, Ryder System Inc., Schneider, United Parcel Service, and US Xpress. The study uses accumulated data on the purchasing habits of these 20 fleets, operating more than 71,844 tractors and 236,292 trailers.

The primary goal of the NACFE AFFS report is to study the fleets' levels of adoption of 85 technologies and practices, and the results those drove in each organization. All 85 technologies are currently available and not prototypes, validation test units, or pre-production units. This study focuses on technologies purchased and implemented onto a fleet's trucks and trailers. Figure 13 shows the adoption curves for all 85 technologies mapped versus time.

# ESTIMATING BENEFITS

Adapting real world freight practices to estimating the potential benefits for deploying a SuperTruck 2 trailer is complex, affected by a number of real world operational factors. Viewed statistically, a specific trailer has a certain probability of being mated to a particular tractor configuration on any given trip. The trailer loading for each trip varies so is also a probability of any particular weight. The duty cycle of that specific trailer on that specific trip is also modeled as a probability from a myriad of potential duty cycles, speed profiles, grade profiles, range profiles, etc. The environmental factors also play into each trip, with significant variations by region and by season.

National averages are really only relevant to national questions. Each fleet's operations and business investments can vary significantly from these averages. Fleets also tend to have a mixture of operations, so there are multiple models possible within each fleet.

The tractor driver is also a significant variable from vehicle to vehicle. A skilled, focused driver can be 30% better as exemplified by the AFFS study versus the Run on Less results where the average of the 20 NACFE fleets was 7.26 MPG but the RunonLess vehicles from those fleets were exceeding 10 MP. Aerodynamic devices that require driver deployment have been shown to have less than 100% activation on each trailer trip.

### 7.1.1 Larger Fleets

A simplistic analysis of the benefits of one trailer might be to assume it is constantly paired to a specific type of tractor. That can occur where 100% of the tractors and 100% of the trailers owned by a fleet are the same configurations. Fleets typically make tractor and trailer capital investments over years so that only a portion of their fleets are replaced each year. If the fleet tractor trade cycle is six years, then they likely replace 16% of their fleet each year. If the fleet trailer trade cycle is 12 years, then they likely

<!-- page: 29 -->
replace 8% of their trailers each year. This purchasing means fleets are always made up of multiple model years of products.

A new tractor introduced in 2020 would have to be purchased on average for six years, or 2026, before that fleet had migrated 100% of its product to the new design. It would be 2032 before the trailer fleet had completely migrated.

Pairing a tractor and trailer then becomes a matter of probabilistic estimation. At each trailer pick up, what is the probability that the new SuperTruck tractor would be paired with a SuperTruck trailer? This probability would change each year that the fleet purchased new vehicles. Simplistically, in year 1, assuming the fleet only used its own trailers, a SuperTruck would have a 16% chance of being paired with a SuperTruck trailer as shown in Figure 21. The actual probability distribution would likely be affected by the density of routes and locations serviced, weather conditions, or other operational conditions.

### 7.1.2 Smaller Fleets

OOIDA estimates, "There are approximately 350,000-400,000 owner-operators driving in the United States today and owner-operators represent 10% of the truckers on the road [69]. OOIDA concludes, "The average owner-operator has a 2006 model year truck. While van trailers are the most common trailer pulled in industry, the majority of owner-operators pull flatbed or reefer trailers. Owner-operators logged nearly 101,000 miles in 2018, of which 19,000 was deadhead [69]."

A 2013 Owner Operator magazine article by Randy Grider reported research that "93% of independent owner-operators own their own trailers, as do 48% of leased owner-operators [43]." The trade-off in trailers dedicated to tractors is typically in paid miles per year, as the loading and unloading operations idle the tractors more than drop and hook operations. The trailers often are also specialized for particular load types making them less flexible for alternative loads. This may lead to greater dead heading of near empty trailers, which while an improvement to annual fuel economy is a net reduction in the more important freight efficiency. Grider quotes Todd Amen, president and chief executive officer of the owner-operator financial services company ATBS, that an owner-operator's "biggest pitfall is owning a trailer that hinders your productivity in a high-volume drop and hook segment of the industry — dry van and reefer," Amen says. "You may have to wait hours to get your trailer unloaded, whereas if you were operating in a pool of trailers provided by a fleet or shipper, you can drop and hook in minutes and continue driving, which is what you get paid for."

### 7.1.3 Potential Market

Any fleet could benefit from technology that improves freight efficiency. The extent of benefit would depend on a number of factors. NACFE has reported that a fleet purchasing, for example, a Model Year 2018 tractor might see a significant gain over their MY 2012 tractor because of the improvements made in the interim years. That fleet is unlikely to be replacing 100% of their vehicles, so they are accepting

<!-- page: 30 -->
that the fleet would have a net fleet fuel economy gain, but the gain would be graduated. The introduction of SuperTruck tractors and trailers would provide benefits even if intentional pairing did not occur.

Those fleets where 100% of tractor and trailer assets are replaced at one time could see 100% of the potential fuel economy benefit in a year. The number of those fleets is, however small — OOIDA's FMCSA estimate is 17% of tractors are in fleets smaller than 20 vehicles (1.5 million tractor in use in 2018), only 10% in fleets smaller than 6 vehicles (0.9 million tractors in use in 2018). Many of those are dedicated to flat beds or other non-van type trailers not suitable for SuperTruck tractor-trailer pairing.

Today the group hauling refrigerated trailers is the most likely candidate where the tractor and trailer could likely intentionally stay paired due to the duty cycle with multiple stop deliveries or truly dedicated routes and loads, as illustrated in Figure 22 with a delivery of cold foods to a gas station convenience store. ## 7.2 TRAILER TRIP LENGTH

Trailers primarily exist to move freight. Fleets generally do not care which trailer is used, only that the freight gets delivered on time. Only in recent years have fleets started using GPS tracking of trailers, and recording which trailers are mated to which tractors on each run. This growing database will aid in optimizing future trailer asset utilization, but at this point in time, NACFE believes the state of the

industry is that fleets are not focused on pairing tractors and trailers based on performance. In the current state of freight movement, fleets are scrambling for drivers and any tractors.

## 7.5 TRAILER DUTY CYCLES

Specific trailer duty cycles vary because they are not dedicated to any particular route, load, or use. Fleet information indicates that as trailers age, they may migrate from long haul to more urban routes, and then to warehouse extra storage. At present, with market demand high for freight assets, NACFE expects that trailer age or configuration is not a significant factor in choosing routes, but it could be in the future with increased use of trailer tracking and data mining.

A typical week for a trailer should be considered when estimating benefits from new technology. The average national trailer to tractor ratio varies between 2.5 and 3.5. If we assume 3 dry van trailers for each tractor that means two are not moving at any given time. Vehicle miles travelled for each trailer is one third that of the tractors. It takes three new SuperTruck trailers for each SuperTruck tractor to attain its full annual potential savings from vehicle miles travelled as a unit.

## TRAILER AGE

Dry van trailers can have significantly long commercial lives exceeding 20 years. During the course of their utilization, they may see duty cycles that include significant long-haul highway miles at various levels of loading from zero to the maximum allowable. As they age, they may migrate to shorter urban routes or be used as temporary storage at facilities that lack sufficient warehouse space. Predicting actual annual miles for each trailer using national averages is inherently problematic since the duty cycles can vary significantly.

## INTENTIONAL PAIRING EXAMPLES

NACFE discovered in its interviews and research that there are specific examples of intentional pairing around fleet operations. In one case, the fleet operates both long combination vehicle (LCV) doubles and refrigerated singles. Over time, they have evolved to having a preferred lightweight specification for single tractors that permits them to operate with 45,000 lb. freight loads for a market advantage. They also have a preferred high horsepower tractor for pulling the doubles. Their trailers follow a common lightweight specification and are equipped for either single or double operations, providing flexibility. Their load management system tracks both tractors and trailers and ensures the correct models are joined.

A second example NACFE uncovered involved lightweight trailers to handle maximum weight freight loads (bottled liquids). The fleet had challenges in demonstrating benefits because of market demands with a limited population of configured trailers. A third example attempted to make use of extend length trailers beyond 53’. This investigation found that the trailers became geographically isolated in the system due to the variety of regional length and weight laws, reducing their utilization.

Intentional pairing occurs today where a fleet owner, for example, provides both tractor and trailer for a customer requiring freight movement, termed a freight forwarder [79]. Special pairing occurs where the trailer has specific operational configurations such as flatbeds for heavy equipment, bulk tankers for specific chemicals, or any trailers that are purpose built for custom load types. Other pairing occurs where the freight forwarder provides both the tractor and the trailer and chooses to keep them paired for operational reasons, for example dedicated recurring refrigerated routes.

## TEAM DRIVERS AND SLIP SEATING

Team driving and slip seating increase the miles per year for vehicles. They both represent efforts to keep the tractor and trailer assets continuously gaining revenue miles, expanding the permissible daily and weekly mileage limited by the single driver Hours of Service rules. Team driving means two drivers are in the truck at the same time each driving their allowed hours of service. Slip seating occurs when one driver leaves the truck at the end of his or her shift and another driver takes the same truck for his or her shift

Team or single driver (private fleets dual driver 25% of private fleets use team drivers, and for those, 33% of miles are team driven in 2017 data [17]). The 2017 data for private fleets shows 25% of private fleets use team drivers, and for those, 33% of miles are team driven [17]. Slip seating, which is not team driving, occurs in 59% of fleets and of those, 48% of miles are slip seated [17]. Both these factors can affect the amount of miles accumulated per year per tractor, and hence impact the location of trailers by region and season.

## TRAILER FUTURE CONFIGURATIONS

The basic configuration and purpose of trailers has evolved over decades. Trailer lengths and weights have changed over time, gradually progressing from straight trucks, to short semi-trailers and then to 48-foot and 53-foot lengths. There is nothing particularly sacrosanct about the 53’ length other than there is a huge volume of existing units in use. One concept floated by NACFE in prior reports is suggesting the double 53-foot trailers, permissible in some regions of the U.S., much of Canada, Australia and other locations, could be more commonly allowed taking advantage of advances in both car and tractor safety sensor systems and automated driver assistance systems. This seems increasingly viable if platooning trucks becomes a reality, as one truck pulling two 53-foot trailers has been shown to be a net performance and cost advantage over two tractors pulling two trailers separated by very short gaps [82]. Electric vehicles combined with autonomous operations suggest the elimination of the cab and human driver system is possible, as prototyped with the Volvo Vera shown in Figure 96, or the Einride T-Pod units that consolidate power systems into the trailer as shown in Figure 97.

NACFE suggests that the freight hauling has been gradually migrating from one-size-fits-all tractors and trailers to specialization, where fleets may have multiple tractor specification for specific routes and duty cycles. Average route lengths may change as drivers push to be closer to home and family each week. The growth in e-Commerce may change volumes from larger trailers to smaller trucks.

Trade cycles may change as the level of electronic sophistication increases, responding to the need to more regularly upgrade hardware for new capacity and capabilities.

In this future there seems opportunity for intentional pairing to be a viable option for fleets to investigate.

## 11 SUMMARY FINDINGS

1. Intentional pairing of tractors and trailers by models in volume is technically feasible, but likely requires all assets to have independent GPS tracking and fleets need to be able to identify tractors and trailers by model (hence their characteristics) in assigning load using their load logistics systems algorithms. Intentional pairing today only occurs in isolated cases such as special purpose hauling where the trailers are customized for specific loads, promotional or event vehicles, and fleets with small numbers of tractors and trailers. Fleets pair in specific niches/examples where realities demand and economics have a good return. And while intentional pairing is feasible, it is not realistic given the realities of todays’ trucking industry
2. Trailer pools or “drop and hook” is a fixture in North American goods movement and helps with asset utilization and overall goods movement efficiency. The focus is on keeping both the tractor and the driver in motion to maximize revenue miles by reducing dwell time at facilities, as documented in NACFE’s report *More Regional Haul: An Opportunity for Trucking?* [97].
3. New technology increases the feasibility and lowers the cost and complexity of matching/pairing. The evolution of the internet-of-things means real time asset management systems can have independent access to driver information, tractor characteristics, trailer characteristics, asset status and location, loads, routes, weather, etc.
4. Fleets would consider matching more tractor-trailer by models for savings. A 5% to 10% net improvement in truck operating costs could be sufficient to interest fleets in changing operations to facilitate intentional pairing. The analysis needs to include factors such as net annual revenue miles, driver hours of service limits on mileage, customer retention, etc. in addition to trip mpg factors.

5. Large fleets will likely never have 100% of the same model year tractors or trailers because of the partial annual replacement of fleet assets. Every new model year likely introduces new technology improvements, even for SuperTruck tractors and trailers, through the natural market pressure for continuous improvement in performance and need for cost reduction.
6. Intentional pairing has potential for a number of vehicle parameters — for example, lightweight tractors paired to lightweight trailers can maximize payload in a tradeoff with service life and gradeability. Down-sped transmissions can be optimized for specific routes and loads. High power tractors can be focused on demanding mountain routes. Aerodynamically optimized units can be focused on higher speed routes where they obtain the best benefits versus non-aerodynamic ones.

## 12 RECOMMENDATIONS

Intentionally pairing tractors and trailers by model characteristics has the potential to benefit freight transport, but only in some specific circumstances. Intentional pairing would be a fundamental shift in the majority of current operations where drop and hook prevails. However, whether implicitly or explicitly, some level of intentional pairing is already done today, such as deciding if a trailer needs to be refrigerated or dry, or deciding if a 48-foot or 53-foot is required, or if in a trailer yard, a particular empty trailer needs to be moved to another site to meet market demands.

Intentional pairing has costs in hardware, software and operations. The total cost of ownership model needs to continue to mature to more accurately represent specific fleet operations and account for net cost/benefits that include the impact of driver dwell time on net annual revenue miles. The added detail can improve confidence in the ROI projections that must compete for capital investment at each fleet.

GPS asset tracking technology penetration at fleets must include trailers. Intentional pairing may be a software product addition to offerings from companies like OrbComm, Qualcomm, Geotab, VerizonConnect and others. Load matching services such as DAT Solutions also are strong candidates for housing the software for intentional pairing. Truck makers might benefit from partnering with such load board companies to flesh out the details of an operational system.
