# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The COVID-19 pandemic has illustrated the important role the trucking industry plays in bringing us critical goods. And although fuel costs have dropped due to the virus, they are still a significant part of the expense to operate a tractor-trailer in North America. Fuel costs for 2018, according to the American Transportation Research Institute (ATRI), were $0.43 per mile, accounting for 23% of a fleet's total operating costs—the second largest expense for fleets behind only driver wages. The price per gallon for diesel as of April 2020 is approximately $2.48 and all indications are that fuel prices will continue to be volatile. Thus, the industry is in need of solutions that reduce its fuel dependency if it is to stay profitable.

In addition, the United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) have enacted greenhouse gas emissions regulations on commercial vehicles extended to 2030 that require manufacturers to develop and sell technologies to improve efficiency. These factors have driven fleets, manufacturers, and others to improve the efficiency of over-the-road tractor-trailers.

Fortunately, myriad technologies that can cost-effectively improve the fuel efficiency of Class 8 trucks are readily available on the market today. Unfortunately, multiple barriers have stymied industry adoption of such technologies, including a lack of data about the true performance gains these technologies offer, and a lack of confidence in the payback for investment into these technologies. To overcome those barriers and facilitate the industry's trust in and adoption of the most promising cleaner operating technologies, NACFE produces a series of Confidence Reports.

This report highlights the results of *Run on Less Regional* (RoLR), a cross-country roadshow to raise broad industry awareness of and excitement for the opportunities created by efficient trucking, while celebrating the people, equipment, and innovations that deliver our products every day in the segment known as regional haul. Regional haul for Class 7 and 8 tractors is defined by NACFE as an operation where the truck stays within a 300-mile radius of a home base. This may include tractors that return to a home base every day or ones on a route for multiple days but that stay within that 300-mile radius.

RoLR benchmarked fuel and freight efficiency for regional haul of current production diesel and natural gas tractor technologies driven by well-trained drivers in real world operations. The 2019 demonstration followed NACFE's 2017 *Run on Less* that focused on long haul where seven tractors over a three-week period of commercial operations demonstrated that current production technology tractors in the hands of skilled drivers in real-world operations could exceed 10.1 MPG.

The goals of this report are: (a) to help the industry understand the growing segment of regional haul, (b) to demonstrate that 8.3 MPG is possible for Class 8 trucks performing regional haul (compared to the national average of 6.0 MPG), (c) to highlight the technologies and practices that make ultra-low fuel consumption in regional haul possible, and (d) to explore the opportunities of emerging technologies such as electric trucks, hydrogen, and connectivity.

## METHODOLOGIES

This report's conclusions were generated through the data collection and calculations from *Run on Less Regional*. Data was collected using multiple data collection devices and methods, and where practical, two separate methods for collecting and calculating each metric were used. All tractors were instrumented with a Geotab telematics device and an engineering data logger supplied by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory to stream data to the NACFE team. The truck drivers also sent data, such as freight unloading or loading, trailer dropping or hooking, and fuel events to NACFE via an online portal. Fuel use and distance traveled were the two most critical parameters tracked. Additionally, to supplement the MPG data, the team recorded other factors that can affect fuel efficiency, including payload weight, vehicle speed, wind, and elevation change. A NACFE analysis team completed this work with advice from multiple organizations, such as telematics companies, MPG data mining, fleets, manufacturers, and governmental and non-governmental groups.

## OBJECTIVES OF THE RUN

The main objective of all of NACFE's *Run on Less* events is to demonstrate the opportunities created by efficient trucking. NACFE knows, given the work completed to date, that there is not a single tractor or trailer specification that all fleets should employ. There are approximately 1.7 million Class 8 over-the-road tractors hauling the goods we want and need on our highways and there is a great deal of variation in the solutions employed. NACFE estimates that about 800,000 of these operate in the regional haul segment.

NACFE's 2019 thought leadership report, *More Regional Haul: An Opportunity for Trucking?*, focuses on assessing the current state and future of regional haul as a result of trends in driver retention, growth in e-commerce, the push toward immediate delivery, growth in GPS-based asset tracking systems, advances in technologies such as electric and hybrid vehicles, vehicle automation, and innovations in load matching systems.

The two biggest takeaways from the report revolve around drivers and alternate-fueled vehicles. With regional operations, drivers are more likely to get home on a regular basis, which could help the industry with its driver recruitment and retention problem.

Regional operations are also fertile ground for alternate-fueled vehicles because by their very nature they make it easier for the installation of fueling infrastructure for vehicles that use an energy source other than gasoline or diesel fuel.

RoLR included 10 fleets representing some of the best-of-the-best fleets operating in this segment of the trucking industry. Combined, they operate approximately 64,000 tractors and 190,000 trailers. NACFE wanted, very specifically, to share the results of these innovators while not making the Run a competition among them. They are all winners in our industry's efforts to improve freight efficiency.

A strict ground rule for RoLR participants was that vehicle technologies used in the demonstration had to be in production and commercially available. The intent was to demonstrate the current state of the market rather than a series of one-off prototypes that may or may not make it into the marketplace. This meant that vehicles were powered by conventional diesel or compressed natural gas (CNG) internal combustion engines. There were no battery electric, hydrogen fuel cell, or other hybrid powertrains as most current versions are prototypes. The vehicles were also variously equipped with currently available connected telematic systems.

The 10 fleets participating in RoLR included: C&S Wholesale Grocers, Hirschbach, Hogan Transportation, J.B. Hunt, Meijer, PepsiCo, Ploger Transportation, Schneider, Southeastern Freight Lines, and United Parcel Service. The vehicles were production model year 2019 and 2020 tractors, and both day cabs and sleepers. Nine of the tractors had diesel powertrains and one used CNG. Drivers were dedicated to their tractors for the duration of the demonstration. Other RoLR details included:

* Trailers were not dedicated to the tractors but varied from run to run as is typical in many regional drop-and-hook fleet operations.
* The tractors employed various levels of aerodynamics, low rolling resistance tires, powertrain improvements, and other efficiency technologies.
* The trailers were variously equipped with a range of efficiency improvement devices including skirts, low rolling resistance tires, and/or aerodynamic tail devices, per their fleet's current specifications.
* Freight loads varied from zero, termed deadheading, to maximum allowed vehicle weights.
* Estimates of real-time freight payload during the routes were made to reflect multi-stop deliveries and pick-ups.
* Routes were chosen by the fleets, spanned a variety of locations in the United States, and were based on each fleet's commercial operating demands.

## REGIONAL HAUL

During the planning, execution, and analysis of *Run on Less Regional*, NACFE learned a great deal about this segment of the North American goods movement including:

* Drivers want regional. Truck driving is a hard job and drivers favor getting home more often—daily, with an overnight weekly, every few days, or absolutely for their 34-hour reset, common after four days of driving. NACFE concludes that the industry will more easily attract and retain drivers with growth in this sector.
* As the trucks remain in a certain region, these operations are fertile ground for alternative fuels as their routes are more consistent and predictable. This increases the confidence fleets, utilities/fuel suppliers, and others have in making infrastructure investments.
* Having their equipment return to base often, in many cases even daily, can help fleets find maintenance or repair items earlier and in the correction of manufacturer-authorized field changes.
* Fuel economy seems to be a lower priority for regional haul fleets. Although not universal, delivery times and getting the equipment and driver home when needed tends to be a higher priority than the fuel used.
* The variety in routes conducted prevents fleets from taking advantage of many efficiency technologies. Fleets prefer to use a common truck specification to keep their equipment interchangeable.
* We learned that the three route classifications were insufficient to characterize their operation and suggest the following for future technology product development. (See Figure ES1.)

## RESULTS

In total, the 10 trucks traveled more than 58,000 miles during the 18-day event, recording 237 deliveries while achieving a cumulative 8.3 MPG (103 ton-miles/gallon), a 38% improvement over the average Class 8 regional haul operation, estimated by NACFE to be 6.0 MPG. (see Figure ES2.) If all regional haul fleets were to operate at this fuel efficiency, the industry would save more than $9 billion in fuel and 30.6 million tons of $CO_2$ over the course of a year.

After the *Run on Less Regional* event, a group of people from various organizations analyzed the data for interesting conclusions which are presented in the full report. Two complementary reports contain findings from separate studies conducted over the past few months. The first is *Battery Electric Powertrains for Class 8 Regional Haul Freight Based on NACFE Run-On-Less*, which analyzes how electric trucks might be employed for these duty cycles. The second is a white paper written in conjunction with Ballard titled *Fuel Cell Electric Trucks: An analysis of hybrid vehicle specifications for regional freight transport*, which does the same for hydrogen fuel cell electric trucks.

## EFFECTS OF CONDITIONS ON MPG

The team collected and showed data on the conditions and speed during RoLR in part to demonstrate that the trucks were not operated in carefully controlled settings but in the real world where wind, elevation changes, congestion, and other factors can and do impact fuel efficiency. As with the *Run on Less 2017* dataset, NACFE developed a regression model for RoLR to identify the impact various conditions and duty cycle characteristics have on MPG. However, in this case, data from the data loggers was used rather than the telematics data. The primary advantage is that the data logger collected data at a very high density, much more frequently than is typically collected by a commercial telematics device.

In RoLR, five North American Class 8 tractor brands were represented. This resulted in uneven data, with the logger able to record more than 100 different parameters from some trucks while only gathering a few dozen from one. In order to develop the model, only the most complete datasets from four trucks were used. Those trucks included four different truck models operating in different parts of North America in seven distinct duty cycles.

For the regression model, the predictors of MPG are the independent variables that were tested. They were selected to account for road and weather conditions, terrain, duty cycle characteristics, and traffic. Table 1 lists each of the independent variables and their variations that were tested in a regression as well as the expected impact on MPG. ## CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS

*Run on Less* provides insights for fleets trying to maximize the return on investment in fuel efficiency technologies, for manufacturers to improve their products exploiting benefits and mitigating consequences, and for other industry supporters, data and information to help promote these successful uses of technology and practices. Upon completion of *Run on Less Regional*, NACFE concluded:

* High efficiency requires a commitment from both fleet leadership and drivers. The 10 fleets in *Run on Less Regional* averaged 8.3 MPG with the nine diesels at 8.7 MPG.
* Big data and connectivity can be used to further optimize tractor operations for each route. Many technologies exist to improve the efficiency for these regional routes. Understanding each in depth will help in making choices to save fuel, money, and emissions.
* Fleet managers must understand and act on the variety in duty cycles so they can specify vehicles properly and coach drivers in efficient driving techniques. NACFE suggests seven duty cycles for regional haul: shuttle, dedicated, dedicated fast turn, hub and spoke, city, diminishing load, and milk runs.
* The expansion of regional haul will help attract and retain drivers as drivers get home on a more regular basis. More regional haul opens many more potential people to truck driving jobs.
* Because of its return-to-base operation, regional haul is ideal for alternative-fuel vehicles, especially battery electric trucks. Infrastructure to charge electric or alternative fuel trucks is a critical barrier to deployment. Having confidence in the location of the infrastructure to support these vehicles is important in deciding to move forward with electric trucks.

# Introduction

The North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) created the *Run on Less Regional* (RoLR) demonstration to benchmark fuel and freight efficiency for regional haul of current production diesel and natural gas tractor technologies driven by well-trained drivers in real world operations [1]. The 2019 demonstration followed NACFE's 2017 *Run on Less* that focused on long haul where seven tractors over a three-week period of commercial operations demonstrated that current production technology tractors in the hands of skilled drivers in real-world operations could exceed 10.1 MPG [2].

North American truck freight duty cycles are typically categorized as long haul, regional haul and urban. Regional haul for Class 7 and 8 tractors is defined by NACFE as an operation where the truck stays within a 300-mile radius of a home base. This may include tractors that return to a home base every day or ones on a route for multiple days but that stay within that 300-mile radius [3]. There are many permutations of possible duty cycles in this definition. Urban cycles are seeing early deployment of electric trucks as route distances are short compared to long haul routes where range challenges battery capacity and unpredictable routing complicates locating a sufficient number of high-power chargers.

Fleets continually innovate to make their operations more efficient, improve market share and reduce their costs in pursuit of improved profitability. NACFE recently identified a trend where freight movement is migrating from long haul operations to regional haul [3]. There are a number of contributory causes of this trend described in this report, but fundamentally, freight hauling requires the driver, the tractor, the trailer, the load and the route.

The driver is clearly one of the critical components to improving freight efficiency, often considered to be responsible for as much as 30% to 35% improvement [4][5]. The RoLR drivers, shown in Figure 1, were selected by their participating fleets as drivers representing a cross section of ages, genders and experience.

## A Demonstration, not a Competition

As described above, the main objective of all of NACFE's *Run on Less* events is to demonstrate the opportunities created by efficient trucking. NACFE knows, given the work completed to date, that there is not a single tractor or trailer specification that all fleets should employ. The business of trucking is simply too diverse for a one-size-fits-all solution. Therefore, the group expected a diverse set of the technologies to be included by the various fleets participating in the Run. More will be discussed later, on the similar and dissimilar solutions used by the participants.

There are approximately 1.7 million Class 8 over-the-road tractors hauling the goods we want and need on our highways and there is a great deal of variation in the solutions employed. NACFE estimates that about 800,000 of these operate in the regional haul segment. This group of 10 fleets represent some of the best-of-the-best fleets operating in this segment. NACFE wanted, very specifically, to share the results of these innovators while not making the Run a competition among them. They are all winners in our industry's efforts in improving freight efficiency. The group also received strong industry feedback to stay focused on the team rather than the performance of each tractor and trailer or the combination of solutions employed. Therefore, most of the results, for example average speed, route, payload, number of stops, were shared for each fleet but the fuel efficiency in miles per gallon or ton-miles per gallon were only shared as an average.

## Duty Cycles Within the Regional Haul Segment

An important early step in developing a product for a given market is to thoroughly understand the ways that product will be used. In truck development, duty cycles are commonly described as how the trucks are used over time. Often stated as the number of miles traveled, dwell times during stops, vehicle speed, etc. In NACFE's thought leadership paper, *Regional Haul; An Opportunity for Trucking* [3], the variety of duty cycles in this segment was discussed in general and a goal of *Run on Less Regional* was to more deeply understand duty cycles. There are no universal definitions for regional haul so, NACFE began to take on this task.

First, it seems obvious that regional haul would be defined such that the equipment and the driver would remain in a certain region. But how big would that area be? After discussions with many in the industry, the primary reason for a region definition in this context had most to do with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's Hours of Service Rules. Current rules allow a driver to drive for 11 hours before needing to take a 10-hour rest and given average vehicle speeds, a driver could go out about 275 miles and still return home. NACFE chose a radius from base of 300 miles for a definition of regional haul.

With this base requirement, the group continued investigating duty cycles that existed in regional haul and after selection and discussions with the RoLR participating fleets, determined that there were three major categories of duty cycles described as follows.

* A-B-A where the truck goes out to the same location and returns to base, doing this once or several times in a driving shift.
* Hub-and-spoke which is like A-B-A, but has different B locations over a day, week or month.
* And finally, A-B-C-D-A where there are multiple pick up and deliveries over the course of the route with the vehicle still returning to the same base.

* Payload of course will vary from heavy (beverages) to light (snacks) to empty
* And likely others to be determined. NACFE believes this to be an important area for definition now and in the future.

## What NACFE Learned about Regional Haul

During the planning, execution and analysis of *Run on Less Regional*, NACFE learned a great deal about this segment of the North American goods movement. The following is suggested.

* Drivers want regional. Truck driving is a hard job and these 10 drivers shared how they favor getting home more often; daily, with an overnight weekly, every few days, or absolutely for their 34-hour reset,

<!-- page: 28 -->
common after four days of driving. NACFE concludes that the industry will more easily attract and retain drivers with growth in this sector.
* As the trucks remain in a certain region, these operations are fertile ground for alternative fuels as their routes are more consistent and predictable. This increases the confidence fleets, utilities/fuel suppliers and others have in making infrastructure investment.
* Many fleets shared the advantage in maintenance and repair of having their equipment return to base often, in many cases even daily. This can help find maintenance or repair items earlier and in the correction of manufacturer authorized field changes.
* Fuel economy seems to be a lower priority for regional haul fleets. Although not universal, NACFE recognized that delivery times and getting the equipment and driver home when needed tended to be higher priority than the amount of fuel used. NCACFE believes this to be a significant area of opportunity to save fuel and create less emissions.
* The variety in routes conducted prevents fleets from taking advantage of many efficiency technologies. Fleets prefer to use a common truck specification to keep their equipment interchangeable.

### Run on Less Fleets and Operation

During April and May 2019, NACFE asked fleets to nominate themselves if they were interested in participating in the Run and selected 10 fleets to participate. The fleets then chose the tractor and trailer technologies, the specific driver, the routes and loads they would haul. This was important because NACFE wanted the fleets to deploy equipment with features they believed provided acceptable payback on their investment as well as improved the fuel efficiency of their fleet.

The requirements for the equipment used in the Run were as follows.

* 2017 Model Year tractors or newer. This would have all the tractors be Greenhouse Gas Phase 1 compliant.
* Knowing that some fleets would likely be dropping and hooking different trailers, as they do normally, there were no specific requirements for the age of the trailers.
* The technologies employed must be commercially available, defined as being available from the tractor or trailer manufacturer or easily added to the equipment as a retrofit. Examples of easily added technologies would be the addition of aerodynamic features, auxiliary power units for HVAC, engine parameter settings, etc.
* In a few cases, NACFE mediated decisions by a fleet as to the commercial availability of a feature. Innovative fleets are constantly testing emerging technologies, which may not be commercially available.

One aspect that came out from driver interviews was how early many of these drivers wake up to start their day. ### Payback

Fleets select efficiency technologies given their total cost of ownership or payback on the upfront investment as they purchase new equipment. It is generally very challenging to understand all of the elements of the total cost of ownership of a particular technology. Such items as purchase cost, fuel efficiency performance and maintenance impact are at times difficult, but often easier than other cost elements such as driver attraction and retention, negative or positive affect on other technologies, resale value, etc. Each Confidence Report prepared by NACFE [12] addresses the total cost of the benefits and challenges of adoption, but individually fleets must evaluate the actual costs of all of these elements for their own duty cycles and business models.

<!-- page: 38 -->
For an overall perspective, at $3 per gallon for diesel fuel, the RoLR fleets averaged 8.7 MPG and driving 90,000 miles per year, would spend about $31,000 on fuel per year. Using an average of 6.5 MPG (as an estimate for a truck without the technologies) a "typical" truck would spend $41,500 on fuel. This equates to a $10,500 savings in fuel cost per year. Given the performance of the RoLR drivers and that it might be unrealistic to assume all drivers will perform at this level the savings might be reduced to $8,000. The NACFE team estimates the up-front costs to get to this 10+ MPG level to be about $20,000. This then provides about a 2.5-year payback at the $3 per gallon fuel price.

Obviously, this is a very high-level estimate of payback for these combinations of technologies. Each fleet must complete their own analysis to predict their specific return on investments.

## CNG and Diesel

As discussed previously, the UPS truck in RoLR was a CNG-powered truck, making some comparisons in efficiency difficult. CNG is generally measured by volume at a standard pressure and temperature. When reporting fuel efficiency, this value is often converted to a diesel gallon equivalent based on energy content in an equal mass of natural gas and diesel. However, even with this unit conversion, CNG-fueled trucks are difficult to compare to diesel fueled trucks on an MPG-to-MPG basis. For a number of reasons, the diesel engines on the road today operate at a higher thermal efficiency than CNG-fueled engines resulting in lower MPG for CNG-powered trucks. This was the case with the UPS truck. Analysis of the data indicates that its MPG was 2.5 to 2.7 MPG lower than the diesel-powered trucks in RoLR after other conditions such as weight, terrain and weather are accounted for. In discussions with other fleets not participating in RoLR but experienced with CNG trucks in their own fleets, the UPS, CNG powered tractor's performance was very impressive. According to one fleet executive, "We would give our right arm for MPGs like that in our CNG fleet."

## Stops and Deliveries

In addition to the radius of operations, one of the aspects that differentiates regional haul from traditional line haul is the number of stops a truck may make on a given route. NACFE attempted to capture this metric through driver input which resulted in the metrics shown on the website. For the purpose of this report and the data analysis, NACFE has re-evaluated this metric to include extended stops (stops where the truck came to a full stop and did not exceed 25 MPH again for at least 10 minutes) for several reasons.

* An extended stop affects MPG, distance traveled and other metrics similarly, regardless of whether a trailer is being unloaded, fuel is being added, or a driver is simply taking a break.
* Multiple stops in a day are much more prevalent in regional haul than in line haul. For some fleets, drivers may drive the same route day after day and know it well enough to plan a rest or fuel stop at the same location. Some fleets have on-site fueling capability or arrangements with specific fuel stations. Other fleets may work hard to fill back-hauls, requiring drivers to make at least one additional stop to pick up freight, a stop that would not have counted as a "delivery" in the website metric.
* Human error, misunderstanding or missed entries may have caused some deliveries not to be recorded on the website while other drops (such as an unloading and subsequent drop of the empty trailer) were double counted.

$\varphi$ = difference between wind direction and truck bearing

## Average Performance of the North American Fleet

North American trucking is complex, with a large variety of duty cycles. There are no average trucks. The use of national averages can be misleading. For example, a truck that is highly utilized may deadhead without cargo less than 10% of the time, while a competitor on a dedicated route may deadhead 50% of the time in order to attain their next load. The average fuel economy of the highly utilized truck will be significantly less than the dedicated one, all other factors being equal. The average of these two trucks would not accurately reflect either operation. The use of national averages for vehicles is an analytical simplification, but the spread of real-world duty cycles around those averages should be understood to be very broad.

As the *Run on Less Regional* team developed the reporting protocol for the program, they considered various options to allow comparing the performance of the Run participants to the current performance of the national fleet. One option was to include various trucks in the Run that would have the range of MPGs operating on the road today. This would have required including for instance, a 10-year-old truck with little to no technologies to a 2019 model with nearly all technologies and probably some number of others in between. This could have determined a baseline for the national average. This option would have required significant resources and funds to execute and was deemed not an option.

Several other scenarios were considered with the selected approach to calculate the best estimate of the average fuel efficiency of the Class 8 regional haul fleet nationally.

For 2017 the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) reported that the total miles driven, and gallons consumed by U.S. combination trucks (tractor-trailers) were 181,490,000,000 miles and 30,363,561,000 gallons [8]. This calculates an average U.S. fuel efficiency of Class 8 tractor-trailers as 5.98 MPG. FHWA publishes this data obtained via the International Fuel Tax Agreement (IFTA) and combination trucks include all tractor-trailers which consume fuel and travel our roads. This approximately 6.0 MPG is the generally referenced number used by most organizations in defining the current efficiency of the U.S. tractor-trailer fleet.

NACFE therefore has used this statistic in its Annual Fleet Fuel Study (AFFS) [15] for comparing the AFFS fleets' performance to the national average, as its source is actual mileage traveled and fuel consumed by the U.S. fleet. Figure 24 shows the 16-year comparison of the fleet-wide average of the 21 fleets that participated in the 2019 AFFS to the all-in national MPG data from FHWA. The FHWA data includes tractors in over-the-road goods movement, pulling 53' and 28' dry van and refrigerated trailers as well as other types of trailers, typically referred to as vocational. This includes flatbed, tanker, dump, waste transfer, etc. which have a mix of over-the-road and off-road routes. These trucks are included in the miles and gallons of the FHWA data but are clearly outside of the scope of the *Run on Less* duty cycle.

## Making Sense of Run on Less Regional

A select group of RoLR participating sponsors, fleets and engaged parties met in January 2020 to review the data collected from RoLR, to discuss lessons learned and recommend future improvements in conducting *Run on Less* events. NACFE supplied all 10 attending organizations with detailed datasets from the Run and a list of questions the group wanted answered. Each company then provided analysis for the entire group at the event. The event followed Chatham House rules, to permit an open and frank discussion of the data with no attribution to attendees, their organizations, or the products they provide to the industry.

A consistent conclusion from the attendees is that there are a significant number of regional haul duty cycles, and that ROLR represents only a sampling. Even within these 10 ROLR cycles, there was variety, with at least three types of routes – A-B-A, hub-and-spoke and A-B-C-D-A. Yet irrespective of the routes, all 10 trucks averaged over 350 miles per day, nine averaged over 390 miles per day, and five more than 430 miles per day. The common denominator for these routes appears to be the hours of service of the driver.

In long haul on-highway operations, an 11-hour day averaging 55 mph is approximately 600 miles. In RoL 2017, seven drivers accumulated 50,107 miles in 99 driving days equating to 506 miles on average per day. Their speed averaged 54 mph. Since the 2019 RoLR event focused on regional duty cycles, average miles per day were lower accounted for by the fact that many of the trucks made multiple stops during the day. The drivers may operate with fewer miles per day, but it is balanced by the benefit of returning home every day.

<!-- page: 51 -->
The RoLR review team determined that definitions were needed to clarify duty cycles, especially when using them in context of evaluating alternative technologies like battery electric or fuel cell electric vehicles. A common statement seen in the media is that most runs are 100 miles or less, making them suitable for battery electric vehicles. The prototype trucks being tested in Los Angeles and Long Beach port drayage use are examples of these routes. What is not often stated in this media coverage is that the 100 miles refers to one trip only, and that these trucks may do three or four trips per driver shift, accumulating 300 or 400 miles per shift. Further, they rarely mention that these vehicles are often slip-seated, meaning they are operated in multiple shifts per day, bringing the total daily mileage to 600 to 800 miles for double shift operations, and possibly 900 to 1200 miles per day on triple shift operations. The RoLR review team decided that statements of trip range need to be in context of miles per day for the tractor and in context of miles per shift for the driver, to avoid artificially biasing the information when discussing battery electric or fuel cell electric ranges.

The fleet of 10 duty cycles also revealed the complexity of back hauling. A review of annual financial reports from publicly traded fleets from the SEC Edgar database shows that empty backhauls, commonly termed deadheading, perhaps occur 13% of miles traveled [19]. Interviews with public and private fleets by NACFE have highlighted that there is a significant variance to that, and it's not uncommon for numbers to be as high as 50% deadheading in some fleets, and others have nearly zero deadheading.

One fleet in RoLR operates as a private delivery fleet on outbound legs of its routes, then operate essentially as a for-hire fleet on the return legs, arranging for loads so that both outbound and inbound legs are loaded. The variability in planning routes for the return trip led to one day's total miles exceeding 800 miles when a return load took the vehicle on an extended delivery trip. The key point here is that the driver and truck had to be both flexible and robust enough to take a light load out on the initial delivery, then a heavy load on an unusually long return route. The diesel truck could do both routes and loads in the same day with only one fueling stop. Another aspect of this duty cycle was that these trucks were then available for use in second shift operations by another driver, increasing the miles per day and reducing dwell time availability at the depot.

One of the routes the RoLR review team highlighted was a very consistent trip length each day, averaging 545 miles with a standard deviation of only 12 miles. This A-B-A route was meeting up with a second A-B-A route truck and swapping trailers at the turn point, in a pony express type operation allowing both drivers to be home each night but moving the trailers across the country. The route was predictable, consistent other than load weight, which might vary from light to heavy, and both legs were loaded trailers for both drivers.

A second interesting route was again an A-B-A route averaging 397 miles per day, but in predictable, consistent legs with consistent heavy loads (Figure 27). The truck drove 70 miles out, spent less than 18 minutes doing a drop and hook and then drove 70 miles back with an unloaded trailer, repeating a drop and hook, and then repeating the A-B-A route again. Each leg of the trip was only 70 miles, but each round trip was approximately 140 miles, and each day's net mileage was approximately 400 miles. The truck had only minutes of dwell time at each stop. Another key aspect of this duty cycle was that the truck went home with the driver at the end of the shift, being stored at his residence overnight. This route, while appealing for electrification due to the 70-mile leg length, is extremely challenging for electrification due to the minimal dwell times at stops during the shift, and the fact the truck is at the driver's home overnight, rather than the depot.

A third duty cycle involved grocery delivery to multiple sites during each week. The vehicle and driver averaged 407 miles each day, with a standard deviation of 78 miles. Trailers typically started heavy and ended empty. Weather was another interesting factor, with temperatures starting in the mornings in the low 30°F range and ending the days in the 80s or 90s°F. Temperature variables are key factors in fuel economy and for battery and fuel cell operations.

One analysis from fleet data presented to the RoLR review team indicated that newer tractors are less prone to seasonal temperature sensitivity on fuel economy, concluding that improved aerodynamics tends to lessen the effect of temperature changes on MPG. The data also suggested that maintenance costs of aero devices need to be factored in as they can be significant.

The RoLR review team concurred with NACFE's plan to deep dive the data sets with NREL and Ballard Power Systems to evaluate the potential for battery electric and fuel cell electric vehicles to replace the diesel units in RoLR. NACFE co-authored a paper with NREL on battery electric vehicles titled *Battery Electric Powertrains for Class 8 Regional Haul Freight Based on NACFE Run-On-Less* by Rick Mihelic and Andrew Kotz for the EVS33 conference originally scheduled for June 2020 and will be formally published by EVS33 in the fall of 2020 [20]. NACFE also co-authored a paper with Ballard Power Systems on fuel cell electric vehicles for the RoLR routes titled *Fuel Cell Electric Trucks for Freight Transport: An analysis of hybrid vehicle specifications*. Both reports will be available on NACFE's website in mid-June 2020 [22].

The data loggers accumulated data at 1 sec (1 Hz) intervals from the trucks. In some cases, over 700 channels were recorded per truck over all days of the trip. The RoLR team found that different manufacturers had different channels for information, or different ways of interpreting data from sensors. In some cases, many fewer channels were recorded. This lack of true commonality between OEMs made subsequent analysis more time consuming and open to error in comparing results between vehicles. The RoLR team recommends that the industry work to better standardize recording. While SAE J1939 is a foundation for all fleets, there is still variation in how each OEM uses channels on the bus.

While NREL provided data loggers recorded data at 1 Hz frequency, the commercial loggers record in longer intervals, reducing the amount of data collected. There are practical reasons for this, using averaging and algorithms to monitor for changes in settings allows the data files to be significantly smaller in size for transmission to back office applications or for storing in devices on-board the vehicles. The RoLR review team felt that the 1 Hz data was extremely valuable for evaluating the details of the Run, and the database likely can be mined for future uses. The commercial data loggers also are valuable allowing access to large quantities of vehicles in operation and providing reasonably accurate performance assessment. Care should be taken though when using the commercial data logger information for evaluating details of engine performance as the averaging can hide significant factors.

<!-- page: 53 -->
The RoLR review team concluded that aerodynamic devices for regional haul do improve performance. The significant percentage of time at higher speeds by these 10 RoLR trucks rationalizes that investment in aerodynamic devices and will result in performance improvement. The RoLR trucks were both day cabs and sleeper cab configurations. Some had refrigerated trailers. They all had aerodynamic tractors with roof transitions or fairings matching trailer height. Some day cabs had chassis fairings. All trailers had skirts, even the doubles. Gap closure devices were recommended.

Fleet experience data was presented indicating that sometimes technology can negatively impact fuel economy. An example was automated transmissions which make it easy to request maximum acceleration every time the truck launches. The fleet stated even good drivers are more frequently requesting full throttle on left turns compared to right turns.

The use of cruise controls in traffic is also a challenge for fleets. While cruise control use in general should result in an improvement in consistency in driving, traffic conditions can cause extra deceleration and acceleration events, and drivers may manually override the systems negating any benefits.

While MPG is a typical metric, there are others such as freight-ton-efficiency (FTE) and brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC). Discussions by fleets with NACFE is that MPG is a default metric for performance evaluation. FTE is less frequently used. One attendee demonstrated how an estimate of BSFC from CAN data may be a useful tool for evaluating the efficiency of a powertrain in a duty cycle. While BSFC normally requires direct measurement of fuel mass flow and engine torques/speeds, the presenter indicated that CAN data can be used to approximate values but cautioned that CAN data on fuel economy may include significant tolerance versus true measurements of fuel use.

## Effect of Conditions on MPG

### Modeling

As with the RoL 2017 dataset, NACFE developed a regression model for RoLR to identify the impact various conditions and duty cycle characteristics have on MPG. However, in this case data from the data loggers was used rather than the telematics data. The primary advantage is that the data logger collected data at a very high density, much more frequently than is typically collected by a commercial telematics device. When modeling for *Run on Less*, data from the telematics device was not collected at regular intervals, requiring a large amount of interpolation that may have affected the results. Additionally, since the data logger collected all public CAN data, many more parameters were available. However, this also became the chief disadvantage of the data logger dataset. While the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) publishes standards for a large number of parameters, many are not required, and manufacturers choose which are actually populated and broadcast. Finally, manufacturers also adopt different standards for the rate at which data is broadcast and even the connector that is used which caused some difficulty in reading and storing data from trucks. In RoLR, five North American Class 8 tractor brands were represented. This resulted in uneven data, with the logger able to record over 100 different parameters from some trucks while only gathering a few dozen from one. In order to develop the model, only the most complete datasets from four trucks were used. Within the four trucks are four different truck models operating in different parts of North America in four distinct duty cycles.

### Data Processing and Variables

In order for the data to be useful in a regression model, it had to be consolidated into regular intervals. NREL processed the raw data for NACFE into human readable form at one second intervals in a comma separated format. Further data processing was therefore not required by NACFE. NACFE only consolidated this data into various time intervals and combined it with wind and temperature data, collected at 10-minute intervals, to create datasets used for regression modeling.

### Predictors (Independent Variables)

For the regression model, the predictors of MPG are the independent variables that were tested. They were selected to account for road and weather conditions, terrain, duty cycle characteristics, and traffic. For most of the variables, there is sense for how each will affect fuel economy. For example, it is expected that a heavier load on a truck will reduce its MPG or that a tailwind will improve it. In most cases, the question to be answered by the regression model is how much each variable changes MPG, not the direction in which it is affected. However, for some variables, there is the possibility of an optimal point. Average speed and temperature may be two variables for which an optimum point exists. In the case of average speed, as the truck increases speed, the aerodynamic drag the engine must offset increases, thereby reducing fuel efficiency. However, the engine and driveline do not operate at the same efficiency across the speed range and likely have an optimal speed where efficiency is maximized. Going above or below that optimal speed may reduce a truck's fuel efficiency. Similarly, temperature can affect MPG in different ways as well. As temperature increases, air density decreases reducing the aerodynamic drag on the truck. However, as temperatures rise, the truck has to work harder to keep the engine and driver cooled which consumes more power and will reduce efficiency. Therefore, for average speed and temperature, variables where there may be an optimal point above and below which MPG should decrease, variations of those variables around optimal points were tested. Each of the independent variables and their variations that were tested in a regression as well as the expected impact on MPG are listed in Table 9.

When building a regression model, there are several indications as to whether a variable and the resulting model is valid. One indication is discussed above: the direction of the relationship should make sense. Another is the p-value. The p-value is the probability that the variable is irrelevant to the outcome. In other words, if the regression model shows that every 1 MPH change in wind speed increases MPG by 0.1 but the p-value is 0.5, this means that in the data, there is a 50% chance wind has no impact on MPG and that the relationship is a coincidence. Typically, a p-value of less than 0.05 is desired indicating that there is a 95% or greater probability that the variable does impact MPG. A further indication of the usefulness of a variable is the impact it has on R squared ($R^2$), known as the coefficient of determination. In simple terms, the $R^2$ of a regression model shows how much the independent variables explain changes in the dependent variable (MPG in our case). If the model shows that gross weight, wind speed, and elevation change (the independent variables) all have p-values of less than 0.05 and the regression model has an $R^2$ of 10%, it indicates that all of the independent variables are valid but their combined impact on MPG is relatively small compared to some other unknown variable (perhaps the driver as an example). Even if the whole model only explains a small portion of MPG, the coefficients are valid as long as the p-value for each coefficient is below the threshold. One more critical test for independent variables is that they are not significantly correlated with each other. In other words, if a change in the value of one independent variable is closely tied to a change in one of the other independent variables, the model will have difficulty discerning the impact from each variable. A test for correlation will be discussed later in this section.

Finally, a model is easiest to understand and less likely to be influenced by the specific dataset used to develop it if it explains as much of the dependent variable with the least number of independent variables. Therefore, if the addition of an independent variable, even one with a p-value less than 0.05 does not increase the $R^2$ of a model very much, the model may be more useful if it is left out. In this case, since there are many possible independent variables for MPG, some may be left out in the final model for this reason.

### Regression Results

The data collected from RoLR proved to be very useful in developing a model that explained a large amount of the variation seen in MPGs both day to day and across different vehicles. Several models were developed, one using data in hourly intervals and a second one using 30-minute intervals. The model using the hourly data had an $R^2$ of 78%, meaning that the predictors explained 78% of the hourly variation in MPG while the model built using the 30-minute intervals had a $R^2$ of 72%. Where a predictor appeared in both models it had a similar coefficient, meaning that its impact was consistent in the two models.

### Average Weight

As expected, average weight is a significant predictor of MPG. The impact was in the range of 0.24 to 0.33 MPG per 1000 lbs. of weight. This value is smaller than was found in the RoL 2017 (0.47 MPG per 1000 lbs.) but is closer to several other studies performed by SAE and other parties. One explanation for the difference may be that the method for determining vehicle weight was different. Another may be more related to the particular data used to develop the models. Regional haul trucks, due to the nature of their operation may haul more consistent loads day to day, resulting in less variation in the data that may affect the model.

### Speed Variation from 54 MPH

The impact of average speed was tested several different ways during modeling. Vehicles were operated very differently, with some used on the same route consisting of interstate highway travel while others made multiple stops and traveled a mix of interstates and rural roadways. For modeling purposes, the speed-related factor that produced the best results was when speed variation to 54 MPH was used indicating that it was equally detrimental to MPG to travel much above or below that speed. The model using 60-minute data and 30-minute data gave consistent results, indicating that a variation in average speed of 5 MPH resulted in a 0.6 MPG decrease. Average speed over 30 or 60 minutes is a function of several factors including the route, traffic, road type and the driver. Traveling 54 MPH when conditions and limits allow for higher speeds is for most drivers and fleets not realistic. Particularly on regional routes where a driver needs to complete a route within the allowed on-duty hours of service, productivity can easily outweigh MPG when it comes to speed.

### Headwind and Crosswind

Another condition for which both models produced consistent results is for wind both in the direction of travel and for crosswinds. The wind component in the direction of travel affected MPG by 0.05 to 0.06 MPG per one MPH wind speed. Interestingly, both models also indicate that crosswinds are just as damaging to fuel economy as a headwind, reducing MPG by 0.06 to 0.07 MPG per one MPH of crosswind. One of the vehicles in the dataset made use of a gap closure device. When that vehicle was analyzed separately for the impact of wind on MPG versus the other vehicles, the model indicated that wind in the direction of travel had a smaller effect on MPG by 0.01 MPG per MPH. This is not a conclusive analysis on the effect of gap closure devices as the dataset is comparing different tractor and trailer makes operating in completely different environments. However, it does point to a potential analysis method for understanding the impact of an aerodynamic device in real-world conditions.

### Elevation Change

Elevation change is as expected also a large predictor of MPG adding about 10% to the $R^2$ to each model. The impact of a change in elevation is a reduction in MPG in the range of 0.14 to 0.18 MPG per 100 'of elevation gain though the models were a little inconsistent on the impact of an elevation decrease. It would be reasonable to expect that the impact would be similar, though at a slightly lower magnitude (i.e. the MPG lost going uphill is not completely gained back on the return downhill). One model indicated that this was the case while the other did not.

### Time in Top Gear and Gear Changes

Considerable effort was made to remove both of these factors from the MPG models since both are often indicators of other factors including speed, elevation change, road type and perhaps more. However, time in top gear increased $R^2$ by over 9% even with speed and elevation in the one-hour interval model while the number of gear changes had a similar effect in the model using the 30-minute interval data (slightly over 7%). As expected, the two predictors are highly correlated and were not used in the same model. Both predictors therefore appear to inform their respective models on factors not captured by elevation change, speed or the

<!-- page: 57 -->
number of stops. In a time before automated manual transmissions (AMT) were the norm, time in top gear was a metric fleets have watched more closely. All of the trucks in RoLR were equipped with AMTs and the drivers that operated them are very skillful at achieving high MPGs. It may be that these two predictors are indicative of the duty cycle and the type of roads on which regional haul trucks are often operating.

### Stops

The number of stops in a day is one of the main differentiators between regional haul and typical line haul operation. In both models, the number of long stops (defined as a truck coming to a complete stop and not exceeding 25 MPH for at least 15 minutes) had a negative effect on MPG, though the impact was different. In the model based on one-hour data, every stop reduced MPG by 0.19 MPG while in the model based on 30-minute data, the effect was 0.66 MPG per stop. The fact that a stop has a larger effect on MPG in the 30-minute model is to be expected for 15 minutes (or possibly longer) is a significant portion of a 30-minute interval. As shown earlier several trucks stopped for longer periods 10 times or more nearly every day. In the course of an 11-hour driving day, this factor alone would reduce MPG by nearly 0.2 MPG versus a truck in line haul operation.

### Temperature and Barometric Pressure

Ambient temperature and barometric pressure had relatively small impacts on the overall MPG models. Temperature was tested several ways including linearly or as a quadratic factor. Temperature and barometric pressure were also combined into an air density factor (using a dry air assumption). It is commonly found that MPG increases as temperature rises since the density of air decreases giving the truck lighter air to push through. Both models confirmed that this was the case in RoLR. Given the fact that RoLR was conducted over 18 days and since the trucks stayed in a particular home region, none of trucks was exposed to long operations in consistent hot and cold conditions, making this predictor less important in the overall MPG models.

### Following Distance

Following distance to other traffic was also a small though somewhat surprising factor in both models. It was not clear what effect traffic would have on MPG but generally it was assumed that the effect on MPG would be negative. However, both models show that time spent following a vehicle at a gap of 100 feet or less improves MPG. The models are inconsistent on the impact (0.03 MPG per minute in the one-hour model, 0.07 MPG per minute in the 30-minute model) but the fact that they both show a small improvement may be indicative of a platooning effect even at a distance of 100'. A similar effect was not observed when looking at distances of 100' to 300'.

As with many studies, it is important to keep results in context to the data from which it is derived. This data was collected over a relatively short period from trucks selected for high fuel efficiency. In an ideal setting, comparable data from "average" trucks or data from these same trucks operated by other drivers in different regions and seasons would be compared to the findings here. While that may be useful from an analytical standpoint, it is not the goal of RoL. But it is hoped that results from this analysis, the RoL 2017 analysis as well as many other studies in the public domain will build on the body of knowledge with respect to the effect various conditions have on fuel efficiency.

## Questions, Feedback, and Comments

Throughout the event and since its conclusion, some recurring questions have been asked regarding *Run on Less Regional* by observers and even by some of the participants.

Q: How well do these 10 fleet operations represent regional haul in total?
A: *There is no exact definition of regional haul tractors. NACFE defined it as operations where the trucks return to base on a somewhat frequent basis and remain within 300 miles of base. For this report, NACFE expanded the number of duty cycles from three to seven and recognized that these 10 trucks generally ran longer distances than the average regional haul daily routes. But, most drivers are still compensated by the mile and fleets want high asset utilization, so fleets are encouraged to keep the equipment running.*

Q: Why did you not identify the MPG results by named fleet?
A: *Each fleet operated in significantly different duty cycles with various technologies deployed. Also, the terrain, temperature, wind, elevation, etc. all play a role in achieving high MPGs and they varied significantly. Run on Less Regional was a fuel efficiency roadshow, not a race or a competition, designed to better understand regional haul and provide a benchmark average fuel efficiency. All 10 participants achieved 8.3 MPG with 8.7 the average for the nine diesels. We'd like to think all the drivers and fleets that participated and the industry as a whole are winners as a result of our efforts.*

Q: Will you do another Run?

## Conclusions

*Run on Less* provides insights for fleets trying to maximize the return on investment in fuel efficiency technologies, for manufacturers to improve their products exploiting benefits and mitigating consequences and for other industry supporters, data and information to help promote these successful uses of technology and practices. Upon completion of this *Run on Less Regional*, NACFE sought to better understand the data and information gathered during this event. The team reached the following conclusions.

### High efficiency requires a commitment
* High efficiency requires a commitment from both fleet leadership and drivers. The 10 fleets in *Run on Less Regional* averaged 8.3 MPG with the nine diesels at 8.7.

### Optimize performance using big data and connectivity
* Big data and connectivity can be used to further optimize tractor operations for each route. Many technologies exist to improve the efficiency for these regional routes. Understanding each deeply will help in making choices to save fuel, money and emissions.

### Understand and act on the variety in duty cycles
* Diversity in duty cycles makes it essential that fleet managers understand the nuances of their business so they can spec vehicles properly and coach drivers in efficient driving techniques. NACFE suggests seven duty cycles for regional haul: shuttle, dedicated, dedicated fast turn, hub-and-spoke, city, diminishing load and milk runs.

<!-- page: 59 -->
### Drivers are attracted to regional haul
* The expansion of regional haul will help attract and retain drivers as drivers get home on a more regular basis. More regional haul opens many more potential people to truck driving jobs.

### Return to base is ideal for electric trucks
* Because of its return-to-base operation, regional haul is ideal for alternative-fuel vehicles, especially battery electric trucks. Infrastructure to charge electric trucks or fuel alternative-fuel ones is a critical barrier to deployment. Having confidence in the location of the infrastructure to support these vehicles is important in deciding to move forward with electric trucks.
