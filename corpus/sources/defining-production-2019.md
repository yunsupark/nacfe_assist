The term “production” is not strictly defined and can mean different things to different OEMs, customers, regulators, media and the public as illustrated in Figure 1. Prior to this decade, changes in the heavy truck OEM club were extremely rare. Brand marks like Diamond REO, White, Sterling (formerly Ford Louisville), Marmon and LaFrance were absorbed or changed ownership before disappearing from the North American marketplace. Other were absorbed by parent corporations as with Daimler’s purchase of Freightliner or rebranded such as when International Corporation became Navistar. Entirely new OEMs were largely non-existent between 1950 and 2000.

The past decade has seen a significant increase in the number of North American OEMs with recent start-ups such as Tesla, Nikola and XOS (formerly Thor Trucks). Older smaller volume nameplates are getting greater media attention and investment such as Lion and BYD. Tier 1 suppliers such as Ballard Power and Cummins have helped build prototypes. These are all now competing with the well-established big brands such as Freightliner and Western Star (Daimler Trucks North America), Volvo and Mack (Volvo Group), Kenworth and Peterbilt (PACCAR), International (Navistar) and smaller nameplates like Autocar and Oshkosh.

The clash of old versus new companies is clouded by hype and hyperbole, and intense competition for billions of dollars in venture capital. Key to these investments is when does the cash flow turn positive? When are products actually being sold and delivered?

<!-- page: 7 -->
Unlike cars, trucks are capital investments. They are tools for making money primarily through hauling materials between locations, whether cement, refuse, logs, freight, bulk materials, bottled products, frozen or fresh fruits and vegetables, dairy products, etc.

These vehicles share the road with automobiles and pedestrians. As a society we demand, through regulatory and market forces, that they meet or exceed safety and performance standards.

However, there are differences between the depths of product validation before the vehicles enter the market, essentially the level of risk each OEM is willing to assume when announcing their first vehicles have been sold.

Definitions we have witnessed for being “In Production”.
* 1st vehicle off low rate production tooling, limited option content available
* 1st vehicle off high rate production tooling, limited option content available
* 1st vehicle off high rate production tooling, robust option content available
* 1st commercially sold vehicle but with limited trained field support and limited parts availability
* 1st commercially sold vehicle with substantial trained field support and robust parts availability
* Low rate production line at factory, factory ramping assembly training and supply chain
* Full rate production line at factory, factory staff trained, supply chain fully operational
* 1st commercially leased vehicle, with intent to retrieve and replace vehicle with later version
* 1st commercially leased vehicle with full service life
* 1st vehicle to be ordered via the production ordering process
* 1st vehicle but requiring frequent software updates and recalls to service centers
* 1st vehicle with stable software configuration and no recalls
* 1st vehicle in customer commercial use, low uptime
* 1st vehicle in customer commercial use, high uptime

Established companies have evolved significant organizational experience in launching new products, regularly incorporating lessons learned. NACFE offers a framework for discussing the term production in the context of commercial truck manufacturing in North America with historical examples from a variety of perspectives.

Four key conclusions were reached by the study team.
1. Many definitions exist for a statement by truck builders that they are in production causing confusion with stakeholders.
2. Product development is an investment in a new product that reduces collective risk over time through testing and decision making.
3. There are dramatically differing situations for established versus new OEM entrants, including such items as established development processes, revenue needs, variation of product offerings and risk acceptance levels.
4. Fleet expectations for cost of operation, performance, reliability and durability, etc. will not change and in fact be more demanding.

# INTRODUCTION

We’ve all heard or read that a particular new technology truck will be “in production” at some future date. NACFE has found that “production” has a range of interpretations. We’ve identified clear disconnects in planning by utilities, regulators, OEMs and fleets from differing interpretations. Media, advertisers, non-government organizations (NGOs), investment researchers, public bloggers and others compound the confusion surrounding this term.

NACFE’s purpose for this report is to provide context and clarity in use of the term “production” at this critical inflection point in truck technology innovation, where multiple alternative drivetrains are entering public use, where significant investment in time, money and planning is occurring by parties that largely have not worked together before. This is not intended as a Project Management guide, rather it is insights from NACFE’s authors’ perspective including over a century of combined OEM experience.

# SCOPE

The scope of this report is on Class 3 through 8 trucks and tractors, characterized by vehicle weights above 10,000 lbs. and purpose built for moving freight. Automotive use of the term “in production” while similar, may differ.

# REALITIES OF TIME TO MARKET

## 8.1 BACKGROUND

A common statement is that start-up companies have an ability to launch new products into the marketplace at a much faster pace than established, older companies. The metric of choice is “time to market” which the Cambridge Dictionary somewhat oversimplifies as “the amount of time it takes to design and manufacture a product before it is available to buy [1].” Every product, whether a candy bar or a Class 8 tractor, has to meet some level of requirements before it can be sold [2][3][4]. It is relatively easy to create “show” trucks for public events and low volume prototypes for limited track and road testing as a judicious application of money, priority and people can get one to a few built in six weeks to six months. Commercial production trucks, however, to be legally and safely classified as “production” can take years (four to six years is common).

New technologies in trucking are in context of a century of freight vehicle development. Any new vehicle is put in context of an existing baseline vehicle to show that it has advantages worthy of the investment by both the OEMs and fleet purchasers. This is true even for entirely new products like the 2018 Volvo VERA, shown in Figure 3, which has no cab or driver, but its primary function is still moving some amount of freight between locations and it would be compared to existing tractor-trailers that perform the same task to gauge benefits and inadequacies.

Virtual analysis and controlled lab and track testing increase OEM confidence in designs. There has been a significant growth in the use of virtual development of products in the digital space rather than physical. The use of virtual development and analysis has been made possible by the growth in computing capacity and speed made possible by the relentless computer chip developers. To some extent, virtual analysis is now becoming a necessity because the number of permutations of options and operating conditions cannot be economically modeled in the physical testing. However sophisticated computer modeling has become, the real world and nature continue to prove they are formidably difficult to predict. Ultimately real vehicles are needed to run in real environments to validate safety and performance factors. Established OEMs continually evolve a mix of virtual and real world testing to confirm their products meet requirements. Newer OEMs without that experience base or built out testing infrastructure may rely more on virtual development methods, accepting a higher level of risk that the virtual product and the real product may differ in actual use.

Physical testing begins with OEM test drives on real roads with weighted trailers in a range of weather and seasonal conditions. That is followed, or sometimes in parallel, with selected fleets conducting operational tests from their own facilities with real commercial loads. These tractors range in increasing levels of maturity from initial prototypes to pre-production and then limited early production units. The objective is to highlight and resolve any performance or durability issues that were somehow missed in development, and to gain miles to improve OEM and fleet confidence in the new product.

<!-- page: 16 -->
Problems that appear at this late level of development can originate from expecting carry-over parts from existing products to perform as expected with a new vehicle. Others occur from unanticipated changes in customer expectations — such as when emissions systems are designed for peak operation at highway speeds but are subjected to extreme sub-zero cold weather extended idling conditions that impact system uptime. System level design disconnects are spotted as well, where two independent design groups or suppliers working on different parts of a vehicle discover their parts actually interact in previously unthought-of ways, such as drivetrain and aerodynamic improvements that reduce source noise levels, such that squeaks, rattles, tire and exhaust noise become much more apparent to drivers.

Customer requirements often evolve as vehicles go from idea to reality, and designers may have to make early decisions on duty cycles and market needs to advance designs only to find that the requirements have changed when customers actually get their hands on the real product. Early fleet testing helps spot these types of issues and provides some buffer time to correct issues.

Tooling, that unseen part of making a vehicle in production volumes, has significant complexity. Tooling lead times and costs are a significant planning element in launching new products. Identical parts made from two different processes often also require validation testing for each. Consider metal parts as an example. A 2018 SAE article by Del Williams discusses:

“Original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) that outsource metal parts to contract manufacturers must often guess at the immediate production requirements for new products, so initial forecasts are often conservative or — the complete opposite — unrealistically high.

With this inevitable uncertainty, OEMs are also hesitant to invest significant dollars in hard tooling, which can cost tens of thousands of dollars upfront. Instead, less expensive soft tooling options are often selected, even if it increases the price-per-part initially. However, as production demands increase, soft tooling options can quickly slow production. At the appropriate “tipping point” in demand, OEMs ideally should transition to hard tooling, hybrid approaches, and even progressive dies that speed turnaround time and drive down the price.

<!-- page: 17 -->
This “laddering up” of the range of tooling options requires assessing at each plateau whether greater upfront investment in tooling will pay off with a relatively quick return on investment (ROI). Since tooling plays such a key role in this equation, it also means working closely with OEMs willing to evaluate the best available tooling options at any time as production requirements increase.

“As production requirements increase, transitioning to a more efficient type of tooling can save some OEMs tens of thousands of dollars a year with an ROI measured in weeks or a few months,” says Bob Denholtz, president of DureX Inc., an ISO 9001 registered contract metal fabrication facility in New Jersey. “Any good metal parts supplier should be able to help the OEM determine the most cost-effective tooling for their situation.”

Although, tool selection involves various factors, including part size, metal thickness, complexity, and finish, the cost per part is often related to the type of tooling utilized, Denholtz says. This progression generally ranges from soft tooling to hard tooling, hybrid approaches, staged tooling, and fully progressive dies [23].”

Just purchasing the raw steel or aluminum needed for some tools can have significant lead times, much less the intricacies of designing the tools to make increasingly complex shapes. Something like a truck headlamp with requirements for optical quality and complex styling features and durability in harsh conditions can take two years or more from the point where material is ordered for the injection molds to the point where a production quality headlamp is available. Tools for a large molded part like a truck hood might require months in lead time just in procuring the raw steel due to the tool’s size.

Smaller quantities used in prototypes and pre-production vehicles can often be done using less expensive tooling choices, but risk behavioral differences with parts from more expensive higher volume production tools.

## 8.2 TIME TO MARKET – THE MIRROR EXAMPLE

A real-world example of time to commercial market is provided in recent news on mirror systems. Vehicle manufacturers have legal requirements to validate and certify that their vehicles meet regulatory standards. These standards have evolved over years to address a variety of safety, reliability and emissions factors. They also have market demands where industry wants various options, similarly, evolved from years of experience.

Mirror systems are required on tractors. A typical tractor shows at least five mirrors as seen in the example in Figure 15.

The Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) describes that:

“(a) Every bus, truck, and truck tractor shall be equipped with two rear-vision mirrors, one at each side, firmly attached to the outside of the motor vehicle, and so located as to reflect to the driver a view of the highway to the rear, along both sides of the vehicle. All such regulated rear-vision mirrors and their replacements shall meet, as a minimum, the requirements of FMVSS No. 111 (49 CFR 571.111) in force at the time the vehicle was manufactured [13].”

Where tractors and trucks require each flat mirror to be “not less than 323 $cm^2$ of reflective surface, installed with stable supports on both sides of the vehicle [14].”

Over time, the cab-mounted mirrors grew in size to provide greater visibility than required by the CFR through market demands for improved visibility. More recent focus on improving aerodynamics has seen some cab mirrors decreasing back to the minimum required size, again, due again to market demands. The “firmly attached” provision of the CFR is satisfied through accelerated life testing using cab shake tests, cabs mounted on automated shaker tables and run through a lifetime worth of cycles in a relatively short calendar time. The “firmly attached” implies also that the mirror view is stable — mirrors poorly mounted can blur while driving from vibration and weather conditions. This human factor is validated in track tests and then road tests in a variety of conditions.

The two hood mirrors and the over door mirror shown in Figure 15 are not required by regulation. They are market driven customer demands. They too must be firmly attached and not blur through vibration or from aerodynamic soiling.

At the end of 2018, the Stoneridge MirrorEye, a camera based system, was granted a “five-year FMCSA exemption (that) allows MirrorEye-equipped trucks to operate with an integrated system of cameras and digital displays as an alternative to conventional rear-vision mirrors [15][73][74].” The system shown in Figure 16 replaces the externally mounted cab mirrors with cameras mounted on arms, and in cab displays mounted on each A-Pillar and one centered in the cab. The cameras are designed for all weather and day and night operations. Dean Newell of Maverick Transportation states, “The camera pans out as you turn so that the driver never loses sight of the rear corners of the trailer during the turn [16].”

While these camera systems are reported to eliminate blind spots, possibly eliminating the need for all five of the mirror systems shown in Figure 15, real world testing is needed to validate this. To this end, Schneider has accumulated some 300,000 miles of use with prototypes, along with other miles by Maverick Transportation and other fleets. These road miles serve to satisfying fleets’ and Stoneridge’s confidence in the maturity of the system and plan start of production of commercial units in 2019 [16]. Two other systems are the Bosch Mirror Camera System and Continental Automotive ProViu [16][17][18].

However, OEMs may not be ready to install these yet. Stoneridge’s website states, “The system is available for aftermarket and retrofit truck installation in the United States [15].” The complexity for OEMs may reside in complying with EPA Green House Gas regulations that require the OEM’s products to be compliant for the life of the vehicle, and a five-year exemption is not the life of the vehicle. The OEM, for example, may have to default to having cab cab-mounted mirrors for determining the EPA GHG aerodynamic performance for the truck. In the aftermarket, however, because MirrorEye would improve GHG aerodynamics, vehicle buyers may replace mirrors with MirrorEye without negatively compromising the OEM’s GHG assessment of emissions, avoiding violating EPA regulations. Voluntary programs like SmartWay (and non-voluntary interpretations of them such as in California) may have to be amended to recognize MirrorEye as an aerodynamic improvement. The perturbations of new

<!-- page: 21 -->
innovative technologies in the regulatory pond can ripple for some time, especially where laws require lead times of years.

Systems like MirrorEye, ProViu and the Mirror Camera System are destined to be mounted by OEMs as factory installed options, requiring each OEM to validate the production installations on their own vehicles through their own processes. The path to developing MirrorEye for testing and production started in July 2015 according to SEC filings [19]. OEM production installations may be in parallel or may lag months to years to complete their own integration and testing for their own platforms.

The transition from the current FMCSA exemption to be permanently allowed by regulations may take years, as government and industry groups determine which requirements are appropriate.

This one example illustrates that time to market for new innovative production qualified systems can take years. It also illustrates that prototypes can enter the market relatively quickly.

## 8.3 PACE OF NEW PRODUCTS

The pace of new product introductions is driven by market demand and new regulations. Experience shows that one OEM introducing an improved model is quickly paralleled by many other OEMs who have similarly been working on new products.

Clarissa Hawes of Trucks.com reported in September 2017 around the Mack Anthem new product launch, “In less than two years, the biggest names in semi-trucks have unveiled a combined seven new models, often replacing vehicles they hadn’t redesigned in a decade or more [30].” Hawes reflects that the new Anthem “replace(s) Mack’s Pinnacle Axle Back and marks the company’s first full redesign of its on-highway truck offering since 2005.”

Jerry Hirsch of Trucks.com reported in July 2017 around the Volvo VNL launch, “Daimler Trucks unveiled the latest model of its Freightliner Cascadia semi-truck last September, the first full redesign since its introduction in 2007. Also, in September, Navistar International Corp. launched its new line of International LT over-the-road trucks. Navistar’s last introduction of a large, over-the-road truck was its ProStar in 2007 [31].” Hirsch reflects that, “The VNL had its last major refresh in 2002 and was aging at a time when rival truck makers are bringing out new models.”

Hirsch quotes Göran Nyberg, president of Volvo Trucks North America, “The flurry of new introductions show that the product introduction cycle for heavy-duty trucks is speeding up as a result of keener competition and legislation dictating fuel economy and emissions improvements for big rigs.” Hirsch summarizes industry experience as follows: “While a decade or more can pass between heavy-duty truck redesigns, passenger vehicles typically have a five-year product cycle.”

Daimler’s manager of on-highway product marketing, Mike McHorse is quoted by Hirsch as stating, “The Daimler division has a product development lifecycle of approximately 10 years. What is picking up speed is the pace of updates, especially for electronic systems. We are seeing typical automotive systems make it to trucks much faster than in the past [31].”

Michael Baudendistel, an industry analyst for Stifel Financial Corporation, said, “Although the product cycle for new trucks is speeding up, don’t expect it to catch the pace of change in light vehicles, which typically complete a redesign every five years [31].” Baudendistel continues, “Paccar has (also) refreshed

<!-- page: 22 -->
designs to maintain its status as having the premium products in the market, and its product line includes some high-end models [31].”

Daimler stated in its official public comments submitted in response to the U.S. EPA July 2015 Notice of Proposed Rule Making (NPRM) for the Phase II Green House Gas regulations that, “Development of a new engine or a new cab takes on the order of a decade [32][33].”

Navistar’s official comments to the NPRM reflect on the regulations impact on the development plans and resources, “As a result of the Proposed Rule, then, there is a significant risk that the industry will be in nearly continuous development from 2019 through 2027. The result of this complexity will be added costs, including as a result of aggressive timeframes and constricted validation periods between product launches. From a customer’s perspective, there may be additional costs and also potentially additional down time as new technologies come on continuously for a 17-year period [34].”

PACCAR’s 2015 NPRM comments include emphasis on the need to validate reliability before commercial introduction, stating, “For commercial vehicles, it is imperative that new technologies are sufficiently robust and reliable across the broad range of applications for this market to allow the vehicles to operate throughout their work day. Downtime due to product unreliability negatively impacts the viability of companies and the economy [35].”

Comments by Autocar highlight the challenges of small custom chassis manufacturers in developing new products to regulations. “The company believes that additional lead time would not remedy the difficulties faced by such manufacturers in meeting the proposed standards or the negative consequences for the industry. For example, the manufacturers would still have limited ability to benefit from averaging and to spread compliance costs across many vehicles. We would still suffer from the shortage of technical compliance expertise, and customers would still experience disruption to their businesses due to production delays, upfront cost increases and increased continuing maintenance costs [36].”

Volvo highlights there are differences between technology demonstration vehicles and production vehicles, “It is a significant stretch to associate demonstration of a technology on a Light-Heavy Duty spark ignition engine with commercial feasibility on a Heavy-Heavy Duty compression ignition engine.” According to a news release, Cummins accumulated only 1,500 hours of operation over a 2 ½ year period. In addition, the release notes that, “to complete on-road validation testing the vehicle was subjected to a two-month demonstration on California roads; if this testing were additional to the 1,500 hours we could estimate an additional ~650 hours (12 hours per day, 6 days per week, for 9 weeks). It must be noted that a single engine or vehicle, even approximating 2,150 hours of operation, is not a statistically valid sample to determine either reliability or durability to meet the demands of a mature production technology. In short, the continued reference in the release to a ‘demonstration’ shows this effort was to show the technology could provide a benefit, not that it was reliable or durable [38].”

Component suppliers, such as Bendix in its NPRM comments, echo OEM concerns about providing adequate development, “We must avoid situations where fleets and smaller customers experience increases in vehicle downtime due to component failures or vehicle acquisition and operating cost increases that diminish the profitability of their businesses. The fleets will create a market pull for technologies that save them operating costs with payback times between 18 and 48 months [37].”

<!-- page: 23 -->
Established OEMs have significant experience developing and launching new products. While time to market is critical for all, experience has tempered the speed to ensure initial launch quality meets customer, management, investor and regulator expectations. Product recalls still occur, even with that added focus, as fleet customers flesh out the real-world use of the products and add significant miles in all conditions to the experience base.

## 8.5 RISK REDUCTION

Lindgren referenced PACCAR’s stage-gate Product Creation Process in his 2018 ACT EXPO discussion “Path Toward Commercialization [24].” Stage-gate systems have their origin in the 1950s Program Evaluation and Review Technique (PERT) process developed during the early days of the nuclear submarine and ballistic missile development at the Department of Defense with Lockheed. The processes are tools used in Systems Engineering and Project Management [27][28].

Rick Mihelic in his 2016 SAE Buckendale report, Fuel and Freight Efficiency - Past, Present and Future Perspectives, says, “A fundamental objective of systems engineering is to take complex systems and requirements and break them down to constituent parts and information that can be used to develop the individual elements of the system with assurance that the end product will meet the system top level needs. The process is not a static or serial one, as the level of knowledge and experience about a developing system improves with effort and time, and the top-level requirements are also subject to change due to the evolving nature of customer demands and regulatory requirements. The process requires revisiting requirements and assumptions to agree they are still relevant and appropriate. A project management tool for this has many names, including Stage Gate, PACE (Plan Activate Check Enable), Waterfall, Tollgate, Phase Review, Phase Gate, V-Cycle, APQP (Advanced Product Quality Planning), etc. (see Appendix 1), but they all share the common theme that as projects progress from idea to end product, the project benefits from interim reviews of both the engineering readiness, and review of the objectives and requirements for currency and appropriateness [26].” Each decision point involves an assessment of the risk involved in proceeding to the next phase of development.

An example of a tollgate process is shown in Figure 22, represented as a funnel to signify that decisions made during the course of development at the tollgates gradually refine systems towards products, evolving from a larger number for possibilities. Each assessment point determines what has been accomplished and what remains to be done, the maturity of each part of the project individually and as a whole, and an assessment of the financial, schedule and technical risks moving forward.

Established companies have evolved significant organizational experience in launching new products, regularly incorporating lessons learned. These processes can appear to be bureaucratic and unwieldy but likely have been developed from hard learned lessons. Examples abound from product recalls,

regulatory fines and legal settlements in the automotive world caused by management decisions to accept risk and field history that reflects poorly on those choices.

Start-ups can tap into this experience base by hiring staff from established OEMs or working with consultants or established Tier 1 and Tier 2 suppliers. However, these companies often lack the depth of infrastructure that well-established, deep pocket OEMs have in place.

Start-ups tend to be cash flow driven, since they likely do not have current production products providing income. Their focus is getting product to market as soon as viable to begin getting cash flow from sales and leasing. There may be a greater acceptance of risk in introducing products to market out of necessity to create cash flow. Reliability issues are discovered by customers and are the primary reason warranties of various lengths are provided by OEMs on their products. Warranty is not a guarantee of reliability; it is an estimate of financial risk by the OEM that a part will survive its expected design life, and a similar gamble by the customer that the company will pay to repair or replace systems that fail to meet their design life. An OEM advertising a 10-year warranty on a system is no guarantee that the system will last 10 years. It is instead an advertising statement that the company is willing to bet the part will survive 10 years, but if it doesn’t, they will cover the costs to replace or repair it. Those are financial decisions based on risk assessments. The growth in Lemon Laws highlights that having warranties is no guarantee the products purchased will survive a customer’s use.

## 8.7 TECHNOLOGY READINESS LEVELS (TRLS)

Systems exist for attempting to quantify the readiness of technology to be unleashed on end users. The Technology Readiness Level, or TRL, has been in use in various forms by U.S. government agencies such as NASA and DOE. A brief history presented can be found in a 2007 presentation by Mitchell and Sleefe of the Sandia National Laboratory (SNL), titled “On the Practical Application of Technology Readiness Levels [77].”

* 1980’s: TRLs were originally developed by NASA
* 1990’s: USAF adopted the use of TRLs
* 1995: John C. Mankins proposed descriptions of each TRL in a white paper
* 1999: GAO recommended that DOD adopt the use of NASA’s TRLs
* 2001: Deputy Under Secretary of Defense for Science and Technology issued a memorandum that endorsed use of TRLs in new major programs
* 2003: DOD first developed the Technology Readiness Assessment Deskbook

The National Academy of Sciences points to Mankins’ 1995 Technology Readiness Levels white paper as defining the levels in their 2016 The Power of Change: Innovation for Development and Deployment of Increasingly Clean Electric Power Technologies [78][79]. They conclude, “The technology readiness level (TRL) taxonomy is the most commonly utilized method for determining a given technology’s readiness for ultimate application in electricity generation, energy storage, and power delivery, or utilization in power systems.” The report is a detailed example employing TRLs to describe readiness and is concurrently relevant to discussion of alternative fuel trucks.

Mankins outlined readiness levels as follows:
* TRL 1 Basic principles observed and reported
* TRL 2 Technology concept and/or application formulated

<!-- page: 37 -->
* TRL 3 Analytical and experimental critical function and/or characteristic proof-of-concept
* TRL 4 Component and/or breadboard validation in laboratory environment
* TRL 5 Component and/or breadboard validation in relevant environment
* TRL 6 System/subsystem model or prototype demonstration in a relevant environment (ground or space)
* TRL 7 System prototype demonstration in a space environment
* TRL 8 Actual system completed and “flight qualified” through test and demonstration (ground or space)
* TRL 9 Actual system “flight proven” through successful mission operations

## 8.8 MANUFACTURING READINESS LEVELS (MRLS)

Technology readiness is but one part of introducing new vehicles. Manufacturing readiness is another critical area. The U.S. Department of Defense Manufacturing Technologies Program publishes the Manufacturing Readiness Level (MRL) Deskbook [82]. MRLs were developed to create a uniform system for classifying the readiness of technology for production, to essentially classify the level of risk at a given point in developing the technology for its end use. The handbook states, “As manufacturing readiness increases, demonstration of manufacturing capabilities should be accomplished in more realistic environments.”

The ARENA Commercial Readiness Index clarifies that rolling out new technologies is more than just getting them into production, which is the focus of the Technology Readiness Levels. There are further points in a products life cycle including long-term sustainment in the market and eventual retirement and disposal. The long-term sustainment of products in a market with rapidly changing technology is a concern for the new generations of equipment. Where computer software and hardware rapidly grows and evolves, where sensor technology rapidly becomes more capable, where infrastructure is being developed in parallel with vehicles, etc. there are significant opportunities for trucks to become less competitive more rapidly and for obsolescence to occur much sooner.

While the TRL, MRL and CRI nomenclature is not commonly used inside of truck OEMs today, the foundation of staged maturation in development and concerns about long-term commercial viability are ever present in each OEMs management of new technologies.

## 8.10 OPTION CONTENT

The level of option content covered in production is a possible differentiator of how companies discuss being in production. Henry Ford reportedly stated that customers could have whatever color they wanted as long as it was black when in 1909, he introduced the Model T. Today Ford offers a wide variety of colors each model year as demonstrated in Figure 40. Specialization and customization have been a mainstay of North American commercial truck offerings by all the OEMs for decades. There are literally millions of permutations of option content possible on today’s trucks. While production and procurement managers would like to follow Henry Ford’s one-size-fits-all mandate, the market is much more complex and demanding.

Just because that is the way things were done does not mean the future will be that way, but it is likely. Option content tends to evolve over time as customers get familiar with the product and find shortcomings or opportunities to use them in ways not envisioned by the designers. Competition drives manufacturers to constantly refresh option content offerings to attract new customers or to not be left behind when a competitor introduces their offerings. Option content tends to grow over time.

During their OEM careers, NACFE’s staff members have encountered a number of attempts to simplify product offerings to increase volumes and take better advantage of economies of scale. The long-term perspective is that these attempts may succeed in the short term but eventually demand and market conditions prevail to force option content growth.

<!-- page: 42 -->
Initial market introduction also must suffice to attract enough buyers to make the launch financially promising. Fleets may initially accept some compromises in option content, but trucks are tools where the capital investment must have a positive return. One-size-fits-all approaches generally have too many compromises impacting the fleet’s bottom line, especially in low margin trucking operations.

The International Council for Clean Transportation (ICCT), discussed option content in their 2013 “Barriers to the Increased Adoption of Fuel Efficiency Technologies in the North American On-Road Freight Sector [85].” ICCT presented one path that a technology can take to get into production, as shown in Figure 41, explaining there is a point in time where market interest and technology maturity are sufficient that an “OEM believes there will be high enough demand to justify the engineering. If a technology is gaining adoption and interest, more suppliers emerge and begin developing and supplying the same or alternative products to meet the functionality of the feature. As the technology is offered by more OEMs, later adopting fleets of all sizes and OEM-allegiances can decide to purchase the technologies. With the release of these new products by the OEMs, their dealers are trained and begin to promote the features to the larger industry, driving scale and lowering costs. Eventually, most if not all of the OEMs offer the device as standard in their product configurations and the laggards and non-adopters become users by default when they purchase new equipment [85].”

Each new option added to the production line can create multiple permutations of configurations that previously have not been built or tested. The interaction between option content offerings can cause field issues such as reliability or unintended consequences to performance factors. Some permutations of option content are rare, and problems may be discovered and resolved on the assembly line. Other issues do not show up until the truck is in use. OEMs try to address these potential issues before starting production, but the reality is that unknowns exist and OEMs are not omniscient, which is one of the

<!-- page: 43 -->
reasons manufacturers offer and customers want warranties and service contracts, and why government agencies have product recalls. When issues are discovered, corrections are implemented, both short term for the already fielded units, and longer term for the production line. This can exacerbate the number of configurations requiring support over the life the model.

# 9 SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS

NACFEs goal for this report is to raise awareness of use of the term “production” with respect to new freight hauling technologies. The term “production” has several interpretations that can cause confusion. OEMs likely have differing understandings of what production start means, but customers, regulators, investors, media and the public may not understand the differences.

NACFE concludes that there is no one definition for production beyond being a point in time where a new product comes off a production line and is commercially provided to an end use customer. The maturity of the product at that point may vary significantly among OEMs. That maturity includes the level of risk assumed by the customer that the vehicle will be configured for and operate as needed for their specific duty cycles. That risk will be measureable, after-the-fact through on-going maintenance costs, warranty claims, downtime measurements, number of product recalls, number and frequency of software updates (whether over the air or in shop), life of product, etc.

Definitions NACFE staff have witnessed for being “In Production”.
* 1st vehicle off low rate production tooling, limited option content available
* 1st vehicle off high rate production tooling, limited option content available
* 1st vehicle off high rate production tooling, robust option content available
* 1st commercially sold vehicle but with limited trained field support and limited parts availability
* 1st commercially sold vehicle with substantial trained field support and robust parts availability
* Low rate production line at factory, factory ramping assembly training and supply chain
* Full rate production line at factory, factory staff trained, supply chain fully operational
* 1st commercially leased vehicle, with intent to retrieve and replace vehicle with later version
* 1st commercially leased vehicle with full service life
* 1st vehicle to be ordered via the production ordering process
* 1st vehicle but requiring frequent software updates and recalls to service centers
* 1st vehicle with stable software configuration and no recalls
* 1st vehicle in customer commercial use, low uptime
* 1st vehicle in customer commercial use, high uptime

Technology development is a process that ideally reduces risk through time and investment in manpower and resources. Established OEMs with multiple product lines to support must worry about current product cash flow in addition to future products. Start-ups with no established product lines have a substantial need to turn investments into positive cash flow as quickly as possible. These two situations tend to make some OEMs more risk accepting than others, or conversely, more risk averse.

<!-- page: 44 -->
The depth of resources testing, development, marketing, support, etc. for well-established OEMs tends to be significantly greater than those for newer companies. Capital and expense investments for established OEMs for test facilities, staff training, infrastructure, marketing, R&D, etc. can be shared across several platforms in various stages of life. Newer OEMs are more challenged to provide these foundations, having to be more creative and frugal in their use.

At the same time, well-established OEMs have additional self-imposed constraints on product developments considering needing to support the viability of cash flows from existing products and to recover value from long-term capital investments. These organizations may also be less lean due to the inertia of long-term bureaucracy development. In some cases, these well-established OEMs may even be handcuffed by past infrastructure decisions, unable to economically rationalize wholesale adoption of leading-edge infrastructure due to risk of shutting down operations entirely. New OEMs can embrace emerging technologies for managing their operations as they are not replacing anything. This may allow newer companies to accelerate development by taking advantage of the newer efficiencies and automation available today.

Mergers, acquisitions and partnerships can quickly blur the lines between old and new OEMs. The truck OEM business world is not static. The specialization of vehicles and flexibility of alternative drivetrains like battery electric and fuel cell electric may increase the number of new entrants to the existing pool of OEMs. Medium-duty truck importers may find opportunity to become heavy-duty truck makers.

The significant amounts of investment in new truck technologies are at levels where market shifts and disruption can happen rapidly. The impact of new regulations such as zero emission zones and near-zero emission rules can also make rapid shifts possible in North American markets.

However, in the end, whatever changes the future reveals in OEMs launching new products, fleets will continue to expect to operate their products with acceptable uptimes, improved safety and low cost of operation. The OEMs that place too great a burden on fleets by introducing immature products will lose out in the long run to more robust ones. Fledgling new technologies risk slower adoption as well if immature products have poor real-world performance due to companies accepting greater risk to achieve near term cash flows.

Four key conclusions were reached by the study team.
1. Many definitions exist for a statement by truck builders that they are in production causing confusion with stakeholders.
2. Product development is an investment in a new product that reduces collective risk over time through testing and decision making.
3. There are dramatically differing situations for established versus new OEM entrants, including such items as established development processes, revenue needs, variation of product offerings and risk acceptance levels.
4. Fleet expectations for cost of operation, performance, reliability and durability, etc. will not change and in fact be more demanding.

# 11 APPENDIX 1. SAMPLING OF PROJECT MANAGEMENT SYSTEMS & TERMS

This is not a comprehensive list, but a selection of key terms that are useful in discussing differences between established OEMs and start-ups relating to maturity of project management methods. A trend experienced by the NACFE authors over many years is that there is a demanding competitive market space for new, copyrighted or trademarked terminology for project management systems that in many cases share a great deal of commonality. Project management is not new. The Egyptians building the Pyramids at Giza, the Romans building the Coliseum in Rome, the Chinese building the Great Wall of China, and the Indians building the Taj Mahal all had some form of project management system that involved subdividing the incredibly complex task at hand into manageable sub-parts, prioritizing and sequencing them, assigning resources, managing risks and trying to stay on budget and on schedule.

## 11.1 PERT – PERFORMANCE EVALUATION AND REVIEW TECHNIQUE

The Project Development and Management Association briefly summarizes PERT as, “An event-oriented network analysis technique used to estimate project duration when there is a high degree of uncertainty in estimates of duration times for individual activities [62]” This definition is misleading as PERT is not just a scheduling tool, but a project management methodology.

The originators in 1958 at the Department of the Navy Special Projects Office, Booz, Allen & Hamilton and Lockheed Missile Systems Division summarized the purpose of PERT [27] as:

“The basic approach of PERT is less novel than the execution of the approach. Grossly oversimplified, the approach involves

(1) The selection of specific, identifiable events which must occur along the way to successful conclusion of the project.

(2) The sequencing of these events and establishing of interdependencies between events so that a project network is developed.

(3) The estimate of time required to achieve these events together with a measurement of the uncertainties involved.

(4) The design of an analysis or evaluation procedure to process and manipulate these data.

(5) The establishing of information channels to bring actual achievement data and change data to the evaluation point.

(6) The application of electronic data processing equipment to the analysis procedure.”

James Lewis’ Project Manager’s Desk Reference [63] highlights that:

“When a project contains a majority of activities for which no experience exists — that is, no historical data is available — then the estimating difficulty becomes significant. When no experience is available to use as a guide, the only thing that can be done is make the best possible guess, based on whatever relevant experience one has.

<!-- page: 52 -->
“It seems clear, however, that the more unique an activity is, the less certain the estimate of its duration, and therefore, the more risky the project will be in terms of control. And since a lot of projects (such as research and development) fall into this category, the question naturally arises of whether there might not be some method that could be employed to estimate risk.

“The PERT system is based on the recognition that estimates are uncertain, and therefore it makes sense to talk of ranges of durations, and the probability that an activity duration will fall into that range, rather than assuming that an activity will be completed in a fixed amount of time.”

PERT is therefore a methodology for quantifying and then managing risk across the tasks of a development program, not merely a scheduling networking methodology.

## 11.2 CPM – CRITICAL PATH METHOD

The Critical Path Method, in contrast to PERT, is a network analysis technique used to estimate project duration when there is a high degree of certainty. The Project Manager’s Desk Reference clarifies:

“When a project consists of activities, most of which are similar to others that have been performed a large number of times, CPM scheduling is generally used. With CPM, estimates of activity durations are based on historical data, and are assumed to be the mean or average time that the activity has taken to perform in the past.

Estimates of activity durations for CPM projects are taken as averages based on history, once they are in place, they are often assumed to be more-or-less fixed, or to use the colloquial expression, they are ‘engraved in granite.’”

The PDMA summarizes Critical Path [62] as:

“The set of interrelated activities that must be completed for the project to be finished successfully can be mapped into a chart showing how long each task takes, and which tasks cannot be started before which other tasks are completed. The critical path is the set of linkages through the chart that is the longest. It determines how long a project will take.”

## 11.3 MICROSOFT PROJECT AND PROJECT MANAGEMENT SOFTWARE

Computer based scheduling tools like the ubiquitous Microsoft Project have a basis in CPM and PERT [65][66]. Projects are deconstructed, a term is functional decomposed, into their constituent major tasks, sequenced, resourced, time spans including float specified and critical path can then be mathematically determined by the software using fundamental network analysis techniques [64]. MS Project and similar software applications are just tools, as such, they can be applied appropriately to assist in efficient project management or inappropriately to become a burden to project management. Managing risk is still the responsibility of the company project team members. These tools are digital interpretations of PERT and CPM methods, but they are just software tools, as good or as bad as the inputs and on-going maintenance by people. There is a distinction between the software tools, and the fundamental PERT and CPM project management methodologies. The tools get repurposed in some cases to focus on labor time keeping where they fail at understanding the concept of initiative, innovation and unpaid overtime. Sometimes they are repurposed as accounting tools, where they may

<!-- page: 53 -->
become a drain on progress — where more time is spent tracking a project than is spent actually working on it. The software also tends to assume requirements are understood and defined at the start of a program — but market demands shift regularly, as do company priorities, budgets and schedules based on the competitive nature of the marketplace and the whims of customers. Overly controlled five year projects may find that the requirements they expertly built a product to from five years ago are no longer the focus of the current market place.

## 11.4 STAGE GATE®

Stage Gate is a trademarked brand name for the project management process marketed by Stage-Gate International. Per their website, “Stage-Gate® is a proprietary innovation management model designed to coordinate the cross-functional creative process and integrate an options-based investment decision framework to accelerate time to market, maximize profitable success and minimize risk. It provides a consistent, proven framework for new product development governance [67].” Their use cases include examples from PepsiCo, Beam Suntory, Proctor & Gamble, Sab Miller, Swarovski, 3M, and more. The advertising talks to their promotion of a fundamental phase gate approach to project management.

The PDMA Handbook of New Product Development defines Gate [69] as:

“The point at which a management decision is made to allow the product development project to proceed to the next stage, to recycle back to the current stage to better complete some of the tasks, or to terminate. The number of gates varies by company.”

The Handbook defines Stage [69] as:

“One group of concurrently accomplished tasks with specified outcomes and deliverables.”

The advertising claim, shown in Figure 42, that “80% of global 1000 companies adopt Stage-Gate” might be open to some interpretation. Even the Winning at New Products book from 1993 cited in the graphic recognizes in the chapter titled Stage-Gate that, “Facing increased pressure to reduce cycle times yet improve their new product success rate, companies are increasingly looking to new product game plans, or ‘stage-gate systems,’ to manage, direct and control product innovation efforts [68].” The book uses the term stage-gate approach or stage-gate system, uncapitalized. Whether capitalized and trademarked with a hyphen or simply used as text, the conclusion that some form of stage gate or phase gate management is in use by a majority of companies is fait accompli at this point [68][69].

## 11.5 PLM PRODUCT LIFE CYCLE MANAGEMENT

Product Life Cycle Management, simply PLM, can be an all-inclusive term for describing the development from birth to retirement of a product. Again, there are software systems to facilitate this and it is the subject of a significant number of books and conferences.

PDMA Handbook of New Product Development somewhat underwhelmingly defines PLM [69] as:

“Changing the features and benefits of the product, elements of the marketing mix, and manufacturing operations over time to maximize the profits obtainable from the product over its life cycle.”

Siemens, a provider of PLM system software, defines a PLM System [70] as:

“Product life cycle management (PLM) is an information management system that can integrate data, processes, business systems and, ultimately, people in an extended enterprise. PLM software allows you to manage this information throughout the entire lifecycle of a product efficiently and cost-effectively from ideation, design and manufacture through service and disposal.”

Parametric Technologies, a competing PLM system software provider describes the attributes of PLM System [71] as:

<!-- page: 55 -->
“Having disconnected enterprise systems and siloes throughout the product development process can make it much more difficult for stakeholders to find the information they need — or give them complete insight into what information is already available to them. PTC PLM out-of-the-box applications serve as multi-system data orchestration, enabling your team to break down those siloes, reduce time-to-market, decrease costs, improve quality, and further drive innovation. And with AR design share, it's easier than ever to share designs with disparate teammates — to scale and within its physical environment — all while protecting IP.”

Oleg Shilovistsky reported in Beyond PLM on Al Bunshaft of Dassault Systemes, a PLM System provider, that Bunshaft would focus on lifecycle in the phrase PLM [72]:

“The most important word of those three is lifecycle. That’s what makes it more than just design. It’s managing the lifecycle of an innovation, from when it starts with a concept, an idea, and goes into requirements, definition, and the design phase. Then you have to simulate it. You can even simulate the manufacturing environment. After that we can track changes, parts changes, and defects.”

The distinction should again be made between the management method and the software tool. The management methodology of product life-cycle management includes the responsibility of team personnel to get the project life cycle done well, where the specific software tools may or may not facilitate that methodology. Just having PLM software does not guarantee success, nor does its absence guarantee failure, but a well implemented PLM system properly maintained and supported can reduce risks and improve potential of project success.

## 11.6 V CYCLE

The V Cycle or variously V-cycle, Vee Cycle, V-model, etc., “illustrates some key systems principles about the relationship of the early phases of the development to the end results of the project [89].” One detailed description is found in the Systems Engineering Guidebook for Intelligent Transportations Systems published by the U.S. DOT [89].

## 11.7 APQP ADVANCED PRODUCT QUALITY PLANNING

Advanced Product Quality Planning (APQP) is “a set of procedures and techniques developed by the automotive industry to methodically ensure that a product satisfies the customer requirements. The goal of APQP is to facilitate communication between the design team and the rest of the organization. Designing is a company function, but often it is done in silos, only to discover during the transition that there are problems that are too late to address [86].” A complete overview is in the book Advanced Product Quality Planning and Control Plan available from AIAG [87].

## 11.9 DESIGN THINKING MODEL

The simplistic view that designs follow linearly from requirements belies the non-linear nature of innovation. The very process of inventing something new opens up further avenues for looking at existing designs in new ways, spawning further innovations. While its possible to design an innovative new product to be a direct one-for-one replacement to a prior technology product, that perspective severely handcuffs the new technology by discounting new synergies or new operational methods that the technology enables. Providing greater opportunity to test in real world conditions with real users can spawn significant new approaches to operations, designs and performance expectations. According to the Interaction Design Foundation, “Design Thinking is a design methodology that provides a solution-based approach to solving problems. It’s extremely useful in tackling complex problems that are ill-defined or unknown, by understanding the human needs involved, by re-framing the problem in human-centric ways, by creating many ideas in brainstorming sessions, and by adopting a hands-on approach in prototyping and testing [75].”
