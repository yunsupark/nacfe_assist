# A NEED TO REDEFINE CLASS 8 LONG-HAUL TRUCKING

NACFE believes that long-haul trucking should include both over-the-road and longer regional haul return-to-base operations.

By Mike Roeth, Executive Director

The industry is changing to shorter length of hauls and more hub-and-spoke operations with a larger focus on day cab tractors and return-to-base operations. Let us explain.

The trucking industry is not one homogenous market, rather it features a variety of truck sizes operating in a variety of duty cycles. The Class 8 truck market is not homogenous either as it includes both highway tractors and local trucks. Those local trucks usually are called work trucks, also known as vocational trucks, and are made up of everything from construction dump trucks to concrete mixers, garbage trucks, terminal tractors and many more duty-specific applications.

According to ACT Research those trucks typically make up 25% of heavy-duty truck production. The rest of the market — 75% — is highway tractors. Those tractors can either be day cabs or sleepers. Day cabs are about 40% of tractor market production and sleepers are 60%. See Figure 1.

Day cab tractors are typically used in return-to-base application routes and sleepers are used in over-the-road routes — traditionally called long haul. In return-to-base operations the truck leaves from a depot and at the end of the driver's shift returns to that same depot. See Figure 2.

Of course, there are no absolutes in trucking, so the reality is that there is a small percentage of day cab tractors operating on longer hauls and in those cases, the driver sleeps in a hotel rather than in the sleeper compartment of the tractor. Conversely, some fleets operate sleepers in return-to-base applications. There is one major fleet we know of that uses a standard spec and all its trucks are spec'd with sleepers. They do this for more flexibility in scheduling, to relocate trucks to sleeper operations if their customer base changes, for better resale value and other reasons.

## Defining Market Segments

It can be difficult to find a common language within any industry, and for us in trucking, specifically when it comes to discussing long haul. Let's start with length of haul. What exactly is the length of short haul? Regional haul? Long haul? However, it's important for the trucking industry to use standard language and descriptions when discussing length of haul.

Given our research and Run on Less efforts, NACFE has identified some definitions for length of haul. Typically, short regional haul is less than 50 miles out and back for a total of 100 miles. Medium regional haul is around 100 miles out and back for a total of 200 miles and long regional haul is 300 miles out and back for a total of 600 miles. Finally, there is the traditional segment, more commonly known as long haul, where instead of returning to base, the over-the-road driver spends their rest periods in the truck's sleeper. These include irregular routes where the tractor may never return to a company site.

## Tying in Alternative Fueled Vehicles

The understanding of length of haul becomes critically important as we move through the "Messy Middle," in which fleets have a variety of powertrain options from which to choose.

Currently if there are 200 gallons of fuel on a diesel-powered truck, and the truck averages 8 MPG, it can travel for about three days without needing to stop for fuel. Today's battery electric powered trucks are capable of about 250 miles on a single charge but can go much further when they can take advantage of opportunity charging.

Also, longer ranges are probable with more efficient batteries and other systems being optimized moving forward. This was demonstrated by the Tesla Semi deployed by PepsiCo in Run on Less – Electric DEPOT. During the Run, the Tesla Semis saw ranges of 410 miles on a single charge and one completed 1,076 miles in a 24-hour period.

If battery electric trucks are going to have a place in over-the-road trucking, battery range needs to be improved and/or it needs to become faster and easier to recharge the truck's batteries en route.

Both Run on Less – Electric and Run on Less – Electric DEPOT focused on trucks operating in shorter lengths of haul. Based on results and findings from those two events, and from developments in the trucking industry, NACFE believes that the short regional haul market can be served by battery electric vehicles and medium and longer hauls will become achievable as technology matures.

With Run on Less – Messy Middle, NACFE is expecting to capture real-world data on how vehicles with a variety of powertrains are performing in long-haul applications. The goal of the Run is to clear up some of the messiness surrounding powertrain options in long-haul trucking applications.

## Bringing Clarity to Long Haul

The next Run on Less event in September 2025 will focus on heavy-duty Class 8 trucks — sleepers and day cabs — in long-haul return-to-base and over-the-road duty cycles.

We are calling this demonstration Run on Less – Messy Middle and we will be focusing on the multiple powertrain solutions that are currently available to fleets, including battery electric, hydrogen fuel cells, renewable natural gas, renewable and bio diesel, and hybrids. We will be looking at energy efficiency technologies for all fuel types. We hope to bring some clarity to long haul.

Our goal with this Run is to explain the various alternative fueling options and investigate the need for infrastructure changes to accommodate alternative-fueled vehicles.

Run on Less – Messy Middle will feature around 10 fleets deploying trucks with differing decarbonization solutions. As in previous Run on Less demonstrations, the technology will either be commercially available or representative of the manufacturer's plans.

While long-haul represents a small portion of the total trucking industry (all classes), it contributes a significant portion of emissions. If we are going to decarbonize trucking, we need to better understand the options available to fleets operating in that duty cycle and help them determine the powertrain solution that is best for their specific application.

## Some Other Important Terms

To maximize mileage on a single truck, some fleets use slip seating, which is sometimes referred to as dual utilization. What this refers to is the practice of multiple drivers using the same truck over a 24-hour period. Once the driver's shift is over, a second driver gets in the truck and drives for their allowed hours of service. Slip seating is used to help maximize the utilization of the truck and reduce downtime between driver shifts.

Also, team driving involves having two CDL drivers in the truck at the same time — often a pair who know one another like a husband and wife, other partners or even family members or friends. While one driver operates the truck, the other can be in the sleeper taking their mandated break. This allows the truck to travel around 1000 miles in a 24-hour period while complying with hours-of-service regulations.

## What This All Means

Our industry is changing and that requires us to change as well, including the language we use to define things. Figure 4 represents NACFE's definition of long-haul trucking. Regional haul utilizing day cab tractors is growing. This increase in return-to-base operations assists fleets in locating their truck fueling or charging for alternative fuels, while also supporting drivers' desires to be home more often.

Given the transformation to alternative fuels and the growth in regional haul, NACFE firmly believes that a new definition for long haul should include both long distance return-to-base operations as well as over-the-road ones.

# ABOUT US

## ABOUT NACFE

The North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) works to drive the development and adoption of efficiency enhancing, environmentally beneficial, and cost-effective technologies, services, and operational practices in the movement of goods across North America. NACFE provides independent, unbiased research, including Confidence Reports on available technologies and Guidance Reports on emerging ones, which highlight the benefits and consequences of each, and deliver decision-making tools for fleets, manufacturers, and others. NACFE partners with RMI on a variety of projects including the Run on Less demonstration series, electric trucks, emissions reductions, and low-carbon supply chains.

## ABOUT RUN ON LESS BY NACFE

Run on Less is a biennial demonstration showcasing advancements in freight efficiency. The event takes place over the course of three weeks and has fleets across North America moving freight in their normal operations to show how it is possible to operate efficiently with today's technologies. Each truck in the Run is outfitted with a telematics device allowing NACFE to track key metrics.

The first Run took place in 2017 and featured seven fleets that averaged 10 MPG over in long-haul routes over the course of the Run. Run on Less Regional, held in 2019, featured 10 fleets that averaged 8.4 MPG in regional haul routes during the Run. Run on Less – Electric, held in 2021, featured 13 trucks operating electric vehicles on real routes carrying real freight. Run on Less – Electric DEPOT featured 10 fleets with 15 or more electric trucks at a depot.
