NACFE has described the interim of freight hauling before we arrive at the future net-zero freight system as the “messy middle.” That transition will see multiple alternative fuel powertrains in use as manufacturers and fleets try to keep the nation’s freight moving while the requirements migrate to being first net-zero emissions and ultimately true-zero emissions.

The commercial vehicle marketplace is all about supply and demand—all the alternatives will be in play to fill demand. In time, the supply of zero-emissions vehicles and the associated infrastructure to support them will become a reality. However, in the near- and mid-term, freight still needs to be delivered daily.

Alternative fuel systems outlined in NACFE’s Guidance Report: Viable Class 7/8 Electric, Hybrid, and Alternative Fuel Tractors, will be in use regionally to fill the need. North America’s experience in 2020 with ramifications of the COVID-19 pandemic has emphasized that freight has no choice, fleets cannot wait, and freight must get through daily. Waiting for the perfect solution is not an option for freight haulers and truck manufacturers. Instead, investments need to be made each year based on choosing better and better available solutions.

Long-term, a hydrogen future also will be an electric future, as the two are intertwined. Just as an FCEV is a combination of a hydrogen fuel cell and a BEV, the marketplace will likely have both solutions in quantity, as they are optimized for different duty cycles, uses, and regions.

The technology of both is mature enough for truck manufacturers to begin production, but immature enough that first owners should not expect problem-free use. Decades of experience with battery electric vehicles and fuel cells for buses and cars has matured the technology to an extent, but real-world fleet use for commercial freight is different, and there will be a learning curve for all involved.

Scaling both BEV and FCEV use are tied together; the innovations in the infrastructure and distribution of energy are linked, both requiring significant growth in environmentally responsible energy production. Scale will bring cost reduction, as is expected in technology deployments. Where there is a path to improving profitability, innovation naturally occurs.

NACFE concludes there are five major findings supported by the research for this report.

* Hydrogen fuel cell trucks are just starting to see real-world use and their adoption is being driven by regional or national considerations that are much bigger than what exists for trucking fleets.
* Battery electric trucks should be the baseline for hydrogen fuel cell electric vehicle (HFCEV) comparisons, rather than any internal combustion engine alternative.
* As for all alternatives, fleets should optimize the specifications of HFCEVs for the job they should perform while expecting that the trade cycles will lengthen.
* The future acceleration of HFCEVs is likely not about the vehicles or the fueling but more about the creation and distribution of the hydrogen itself.
* Finally, the potential for autonomous fuel cell trucks to operate 24 hours a day adds significant opportunity for making sense of capital and operational investment in hydrogen.

NACFE started this research looking to evaluate the viability of commercial hydrogen fuel cell trucks. It was readily apparent that the topic is much bigger than the trucks. We conclude that it takes a village for hydrogen to scale quickly. Trucking is not the primary mechanism, industries and utilities will need to participate heavily to scale rapidly. If the hydrogen is there, the use for heavy truck transportation will come. This paper highlights the complexity of the hydrogen future.

## 7 WHAT IS A HYDROGEN FUEL CELL TRUCK?

Scott Samuelsen, professor emeritus of mechanical and aerospace engineering at the University of California, Irvine, is director of the National Fuel Cell Research Center, located at UC Irvine, writing in IEEE Spectrum (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) in 2016 defined a fuel cell vehicle as follows:

> “Rather than relying on combustion to drive pistons that then power an electric generator as in a hybrid car, a fuel-cell vehicle uses electrochemistry to generate electricity directly. This is done by taking compressed hydrogen gas stored on board and combining it with oxygen from the air. The products of the reaction are electricity to power the vehicle and water, which is discharged through the tailpipe along with the nitrogen that entered the fuel cell with the air. Because there is no combustion, high temperatures are avoided and oxides of nitrogen, a smog-inducing pollutant from conventional vehicles, are not produced. And because there is no carbon in the fuel to begin with, no hydrocarbons, carbon monoxide, or carbon dioxide are emitted from the tailpipe [43].”

A hydrogen fuel cell vehicle can be described as a range extended electric vehicle, or conversely as a hybrid electric vehicle, or as its own category, a fuel cell electric vehicle. It really depends on how the vehicle system is apportioned between the fuel cell technology and the battery technology in powering the electric motors that drive the wheels. (See Figure 3.) A pure fuel cell vehicle would convert hydrogen fuel directly into electricity to power the drive motors with no onboard battery storage. A pure battery electric vehicle uses stored onboard battery electricity to directly power the drive motors.

The proportions are very relevant to the specific duty cycles the vehicle must handle, and the many tradeoffs involved in sizing the fuel cell, hydrogen tanks and onboard battery capacity. This is where energy conversion efficiency comes into play. Converting hydrogen to electricity depends on the size of both the tanks and the capability of the fuel cell(s).

A battery dominant FCEV uses the fuel cell to charge the onboard batteries. The batteries then directly power the electric motors. As the batteries deplete running the motors, the fuel cell provides some replacement of energy, but the battery dominant system expects that the duty cycle will reduce the state of charge (SOC). Sized correctly for the duty cycle, the vehicle ends it shift before the battery SOC is completely depleted. Complete depletion generally means some low SOC cutoff typically around 20% SOC [3]. The fuel cell then recharges the parked truck prior to its next shift. 

A fuel cell dominant vehicle will use both the fuel cell and the battery pack to power the electric motors. The battery pack serves to handle short demand peaks, like accelerations or short hills, while the fuel cell is sized to provide continuous power to the motors for a typical average duty cycle load. 

There is a balance between planned typical loads and peak loads that dictates how much battery and how much fuel cell is required for the expected duty cycles. Designers need to statistically predict nominal and off-nominal loads to properly size the systems for the end user. A dedicated route with predictable freight loads and repeatable traffic and weather conditions can allow smaller battery packs for a fuel cell dominant system. Variable routing with a wide variety of payloads and complex traffic and weather conditions may require a more battery dominant system with greater battery capacity to compensate for the unpredictable duty cycles. Conversely, this variable route also might be served by having larger fuel cell(s) rather than battery packs.

## 9 METRICS AND KPIS

The “E” in NACFE stands for “efficiency.” NACFE’s mission for the last decade has been to improve freight efficiency with a focus on improving fuel use efficiency. Energy conversion efficiency is seemingly a simple metric — the ratio of the amount of energy used for a purpose to the total energy supplied to the system. However, energy efficiency can be viewed at the component, subsystem, or complete vehicle levels with a variety of factors that must be considered at each level for the number to have any useful perspective.

Fuel efficiency is the common term used when comparing distance traveled per amount of fuel used. It is a common key performance indicator (KPI) for fleets. The challenge with all KPIs is what to include in the definition of the system. At the single diesel vehicle level, pump-to-wheel (PTW) efficiency is measured in miles traveled per gallon of diesel fuel consumed. This is probably the most common metric easily understood from pumping a number of gallons into a vehicle and seeing how many miles it drives on that fuel. Use of this KPI to compare vehicles requires a deep dive into many factors for the comparisons to be truly equivalent evaluations.

mi/gal or mpg

This metric can be readily expanded on from other easily measured metrics like dollars spent for the fuel. For example, it’s easy to put a monetary value to this where the fuel cost is seen on the pump and credit card, so dollars per mile, or dollars per gallon are easy metrics with firm numbers.

$/gal or $/mi

The income efficiency from moving the freight is similarly quantifiable from what a shipper pays to move the freight found in the back-office accounting records and measured in dollars per mile or gallon of fuel. 

$income/mile or $income/gal

Adding in the weight of freight measured in tons carried gets into a metric termed freight efficiency, measured in ton miles of freight per gallon used, or simply the freight carried times the average mpg.

freight-ton-miles/gal

Measuring emissions like CO2 or particulates like NOx provides a way to quantify environmental efficiency, grams of CO2 per mile or gallon, or particulates emitted per mile or gallon, measured in grams per mile, or micro-grams per mile. This gets more complicated as there are no gauges measuring emissions, only factory estimates, averages, or the occasional field audit using specialized testing equipment. There also are a library of possible chemical nomenclatures as there are a variety of constituent pollutants and particulate sizes.

## 10 HYDROGEN AND ENERGY EFFICIENCY

NACFE’s skepticism on hydrogen originates from the well-to-wheel analysis where hydrogen is not particularly efficient at using energy to move vehicles compared to pure battery electric power. CleanTechnica’s Steve Hanley summarized a similar point stating:

> “Making electricity to electrolyze hydrogen which is then used in fuel cells to power vehicles is not as efficient as making electricity and using it to power vehicles directly in the first place. Every time energy gets converted from one form to another, there are losses. The more transformations there are, the more losses occur [214].”

Bloomberg New Energy Finance’s (BNEF) Michael Liebreich had similar concerns in his two lengthy articles titled *Separating Hype from Hydrogen* [154][155]:

> From a public policy perspective, the real killer for H2FC cars is their wind-to-wheel (or solar-to-wheel) inefficiency. Driving a small family car 100km, whether H2FC or BEV, uses 15kWh of motive energy at the wheels. For the BEV, taking into account losses on the grid and in the battery cycle and drive train, that translates into a need to generate 25kWh at the plant where the electricity is generated. The equivalent for the H2FC car, given losses in electrolysis, compression, transport, storage and reconversion of hydrogen, is at least 50kWh. Put simply, hydrogen cars are half as efficient as BEVs – and there is no reason in physics to think that will change. There is reason why [Teslas’s] Elon Musk calls them “fool cell” cars.

“Unbiased” is part of NACFE’s genetics, so we, like Hanley and Liebreich, dug deeper into the topic of producing hydrogen to understand the breadth of the topic.

The process of first creating electrical energy to use to then create green hydrogen in electrolysis to then create electricity back again in the vehicle using a fuel cell is inherently less efficient than using electricity directly in batteries in a vehicle. When then adding in the entire life cycle analysis, the picture gets even more challenging to make a business case on energy efficiency alone. The explanation then of why hydrogen has so much attention is that there is more to the hydrogen story than energy conversion efficiency, more than emissions, more than just the trucks. But efficiency is a major factor.

This is the elephant in the room that needs to be clarified first. Efficiency and emissions are inherently tied together. NACFE has many reports highlighting that improving efficiency directly reduces emissions, and equally, a goal in emissions reductions is to reduce fuel used for freight transport. Reducing fuel use generally improves profitability. natural gas and does produce greenhouse gases (GHG), NOx and particulate emissions. The DOE

Steam-Methane Reforming:

Most hydrogen produced today in the United States is made via steam-methane reforming, a mature 
production process in which high-temperature steam (700°C–1,000°C) is used to produce hydrogen 
from a methane source, such as natural gas. In steam-methane reforming, methane reacts with 
steam under 3–25 bar pressure (1 bar = 14.5 psi) in the presence of a catalyst to produce hydrogen, 
carbon monoxide, and a relatively small amount of carbon dioxide. Steam reforming is 
endothermic—that is, heat must be supplied to the process for the reaction to proceed.

Subsequently, in what is called the "water-gas shift reaction," the carbon monoxide and steam are 
reacted using a catalyst to produce carbon dioxide and more hydrogen. In a final process step called 
"pressure-swing adsorption," carbon dioxide and other impurities are removed from the gas stream, 
leaving essentially pure hydrogen. Steam reforming can also be used to produce hydrogen from 
other fuels, such as ethanol, propane, or even gasoline. [64]

There are multiple technologies in use or in development to help mitigate emissions from SMR-based 
processes. Capturing and storing carbon is an example using a variety of methods falling under the 
umbrella of carbon sequestration or carbon capture [24][25]. These solutions are aimed at capturing 
and/or repurposing a majority of the critical emissions during production of hydrogen so that the system 
approaches zero-emission as illustrated simplistically in the Figure 20 example from the Canadian Energy 
Systems Analysis Research (CESAR) Initiative study by the University of Calgary [22]. These are typically 
branded as blue hydrogen.

## 12 NUCLEAR ENERGY FOR HYDROGEN

power. While nuclear power plants do have complex waste products to deal with such as spent fuel rods

benefits to air quality from nuclear power plants [84][85]. These are described as being similar to RECs.

Jobs, employment, profits, capital recovery, exports, imports, etc. are clearly integral to the discussion 
of hydrogen. Hydrogen is much more complex for NACFE to discuss than deciding if low rolling 
resistance tires or aerodynamic trailer skirts have positive returns on investment. The ROI equations 
have significantly more factors involved well beyond just the fleet’s own operation. Many of the 
potential uses of hydrogen cannot solely on their own justify the tremendous infrastructure investment 
needed to bring the hydrogen supply base to reality. They all need scale, because once at scale, 
hydrogen costs will come down to an acceptable competitive level and the environmental benefits can 
be realized. The transition to scale, however, may require other users to make the big investments to 
help speed the process. 

To put this in perspective, you want to buy an EV car. Your house and your neighborhood have sufficient 
excess margin for you to charge your EV car overnight in your garage. But now five of your neighbors 
have seen you driving your EV car and they too all want one in their garages. The extra electricity grid 
capacity originally designed into your neighborhood’s grid may now be in question. The utilities have to 
decide how and when to upgrade the neighborhood to avoid brown outs and blackouts as more of the 
neighbors want to buy EV cars. It can take months or years for the utilities to upgrade the local 
substation, and your neighborhood may have to pay for the infrastructure upgrades to the grid through 
increases in electricity costs. Now if the local strip mall puts in a high-capacity electric charging station, 
they likely will absorb the infrastructure cost along with the utility of upgrading the local network and all 
your neighborhood supply and cost risk problems may go away. In fact, having some other project carry 
the infrastructure capital costs likely means improved service to your community through new upgraded 
grid equipment and operations, and potentially reduced electricity costs. 

Hydrogen infrastructure is like this; everyone may benefit from hydrogen once it’s available, but paying 
to get the hydrogen there in scale may not be affordable to individual end users. This is especially true if

The political side of hydrogen is also a key factor. Expansion of hydrogen into new markets like

emerging transportation solution inherently requires political momentum. Success for hydrogen 
invariably will require some level of government involvement. In fact, governments already have been 
actively funding research and development in this field for decades.

Venture capital is also key to improving hydrogen’s future for transportation uses. New products require 
factories, employees, infrastructure, etc. that are invested well before the commercial markets see cash

succeed for transportation use.

### 16.3 SCOTLAND BUSES 

The northern region of Scotland is made up of the Orkney Islands. The past economy of the Orkneys was 
largely based on farming and fishing. Oil exploration in the North Sea opened up new paths for jobs and 
revenue. The future for Orkney is tied to green energy, as “wind is perhaps the one constant in Orkney
[136].” The Orkney Wind Farm project is looking to install a significant number of land-based wind 
turbines. Innovations in wind turbine engineering also are making extremely large offshore based wind 
turbines increasingly interesting.

A September 2020 BBC report cites Orkney islands government research that their population of 22,200 
has about 1,500 businesses on the island, 90% of which have fewer than 10 employees, and many of the 
jobs are part time [137]. Only 10 firms employ more than 50 people and the largest has less than 250. 
The opportunity to export products and grow their economy in the past was fairly limited, but the 
opportunity of wind power related industries could open up significant local capital investment. Wind 
power could generate significant amounts of electricity, far more than the islands require. The challenge 
is how to get that to a demand market. Running high voltage power lines under sea vast distances is 
problematic and costly. Molecular fuel though offers a ship transportable product able to access 
markets all over the world. Green wind turbine electricity could power electrolyzer plants to create 
molecular hydrogen, an exportable product that would create local jobs, provide tax funds for 
government local services, and raise the gross domestic product of the islands. The availability of jobs 
and profits could reverse demographic trends and bring new people to the islands. One such project is

## 17 EXAMPLES WHERE HYDROGEN INVESTMENT MAKES SENSE FOR TRUCKS 

NACFE’s “messy middle” for future heavy-duty truck power systems highlights that likely there are going 
to be multiple solutions required to replace traditional diesel. Each of these solutions NACFE concludes 
are optimal for different portions of the duty cycle map. There is no single “silver bullet” solution. Each 
will make better sense in some applications and be worse in others. These may be regionally dependent 
on multiple factors. In the long term, decades from now, NACFE has concluded that electric vehicles will 
dominate heavy-duty truck transport — these include battery electric, a variety of interpretations of 
hybrid electric generically grouped as fuel cells, and catenary electric. These are the only technologies 
currently capable of being zero-emission at both the tailpipe and well-to-wheel. However, the messy 
middle will see multiple hybrids with a variety of energy types, it will include improved diesels, it will 
include the use of renewable versions of fuels like natural gas and diesel, and there are solutions not yet 
thought of that creative researchers may bring to the table. 

NACFE identified a sampling of regional case studies where hydrogen makes sense for trucks.

## 17.3 GERMANY 

Germany is a net importer of energy and plans to be that way into the future. While Germany does have 
native energy sources and has actively supported growth in solar and wind power producing some 40% 
of its electricity demand from renewables in 2019 [163], the country is an industrial giant with a huge 
appetite for energy, beyond its internal ability to provide. Germany in 2018 imported 71% of its energy 
[161]. Germany is ranked as the seventh largest consumer of energy in the world, but the 25th largest 
energy producer, according to the U.S. EIA [164].

Germany adopted the 2030 Climate Action Plan with a goal of carbon neutrality by 2050. Hydrogen is a 
primary path for Germany to meet that goal as outlined in *The National Hydrogen Strategy* published in 
June 2020 [159]. The California Hydrogen Business Council (CHBC) hosted a presentation on this plan in 
September 2020 [160]. Figure 76 shows that the majority of current hydrogen production is gray with 
only 6.5% produced through electrolysis. The majority of the hydrogen today is consumed in 
manufacturing of other materials and refining fuels.

## 17.4 AUTONOMOUS HYDROGEN TRUCKS 

Hydrogen fuel cell trucks are more expensive to build than today’s diesels. That may change in time, but 
for the next decade, the volume of trucks built will be insignificant in terms of being able to bring 
component pricing down. Technology development will make progress, no doubt. And the baseline 
diesel price will undoubtedly go up as GHG Phase II and CARB rules add more technology to get diesel-powered trucks to be cleaner. Still, as capital assets to fleets, the incentive will be to keep trucks running

## 17.5 CHINA 

China is a nation with immense capabilities from the 1.4 billion population, tremendous industrial base, 
significant natural resources, and variety of energy types. They also have a significantly urgent demand 
to improve their environment. The Chinese government has the ability to mandate change in ways that 
other nations cannot. Economics become less critical to policy, or alternatively, environmental factors 
are more intensely monetized in decision making in China.

China, like the Scotland example, is looking at a regional directive toward promoting hydrogen. China is 
investing heavily in hydrogen fuel cell vehicles and signed a deal with Siemens for a 1 MW green 
hydrogen production facility in Beijing [200]. It is also offering significant subsidies to build out the value 
chain, including production and distribution. China has experience in promoting technology growth. 
“China introduced purchasing subsidies for electric vehicles in 2009. Resulting sales increases helped 
automakers boost development of the vehicles. Now China is the world's largest market for electric

## 18.3 WATER 

NACFE would be remiss to not highlight that while hydrogen is the most abundant atom in the universe, 
water, H2O, is not always abundant. California, Texas and other locations periodically go through 
extended severe drought conditions, requiring water use restrictions on industries and the public. 

Green hydrogen comes from breaking down water through use of electricity in an electrolyzer, 
essentially running a fuel cell in reverse. This process consumes water at the location of the electrolyzer. 
Later when the hydrogen is being used to power a fuel cell, some amount of water is returned to the 
environment by way of moisture in the exhaust system. There is also water consumed in the process of 
making the electricity needed to power the electrolyzer. Electric power plants generally have cooling 
system requirements that are based in part on water. Blue hydrogen production also involves the use of 
water in various processes. 

Recognizing that water use is inherently part of producing energy for transportation uses, the DOE 
began including life-cycle analysis of water consumption as part of the Argonne GREET life cycle analysis 
tool. GREET stands for Greenhouse Gases, Regulated Emissions, and Energy Use in Transportation [227]. 

There are a wide range of influences and factors impacting the estimation of the water consumption 
tied to creating hydrogen, far too much for this report to dive into. However, suffice it to say that 
estimates on the required amount of water to produce hydrogen vary from 2 gallons per kg H2 to 
perhaps 9 gallons per kg H2. It’s important to note that some amount of this water returns as vapor in 
the exhaust system of a fuel cell vehicle, and some is cooling water returned to the environment at 
power facilities. The DOE has funded research on Life-Cycle Analysis of Water Consumption for 
Hydrogen Production with annual Merit Review reports starting in 2016 [228]. Other researchers such as 
Michael Webber, Associate Director at the Center for International Energy and Environmental Policy at 
the University of Texas at Austin, published research on *The Water Intensity of the Transitional 
Hydrogen Economy* in 2007 [229][230]. 

Hydrogen in transportation through powering fuel cells can displace water from one location where it is 
used in an electrolyzer to make hydrogen, to another, where it is emitted as vapor from a tailpipe, to 
hopefully return as rain. This could, for example, take potable water from Los Angeles, California, turn it 
into hydrogen gas at an electrolyzer plant in Los Angeles, then the hydrogen could power a fuel cell 
truck driving to Phoenix, Arizona, with the moisture exiting to the atmosphere from the tail pipe all 
along the trip. 

An unintended consequence of a hydrogen future might be exacerbating local water shortages during 
severe droughts. This may be similarly problematic in those drought periods in context to using corn to 
make ethanol, where alternative uses for the corn might be feeding livestock or ultimately feeding 
people.

## 19 COMPARISON FACTORS 

Comparing a hydrogen fuel cell vehicle to anything else inevitably does not serve well the depth of 
alternatives presented by fuel cells. Just as there is really no average diesel truck, there definitely will be 
no average fuel cell truck. The complexity of battery dominant to fuel cell dominant means there will be 
a variety of vehicles with substantially different factors, likely leading to apples-to-oranges comparisons. 
Similarly, even within one particular configuration of the hybrid fuel cell vehicle, there likely will be 
significantly different option content tuned for fleet-specific duty cycles. Over-simplification further 
complicates comparison to battery electric vehicles as these will similarly have a wide range of option 
content differences, as there also is no “one” battery electric truck model. 

Product line simplification has been a recurring goal for new model introductions, but the North 
American truck market is particularly complicated because there are vast differences in duty cycles, 
environments, loads and operations by fleets. 

Comparing “a” fuel cell truck to “a” diesel truck to “a” battery electric truck also implies they can be 
specified identically. This also does a disservice to all three types of vehicles because each has its own 
advantages and disadvantages, and likely truly equivalent specifications will not be produced. For 
example, a day cab may have two 70-gallon diesel fuel tanks allowing it to go possibly 1,000 miles 
between fill-ups, but operationally, the truck only drives 300 miles a day and likely refills the tank more 
frequently. The battery electric version of this truck will recharge nightly so only needs a 300-mile range 
capacity. The hydrogen version of this truck may be optimized to fill daily as well to keep hydrogen fill 
times shorter. Current diesel truck makers have a significant number of options available on each truck 
and possible permutations of configurations easily can get into the hundreds of thousands.

## 19.2 ENERGY STORAGE SYSTEMS 

Diesel fuel tanks are typically aluminum, steel or sometimes plastic. They come in cylindrical and 
rectangular prism shapes primarily. They operate essentially at atmospheric pressure. Diesel is a liquid, 
and while it can change volume slightly due to temperature, liquids are considered incompressible. 
Hydrogen in gaseous form is, however, compressible, meaning you can put more of it in the same size 
container just by increasing the pressure. For this reason, a gallon of hydrogen gas has very little 
meaning unless the pressure is also stated.

Compressing gas for truck engines is nothing new. Compressed natural gas (CNG) is an example used in 
trucks today, where CNG on the vehicle may be stored at between 2,400 to 3,600 psi. Hydrogen on 
trucks may be stored at 5,000 to 10,000 psi. The industry prefers to show this in Bar units, or barometric 
pressure equivalents, so these are 350 Bar and 700 Bar. Using the Bar system may be a marketing effort

<!-- page: 101 -->

Making Sense of Heavy-Duty Hydrogen Fuel Cell Tractors

December 16, 2020 101

to avoid focusing on the extremely high pressures when stated as psi. Whether in psi or Bar, or MPa, this 
is very high pressure. A typical car tire has 35 psi, and a truck tire is at 110 psi. 

The magic of gas is that you can double the stored energy in a tank by doubling the pressure, while 
keeping the tank the exact same internal size. It originates from the physics ideal gas law:

PV = nRT

Without diving too deep into college physics, P is gas pressure, V is gas volume, T is temperature, R is a 
constant, and n is related to the number of molecules of hydrogen. The proportional relationship is that 
if you double the amount of hydrogen on the right side of the equation, it doubles the pressure on the 
left side of the equation if the volume is held constant.

The exterior of the tank may grow in dimensions to handle the increased pressure. Carbon fiber has 
become a material of choice to use in hydrogen tanks for vehicles. Carbon fiber has the strength of 
steels yet is 10%-30% lighter for the same performance. They can be three to five times more energy 
intensive to fabricate than conventional steel, according to the DOE group that evaluates and promotes 
lightweight material manufacturing and use, the Advanced Manufacturing Office (AMO) [190]. There are 
cost increases with using carbon fiber over steel, as lightweight materials generally carry cost premiums 
since they are more expensive in energy, time and effort to make. 

The DOE AMO describes manufacturing carbon fiber [190]:

> *FRP composite manufacturing can be an energy-intensive process with high heat and pressure 
needed to bond the composite material together. In addition, carbon fiber precursors and the raw 
materials that make up the polymer resin are typically made via conventional energy intensive 
petrochemical processes.*

maximum weight, and many never see maximum weight loadings. But some do. For example, one major 
national fleet NACFE interviewed discussed that 80% of their loads are below maximum weight, only 
20% are at maximum weight. However, the challenge is that their trucks may need to be ready to haul at 
80,000 lbs. GVW the day after hauling 65,000 lbs. 

Other fleet duty cycles have more predictable loads, like furniture haulers. E-Commerce packages also 
tend to cube out regularly due to less dense packaging of individual shipments. Others, like on-highway 
beverage transporters and bulk haulers, may regularly hit maximum weights. The statistical nature of 
the majority of loadings tends toward averages in the range of 65,000 lbs. GVW. Also keep in mind that 
deadheading — pulling empty trailers — occurs and can be from 10% to as much as 50% of the time 
depending on the fleet because trailers are not always where they need to be.

## 19.7 INCENTIVES 

An unknown factor with respect to net cost paid out the door by a fleet is incentives, grants, tax 
benefits, and credits. In some markets, buying and operating a fuel cell vehicle may generate cash flow 
as a marketable credit or offset. This occurs today where Tesla can market emission credits to other car 
manufacturers allowing them to produce cars with higher emission levels. These types of credits exist at 
the truck level under the Average, Banking and Trading rules in the EPA/NHTSA Green House Gas 
regulations. Still other credit systems at state levels may also exist, like California’s Low Carbon Fuel 
Standard (LCFS) and cap-and-trade programs. These are market variables that may significantly change 
cost equations for fleets, dealers and OEMs allowing net vehicle pricing to be adjusted. States with 
established emission reduction programs and goals also will want to incentivize purchases of zero-emission vehicles. 

The ramp up of production of fuel cell vehicles means that high volumes will not be achieved for some 
years. During that ramp up, incentives and grants are likely to be readily available to fleets. Local, state 
and national level incentives will vary based on the winds of politics, and funding may originate from 
both private and public sources, but NACFE expects that incentives and grants will be an ongoing factor 
for fuel cell vehicles for the next decade. Additionally, hydrogen fuel production likely will be subsidized 
by private and public funding during that time, which will temper fuel costs.

## 19.8 MAINTENANCE 

Estimating maintenance costs for fledgling heavy-duty fuel cell tractors depends on a large number of 
assumptions. Today there are a handful of prototype Class 8 tractors in operation expertly put together 
by OEMs and fuel cell manufacturers from available parts. These are not representative of production 
level trucks. The saying, “they came with an engineer in the glove box” comes to mind. These are proof 
of concept vehicles that are fleet deployment level demonstrations for OEMs and fleets to learn and 
guide production designs. Experience with production level buses is as close to reality for available data 
today. As with all new technologies, there are rapid learning curves. Fuel cell buses have shown 
improvement in vehicle uptime in the programs cited in this report, but they still are not at the same 
level as the mature diesels they replace. Volume production and fleet deployments will cure this over 
time as OEMs iterate and mature the technologies for their actual duty cycles. 

NACFE has confidence that fuel cells can be equivalent to diesels in uptime in a reasonable time span, 
and ultimately will be better than diesels due to fewer failure modes from moving parts. Friction and

## 19.10 SAFETY 

Safety invariably is required for any transportation fueling system. The market will not accept unsafe 
products. Isolated accidents may percolate into major delays in market penetration, so companies are 
heavily vested in providing safe vehicles in early production models. Hydrogen brings with it a number of 
potentially significant risks. Hyundai has information on how it has focused on making hydrogen cars 
safe in *What Makes Fuel Cell Electric Vehicles Safe [207]*? Battery electric vehicles carry similar safety 
concerns. New technologies bring with them new opportunities to discover failure modes, but the 
industry understands the potential financial risks failure can cause and takes steps to minimize them.

That said, NACFE has one primary concern with hydrogen safety and fleet operations, that hydrogen-based tractors may not be viable for all routes in the U.S. or Canada due to unacceptable levels of risk in 
locations such as the Eisenhower Tunnel in Colorado or other tunnels and enclosed spaces like 
warehouses or underground facilities. The challenge is that transporting highly combustible fuels is
sometimes restricted on routes. Fuel haulers have additional rules to follow. A hydrogen fuel cell truck is 
hauling not only a highly combustible fuel, it is hauling a 10,000 psi storage container. 

The combination of these two in a 1999 Mont Blanc tunnel type accident, no matter how many 
precautions are engineered in, may still be considered too risky. (For reference – the Mont Blanc tunnel 
is a 7-mile-long single bore double tunnel under the Alps connecting France and Italy. A truck carrying 
margarine and flour stopped and caught fire in the tunnel. The resulting conflagration consumed the 
truck, cargo, fuel, trailer and other vehicles and overwhelmed emergency systems and personnel, taking 
two days to extinguish and reaching an estimated 1000°C temperatures (1,832°F) [226]. 

A further modern element of concern is intentional use of these vehicles as weapons in terrorism. This 
risk is likely similar to that faced by fuel haulers, which may necessitate additional driver certification 
and background checks for hydrogen powered tractors.

## 19.12 FUEL TAXING 

Diesel and gasoline vehicles help fund road maintenance and other operating costs for infrastructure

## 19.13 OTHER OPERATIONAL FACTORS 

The primary factors favoring hydrogen fuel cells are:

* When the hydrogen is produced from green energy sources such as wind, solar or hydro power, 
the vehicles can be true zero-emission transports.

where that is relevant to fleet operations.

current diesels, so freight carried and distances traveled may be similar.

potentially better for averaging out variables such as seasonal demand and supply mismatches.

# 20 SUMMARY FINDINGS 

There are many variables — dominoes that need to fall — to accurately predict the future for fuel cell 
vehicles. The technology is promising and the massive level of investment is encouraging. However, 
factors are beyond just the realm of technology, beyond engineering. And fuel cells have competition. In 
some ways this is a horse race to the future, the zero-emission horse race, with a pack of different 
hybrid electric and pure battery electric horses. Throw in a net zero-emission future, and a wide range of 
additional competitors join the race.

Hydrogen fuel cells have been used as early as the 1960s in spacecraft. In the 1970s, companies such as 
Rocketdyne investigated potential commercial transportation uses for fuel cells including trains, ships, 
buses and trucks funded by the Department of Transportation [20]. Companies, including Ikea, Walmart, 
The Home Depot and others, have been employing stationary fuel cells for on-site power generation, 
[19]. Forklifts are another market segment successfully using fuel cells. Automotive production models 
in North America such as the Toyota Mirai and Honda Clarity are using hydrogen fuel cells. The Clarity 
was initially introduced into California in limited numbers in 2008 while the Mirai began there in 2015. 
Bus fleets began experimentation with fuel cell powertrains as early as 1994, and larger scale fleet use 
of fuel cell buses began in 2010 with companies such as New Flyer Industries. 

Prototype heavy-duty hydrogen fuel cell trucks have been in demonstration since before 2011 in fleets 
such as Total Transportation Services (TTSI) in southern California which has had a variety of makes in 
use. Production Class 8 fuel cell trucks in North America as of 2020 are currently only in development as 
prototypes, or offered as retrofits by a variety of aftermarket installers in concert with companies such 
as Ballard, US Hybrid, Meritor/TransPower, Toyota and Cummins. These trucks are in testing or expected 
to be in testing with fleets such as UPS, Toyota Logistics, TTSI and Southern Counties Express, all in the 
Los Angeles basin region. Truck manufacturers such as Kenworth and Nikola currently have a handful of 
demonstration units in use with production startup unknown yet for Kenworth, and Nikola expects 
production start in the 2023 timeframe. Europe and Asia are seeing implementations of hydrogen fuel 
cell vehicles. 

Buses and cars powered by fuel cells are in use today. There are clear vested interests in producing 
hydrogen, and a variety of end uses that can benefit from its availability. Hydrogen for transportation 
use is only a small part of a much bigger hydrogen economy that must be planned for. In the past, a fleet 
might think it could consider a natural gas truck versus a diesel truck, with only slight consideration of 
other factors. A hydrogen economy requires a regional focus on providing hydrogen as energy to 
multiple end uses, of which transportation may benefit. Once hydrogen production at scale is available, 
these multiple end uses become economically viable. The challenge is getting the hydrogen production 
at scale. 

In diesel terms, diesel trucks were not viable for cross country use until the diesel refineries, pipelines 
and storage were operating and the U.S. highway system was in place to support growth. Isolated

## 20.1 HYDROGEN PRODUCTION 

Hydrogen in some ways is a solution looking for a problem. It has been characterized as “the future of 
fuel and always will be.” It is clear that once hydrogen production is in place, multiple opportunities exist 
to make use of it in industry, for power generation, in storage applications, and for transportation. While 
hydrogen may be the most abundant element in the universe, it does not exist without processing. 

There are many competing methods to make hydrogen, labeled variously in colors black, gray, brown, 
pink, turquois, blue and more. Each has its positives and negatives, and many have the potential of 
being classified as low- or nearly zero-emission with respect to greenhouse gas. 

A few methods can be considered green, for example electrolyzers separating hydrogen from water 
using electricity generated by wind turbines, hydroelectric power plants or solar panels. Green here 
means the production of the hydrogen generates no emissions. 

These green methods will compete with processes that can claim to be nearly emission free, by 
capturing the emissions through a variety of methods so they do not enter the atmosphere. These are 
labeled blue. 

Both green and blue hydrogen have the same theoretical end goal of zero-emissions to the atmosphere 
in the process of creating the hydrogen. This is where politics and societal factors must be considered. 
Blue hydrogen perpetuates the use of carbon-based fuels in a “net zero” approach to managing 
emissions whereas green hydrogen builds a true zero-emission approach. 

This gets into regional geographic differences. A region such as Alberta, Canada, rich in natural gas, may 
find advantages in pursuing blue hydrogen to create a thriving economy with the potential to export 
hydrogen to other regions. A region like California with an abundance of solar and wind power may 
want to pursue green hydrogen. 

Since both these methods offer near zero-emissions, both help these regions meet their emission 
reduction goals, but they get there through different routes. 

The bulk of hydrogen production today is done through steam methane reformation, SMR, a process 
built off of using natural gas. The production is termed as gray, meaning it is an improvement in 
emissions from coal-based processes, but still has emissions, both CO2 and methane, which contribute 
to global warming. SMR has evolved through market forces to become the primary method of producing

hydrogen because it is the least costly method. As environmental factors are increasingly monetized and 
become factors in economic decision making, blue hydrogen processes will evolve these SMR processes 
by adding systems to capture and repurpose the emissions. This will keep SMR-based processes working 
well into the future.

Electrolysis, the process of separating hydrogen from water through electricity, is fundamentally green, 
but electrolysis has no idea where the electricity originates. The fungible nature of America’s electricity 
grid means that electricity could come from green solar, wind and hydropower, or it could come from a 
nuclear plant, a gas-fired power plant, a coal-fired power plant, or combinations of all of them. 

A variety of credit and incentive systems are in place or can be envisioned for incentivizing greener

region, but locally, their power may still be actually originating from a gas-fired power plant. And those 
plants may find ways to be classified as near zero-emission as well in time again through capture and 
repurposing of the emissions. 

The variety of ways to produce hydrogen will fuel competition and innovation. Regional political and 
societal forces also may weigh heavily on which solutions advance in specific locations. Inertia from 
existing market dominance of production processes is also a factor. The only sure conclusion is that 
hydrogen production is likely to increase, making it available for multiple uses. 

If you build hydrogen production, uses will come. Transportation is one of those possible uses.

## 20.2 FUEL CELL TRUCKS 

Experience with hydrogen fuel cells as the “engine” of vehicles has advanced for more than 20 years. 
Several of the companies that make fuel cells for vehicles have been doing so for that period of time. 
High-pressure tank technology also has advanced considerably. The confidence in the core technologies 
for fuel cell vehicles has increased significantly through deployments of fuel cell buses and cars. Safety 
of these systems always has been a focus of the work and the track record of fuel cell vehicles is as good 
as or better than their diesel and gasoline counterparts. 

The primary question is are the technologies robust enough for heavy-duty truck use? The fuel cell and 
high-pressure tank manufacturers believe so. A number of OEM truck manufacturers now have 
prototypes in real-world operations to evaluate this. One manufacturer recently put production level 
heavy-duty trucks into operation in Switzerland. Where diesel heavy-duty trucks have had more than 
100 years of maturation, heavy-duty fuel cell trucks are just starting out. They are in the part of the 
learning curve where multiple issues may surface in field use that did not occur in the laboratory or on 
the test tracks. Numbers and time, along with market forces, will accelerate corrections and 
improvements. While the technology is not new, the application is new, and industry should understand 
there will be a learning curve. All new technologies in trucking have had learning curves. The greater the 
industry involvement in the early vehicles, the faster those learning curves occur and improvement of 
the technology can occur.

## 20.3 COSTS 

Costs of technology are likely to be offset and subsidized for the foreseeable future to promote use. The 
“out the door cost” to fleets for these vehicles will likely be comparable to existing diesel products for 
some years to come, perhaps decades. Cost of infrastructure for fleets similarly likely will be subsidized 
to help in adoption of hydrogen for transportation. 

The volume of hydrogen fuel cell heavy-duty trucks today in North American operations is measured in 
tens of vehicles. There are some 1.8 million tractors in use pulling freight today. It will take some years 
for manufacturers to build and the marketplace to absorb truly high numbers of trucks, when a typical 
year of production for the industry is between 200,000 and 300,000 vehicles overall. NACFE does not 
believe today’s cost estimates for fuel cell trucks reflect production or market conditions in a decade. 
Volume adoption of fuel cells will force mass production techniques and concurrent cost reduction 
efforts, while continuous field operation feedback will improve product quality and reliability. 

Once large scale hydrogen production is available for new uses such as greening up industry, power 
generation, power storage, and transportation, then energy costs will reduce as demand and production 
increase. 

Hydrogen may never actually directly compete on cost with diesel. A market that has mandated zero-emission vehicles will not have diesel as a comparison. The baseline will be battery electric vehicles and 
the price of electricity.

Those future markets that allow both diesel and hydrogen may see decreasing costs to diesel as demand 
decreases whiles supplies increase. In a net zero or near zero future, more transportation may have 
migrated to battery electric and alternative fuels such that diesel production greatly exceeds demand, a 
condition that has generally depressed diesel prices in the past. Energy pricing is never a static point, all 
energy paths will have pricing impacted by supply and demand market forces.

Hydrogen pricing will drop over time, partly through innovation and scaling demand, but more likely due 
to competition between the multiple methods of producing hydrogen, green, blue, gray, pink, turquoise 
and other colors to come. NACFE equates this to a horse race where positioning can impact speed. 
Those in the lead will have a free track while those in the pack have to jockey for openings and may 
struggle to break free to open ground. The bright spot in all this is that future availability of hydrogen is 
not really in question, just how it might be made.

## 20.4 SMART USES 

The efficiency of the use of hydrogen in transportation will be a major topic area as the hydrogen future 
evolves. NACFE has identified that there will be efficient and less efficient ways to produce, deliver and 
use hydrogen. The vehicles themselves, once in production, will mature over time, with efficiency of the 
vehicle systems constantly being scrutinized and improved just as diesel engine trucks have evolved over 
time. Newer technologies and designs will invariably be better than older ones, forcing decisions on 
obsoleting older vehicles or repurposing them to other duty cycles. Components like batteries, high 
pressure tanks, high voltage cables and connectors may have design lives much longer than the vehicles 
themselves, opening up secondary markets for parts and increasing salvage value for older vehicles. 
NACFE finds the future for hydrogen and hydrogen vehicles will have many opportunities for innovation.

# 21 RECOMMENDATIONS 

1). Fleet investment in new fuel cell electric and battery electric vehicles requires vehicles. The debate 
on infrastructure is irrelevant if there is no demand. The chicken-and-egg arguments are meaningless. 
OEMs need to be producing trucks. 

Demand always has driven infrastructure investment. Very rarely has infrastructure led demand in the 
North American markets. Major infrastructure development such as the Interstate Highway System, the 
Electrification of the U.S., the telephone system, the national railroad network, the national cellphone 
network, all originated from demand created by products available to consumers. Cars and trucks, 
lightbulbs and refrigerators, telephones, steam trains, cellphones and smartphones, all were available 
products demanding infrastructure improvement. 

2). Hydrogen is a regional solution, cheap hydrogen even more so. The ability to scale truck demand will 
be limited by the nature of truck production volumes and market forces. It may take a decade to get to 
100,000 hydrogen-powered vehicles. Two or more decades to get to a million. Cheap hydrogen requires 
a broader investment base now. Industrial uses need to be accelerated for transportation to benefit. 
Transportation should not be the lead argument for hydrogen, just a benefactor of it. The numbers are 
just not there for transportation alone to push scaling hydrogen production. We need to broaden the 
focus of hydrogen in the U.S. 

3). Purists pushing green hydrogen as a goal should accept that some compromise is needed to allow 
alternatives in the “how” of getting to net-zero emissions. Governments, advocacy groups, companies, 
fleets and the public need to come to agreement that either “net zero” is the goal or “zero” is the goal, 
because these are significantly different for hydrogen. Net zero allows for innovations in a number of 
energy streams to provide hydrogen in ways that do not let emissions into the environment. True-zero 
emissions on the other hand forces unrealistically rapid growth on solar, wind and hydro to carry the 
massive electrical load needed for a hydrogen future. Clarify the goal as “net zero” and accept that the 
only path to providing rapid scaling of a hydrogen economy must allow environmentally responsible 
alternative paths to innovate solutions. There is a “messy middle” in hydrogen’s future to deal with 
ramping up supply and demand in parallel to meet net zero 2050 timelines.

4). Stop comparisons of fill times. Fill times are moving targets for both fuel cell and battery electric 
vehicles. In a zero-emission future world, as in California’s plans, diesel does not exist. What is the value 
of a comparison on fill times? CharIN and PRYHDE are both working to make fast energy replenishment 
a reality for battery and hydrogen-based vehicles. Optimized fleet operations likely will be different for 
these two versus ICE-based vehicles that burn fuel. The drop-in-replacement paradigm, where fleets are 
just looking at replacing one-for-one vehicles, for these two new technologies limits taking advantage of 
their strong points versus ICE. 

New technologies bring with them new opportunities – a jet airplane allowed longer, faster commercial 
flights with lower maintenance issues. Diesel-electric trains allowed significant reductions in 
maintenance and required less maintenance facilities than coal or oil powered steam trains. Smart 
phones completely eclipsed just making phone calls with a handheld device. Quite likely all three truck 
powertrains, hydrogen, battery electric and diesel will be in use by fleets in the messy middle future. 
Fleets gaining experience with these new technologies will find new ways of using them to maximize

# 22 CONCLUSIONS 

Heavy-duty trucks powered by a combination of fuel cells and batteries applying power to the ground 
through electric motors are just starting to see real-world use. Estimates of costs, reliability, 
performance, comparisons to alternatives, etc. are all speculation at this point. The path forward has 
innumerable variables beyond just estimates on the technology. Decisions will likely be regional and/or 
national, with considerations that include political factors, jobs, resources, potential export or import 
requirements, availability of key resources like water, pipelines, underground storage facilities, and 
more. Fleets are somewhat pawns in a much bigger chess game.

NACFE believes that the baseline comparison, in time, will not be to internal combustion-based vehicles. 
A zero-emission future will not have internal combustion vehicles, so using them as a baseline becomes 
irrelevant. Today, fleets need to consider comparing a multitude of alternatives in their next purchase of 
trucks. The near-term future in some regions will have many fewer possible alternatives due to growing 
zero-emission goals. Those ICE vehicles still allowed in those regions may face significant economic and 
regulatory challenges to operate. 

The baseline for comparison eventually will be between battery electric vehicles and hybrid electric 
ones, primarily fuel cells. These types of vehicles tend to optimize better for different parts of the duty 
cycle envelope. Fleets will need to phrase the questions as “can the battery electric vehicle do the job?” 
If it can, electric vehicles will have lower operating costs than fuel cell ones because battery electric 
vehicles are two to three times more efficient at using energy to move. If the answer is no, then the 
choice will be a fuel cell truck. Catenary wire trucking is an alternative, but less likely in U.S. and 
Canadian spaces than perhaps portions of Europe.

Fleets need to understand that just like with diesels, there are many different ways to spec a fuel cell 
truck, covering the range from fuel cell dominant to battery dominant. There is no one fuel cell vehicle. 
Avoid grouping them all into an average as that average will be meaningless. These vehicles will be 
purpose built for specific duty cycles. A one-size-fits-all truck solution designed for a worst-case duty 
cycle will likely not perform as well in all duty cycles as a portfolio of purpose-built vehicles could.

Fuel cell vehicles may disturb the trade-in paradigm. Vehicles may be leased only, or rented. Trade 
cycles may be lengthened. Second owners may become rarer. As these purpose-built vehicles evolve to 
better meet their first owner use, they may become less useful for secondary markets. Put this in 
perspective of a fuel cell bus today which may have no secondary marketplace beyond salvage.

Hydrogen fuel cell heavy-duty trucks are technically viable. The technology has been going through 
decades of maturation on buses, cars and industrial uses. Adapting fuel cells to heavy-duty trucks will 
invariably have a learning curve as do all new technologies, but fuel cells are much further along in 
production use and are not a brand new technology. The primary manufacturers of fuel cells have been 
in business for decades. While they will face new start-ups due to the immense amounts of money going 
into hydrogen, those start-ups will need to compete out of the box with well proven technology.

The questions are not so much about the vehicles, nor so much about the fueling, rather they reside 
primarily on the creation of hydrogen itself. The path forward may see hydrogen created through 
traditional gray SMR, a variety of environmentally responsible blue methods, and environmentally ideal 
green methods. The production will be relevant to a wide range of end users, not just transportation. In 
fact, transportation alone likely is not a sufficiently sized market to bring the industrial scale needed to 
make the greatest improvements in costs. Industrial scale use of hydrogen to displace carbon-based 
processes in chemical and materials industries will be needed. Once built, hydrogen production at scale 
opens the door to multiple uses. A coalition of support and commitment across these users is needed to 
guarantee the massive capital investments on regional levels will add value. Compromises between 
groups will be needed as this coalition of support will invariably disagree on the details while supporting 
the broader goal of getting to a net zero future. The demand for environmentally responsible energy will 
be significant. This coalition will need to allow room for the “better” solutions while also pursuing the 
“perfect” ones.

Finally, the potential for autonomous fuel cell trucks to operate 24 hours a day will be appealing for 
some duty cycles and may add significant opportunity for making sense of capital and operational 
investment in hydrogen.
