# 1 INTRODUCTION
The commercial freight industry is navigating the Messy Middle of decarbonization, and fleet managers are facing unprecedented complexity in their vehicle procurement decisions. This report is designed to guide fleet owners through the financial realities of this transition by analyzing the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) across four distinct commercial powertrains: legacy diesel, compressed natural gas/renewable natural gas (CNG/RNG), battery electric (BEV), and hydrogen fuel cell (FCEV). See Figure 1.

By combining real-world operational data from Run on Less – Messy Middle with industry trends, this report provides baseline TCO estimates across multiple duty cycles and forward-looking timeframes. Importantly, the North American Council for Freight Efficiency’s (NACFE) estimates are not intended to be a final, universal ruling for every fleet, but rather a starting point for discussion. The true value of this report lies in its comprehensive framework — arming fleet managers with the exact variables they need to calculate their own bespoke TCOs and, ultimately, beat NACFE’s estimated timelines for achieving cost parity with diesel. 

Beyond the fleets themselves, this framework is designed to assist the entire ecosystem supporting them — from OEMs and infrastructure providers to utilities and policymakers — by providing the data-driven variables needed to estimate costs accurately and align on the true drivers of parity.

## 1.1 Purpose & Context
While the commercial transportation industry has no shortage of TCO calculators and theoretical models, this NACFE report is unique in seven specific ways:
1. **Focuses Exclusively on Class 8:** The study team has isolated the analysis to heavy-duty vehicles, stripping out light- and medium-duty trucks to tackle arguably the hardest sector to decarbonize.
2. **Includes Multiple Fuel Choices:** The study team’s analysis includes diesel, renewable diesel, CNG/RNG, blended biofuels, battery electric, and FCEV powertrain considerations.
3. **Grounds Assumptions in Real-World Data:** NACFE’s baseline inputs are heavily informed by the actual, on-the-ground performance metrics captured during the Run on Less demonstrations.

<!-- page: 20 -->
4. **Forecasts Out To 2035:** The study team provides TCO estimates for three different timeframes: Now (2025), Soon (2028), and Later (2035), to help fleets plan procurement strategies for the next decade of equipment lifecycles.
5. **Accounts For Duty Cycles and Utilization:** The study team analyzed how daily mileage acts as the critical denominator in the TCO equation, demonstrating how different duty cycles allow fleets to amortize high upfront capital costs through operational savings at different rates.
6. **Highlights The Variables That Matter:** Rather than just providing a static number, NACFE points fleet owners directly to the specific TCO variables they must actively manage to drive down costs, and provide actions they can take today to do so.
7. **Excludes Incentives:** NACFE took a very conservative approach by assuming zero incentives for its TCO calculations. This provides a worst-case scenario that reveals when parity occurs organically through technology improvements and operational savings.

## 1.2 Study Objectives
Transitioning to zero-emission freight movement is as much a financial and operational challenge as it is an environmental one. While support for zero-emission transportation from regulators and environmentally conscious customers is growing, NACFE recognizes that these external pressures are not enough to drive widespread adoption; ultimately, the transition must be cost-driven to be sustainable for the industry.

Recognizing this, the objectives of this study are to:
* Clarify the full economic costs of deploying different powertrains in today’s market and tomorrow's.
* Identify which specific TCO components matter most, allowing fleets to focus their management efforts where they will have the highest impact.
* Offer a practical framework for fleet owners to calculate bespoke TCOs for their own operations, ultimately helping them decide which powertrain belongs on which route over time.

## 1.3 Why an Average TCO Gives an Incomplete Picture
Statistician George Box famously noted that “all models are wrong, but some are useful.” This report is built with that philosophy in mind. It is critical to note that the figures presented in this report are national baseline estimates. In reality, TCO must be a bespoke calculation for each fleet. Highly variable factors — such as hyper-local electricity rates, fleet-specific infrastructure strategies, and negotiated vehicle prices — mean that a national average will rarely reflect a specific fleet's actual balance sheet.

Because of this variability, economic parity between diesel and emerging technologies is not a perfectly fixed future date. Rather, it is an operational window defined by a fleet's specific duty cycles, local utility rates, and asset utilization. By aggressively managing these variables, proactive fleet managers can beat NACFE’s baseline estimates and achieve cost parity ahead of the curve.

A strict TCO model also does not calculate the Total Operating Economy (TOE). While TCO focuses on minimizing cost, TOE evaluates the revenue-generating potential of an asset. A higher-TCO zero-emission vehicle (ZEV) actually might generate higher overall profitability if it unlocks exclusive freight contracts, premium rates, or guaranteed volumes from shippers with corporate sustainability goals.

While the study team strongly encourages fleets to adapt this framework to their specific operational realities, NACFE has modeled baseline TCO estimates across each powertrain and duty cycle to serve as an industry

* **Diesel:** Diesel remains the incumbent benchmark across all duty cycles, but its long-term cost advantage is eroding. While fuel costs per mile are projected to drop slightly by 2035, the overall TCO actually rises. This is driven by an expected increase in maintenance costs, insurance premiums, and the rising operational burden of maintaining legacy internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles under increasingly stringent emissions regulations. The March 2026 price shock, where diesel jumped nearly $1.00 per gallon in a single week, further highlights the ongoing budgetary risk of this powertrain [22]. Renewable diesel and blended diesel based on biofuels also are emerging as viable, lower emission alternatives to fossil diesel.
* **CNG/RNG:** Compressed natural gas remains a stable middle ground, providing a lower TCO than diesel in the 2028 and 2035 timeframes. Because CNG avoids the steep insurance and maintenance escalators projected for diesel in 2035, it serves as a viable near-term hedge against diesel's rising operational costs, without the intense infrastructure requirements of zero-emission alternatives. However, it is eventually overtaken by the aggressive cost-reduction curve of BEVs by 2035. RNG, based on recovering and repurposing waste methane, also is growing in significance. Approximately 80% of all on-road fuel used in natural gas vehicles in 2023 was RNG [1].
* **BEVs:** Battery-electric vehicles show the most aggressive TCO improvement of any technology. BEVs move from being one of the more expensive options in 2025 to becoming the definitive market leader in regional cycles by 2035. In fact, BEVs achieve the lowest net TCO in every single duty cycle by 2035, driven by plunging battery costs and superior energy efficiency.
* **FCEV:** While hydrogen vehicle and fuel costs are projected to drop significantly over the next decade, FCEVs remain the most expensive powertrain across almost all duty cycles in NACFE’s 2035 modeling. The viability of FCEVs is almost entirely tethered to the delivery price of hydrogen produced by a variety of processes and carries a massive sensitivity range of $4.00 to $15.00/kg, as well as a lack of scalable infrastructure.

## 1.5 Key Findings by Duty Cycle
Trucks operate in a variety of duty cycles. The following is NACFE's definition of the duty cycles in which Class 8 commercial vehicles typically operate.

* **Long-Haul Over-the-Road (OTR):** Diesel remains the cost leader through 2028, but BEVs achieve parity and narrowly win the segment by 2035. The current $0.61 per mile cost gap favoring diesel is projected to flip by 2035, with BEVs ultimately undercutting diesel by $0.09 per mile. The math in this cycle is being fundamentally changed by the emergence of 500+ mile range BEVs. In this duty cycle, CNG/RNG serves as a decent replacement for diesel due to range and payload capabilities required OTR trucking while avoiding the rising maintenance and compliance costs of diesel. Additionally, the high fuel consumption of this duty cycle makes it the primary arena for FCEV, which narrows its gap to diesel significantly by the mid-2030s.
* **Regional Linehaul:** This duty cycle represents the primary shift in the model. By 2035, BEVs will transition from being twice as expensive as diesel today to becoming the most cost-effective powertrain overall ($0.94/mile vs. $1.00/mile for diesel). Notably, by 2035, the TCO for diesel, CNG/RNG, and FCEV all begin to converge within a narrow band. This suggests that the long-term choice between these three options will be driven more by infrastructure availability — such as local grid capacity or regional hydrogen hubs — than by underlying per-mile economics.
* **Regional Return-to-Base (RTB):** RTB represents the strongest economic case for electrification. By 2035, BEVs will provide a 12% TCO advantage over diesel ($1.13/mile for BEV vs. $1.29/mile for diesel). This segment is the “Goldilocks” scenario for BEVs, benefiting from the industry’s broader move toward regional hub-and-spoke models. Drivers being home daily allows for predictable, overnight depot charging, securing the lowest possible energy rates.
* **Urban/Metro Pickup & Delivery (P&D):** In 2025, ZEVs are severely penalized by low utilization, with per-mile costs often exceeding $4.00. However, by 2035, the projected drop in vehicle prices and maintenance savings allow BEVs to finally undercut diesel organically, reaching $1.92/mile vs. $2.43/mile. This duty cycle highlights the maintenance divergence between technologies: while stop-and-go driving is punishing for diesel aftertreatment systems, BEVs thrive by using regenerative braking to capture energy and reduce mechanical wear.
* **Drayage/Port Shuttles:** This sector follows a trajectory similar to RTB, showing very high potential for BEV adoption. The BEV TCO is projected to drop by nearly 50% over the next decade, ultimately becoming $0.19 cheaper per mile than diesel by 2035. Drayage is uniquely suited for multi-shift top-up charging during terminal dwell times, and also serves as a critical hedge as ports seek to regulate new diesel vehicles.

The data collection phase of the Run on Less – Messy Middle was conducted over 18 days in September 2025. Thirteen fleets operating 14 Class 8 tractors representing four powertrain technologies — diesel, CNG/RNG, BEV, and FCEV — participated in the demonstration. A detailed summary of the planning and execution of the Run is documented in the report *How NACFE Helped Bring Clarity to the Messy Middle* [2].

The operational details of the Run are documented in the *Terrain, Technology, and Telematics: The Messy Middle Operations Report* [3]. This section draws on that document to incorporate key information into this report.

While this TCO report uses information beyond that collected in the Run itself, it is limited in scope to Class 8 vehicles, powertrains, and duty cycles operational in the Run, and examines TCO for three different timeframes — 2025, 2028 and 2035. Note that this report is designed to model Class 8 tractors used for transporting goods; it does not include calculations for vocational or specialized Class 8 applications such as dump trucks, concrete mixers, or refuse vehicles. This report covers only Class 8 tractors, including both day cabs and sleepers. Vehicles in the Run included both conventional and cabover designs.

The 14 Class 8 trucks in the Run used the following powertrains:
* Four used fossil diesel, renewable diesel or blends of biodiesel,

For this analysis, NACFE used the following definitions of duty cycle. Of course, any one fleet or truck may experience duty cycles that do not exactly match those specified and a mix of duty cycles.

### 2.3.1 Long-Haul OTR
The long-haul OTR duty cycle has the following characteristics:
* Multi-day or continuous operations spanning multiple regions
* 500 to 800+ miles per typical day; Run validated maximum exceeded 1,100 miles
* Possible team driving operation
* Variable terrain and climate across operating territory
* Fueled at public corridors, infrastructure independence essential
* High sustained highway speeds (60+ mph dominant)

### 2.3.2 Regional Linehaul
The regional linehaul duty cycle represents a form of long RTB operation as defined in NACFE's 2025 white paper, *A Need to Redefine Class 8 Long-Haul Trucking* [4]. While daily mileage may approach traditional long-haul levels (250 to 500 miles), the structured terminal-to-terminal nature and relay driver models enable depot-based infrastructure strategies more typical of RTB operations characterized below:
* 250 to 500 miles per day in structured terminal-to-terminal operations
* Relay networks with predictable route structures
* Possible slip seat operation
* High sustained speeds on Interstate corridors
* Compatible with depot-based fueling or charging strategies

### 2.3.3 Regional RTB
The regional RTB duty cycle is characterized by the following conditions:
* Single-day cycles returning to a home terminal each shift
* 100 to 350 miles per day depending on route density
* Predictable schedules enabling overnight fueling/charging
* Strong alignment with depot-based infrastructure investment

### 2.3.4 Urban/Metro P&D
The urban/metro P&D duty cycle is characterized by the following conditions:
* Stop-dense operations with frequent loading/unloading
* Low-speed routing through urban environments
* High idle percentage affecting energy efficiency
* Daily mileage typically 50 to 150 miles

### 2.3.5 Drayage
The drayage duty cycle is characterized by the following conditions:
* High utilization with repeatable short-haul corridors
* Strong RTB patterns enabling depot and en-route fueling/charging
* 100 to 250 miles per day typically
* Proven BEV application due to predictable routes and manageable daily range

## 2.4 Timeframes
One key characteristic of the Messy Middle is the fast pace of change in the characteristics, costs, and availability of the various powertrains. Vehicle battery and hydrogen fuel cell technology is rapidly evolving, new natural gas engines are entering the market, and diesel technology is evolving with emissions requirements, to name a few trends. Production costs of emerging powertrains are expected to evolve as the number of vehicles produced increases, and supply chains mature. Maintenance costs are expected to change as experience with these new technologies grows and the number of trained technicians and service centers increases. Fueling costs are expected to change over time as well.

To account for these anticipated changes in the components of TCO over time, NACFE has carried out the TCO analysis for three different points in time: Now, Soon, and Later, as defined below.
* **Now:** To represent current baseline conditions, the analysis uses 2025 information.
* **Soon:** To represent anticipated near-term conditions that may affect current procurement decision making, the analysis uses 2028 as the year for projections.
* **Later:** To represent longer-term conditions that may affect longer-term fleet-planning decisions, the analysis projects conditions for 2035.

# 3 TCO FRAMEWORK
In the Messy Middle of decarbonization, successfully navigating the transition to zero-emission freight requires moving beyond simple upfront purchase prices toward a comprehensive TCO calculation of the vehicle. TCO is one of many metrics fleets may use in evaluating investment choices on new vehicles; however, NACFE believes TCO more accurately captures a comprehensive perspective of fleet cost modeling.

<!-- page: 28 -->
This section establishes the framework for that analysis, integrating NACFE’s modeling with a deep dive into the specific cost components that define a fleet’s bottom line. The study team answered the questions: How is TCO calculated? What are the primary cost buckets and how are they changing over time? What key assumptions did NACFE make to ensure these numbers reflect operational reality? And what can fleet managers do to drive down TCO in the Messy Middle?

To provide a consistent structure for this analysis, NACFE categorized TCO components into two primary buckets: Capital Costs (CapEx) and Operating Costs (OpEx). By understanding how each cost component impacts either the upfront sticker price or the daily running costs, a fleet can manage these variables to beat the national averages and achieve parity ahead of NACFE’s own estimates.

For each specific TCO component discussed in this chapter, NACFE applied the following framework:
* **Definition:** A brief explanation of the TCO component
* **Why it Matters:** The context of what fleets should know about this cost bucket and its significance to the bottom line.
* **NACFE's Methodology:** How we approached this component in our TCO model, including the specific assumptions made and the rationale behind them.
* **Trends & Forces:** How the TCO component is changing over time by powertrain type — e.g., rising, falling, staying flat — and the market drivers behind those trends.
* **Actions for Fleets:** How may fleet managers lower this cost component?

Furthermore, because a strict cost model only tells half the financial story, the study team concluded this section by exploring factors beyond the traditional spreadsheet. The study team examined soft costs and benefits — such as driver retention and noise reduction — as well as TOE, which factors in the revenue premiums that fleets might command by offering zero-emission freight services.

## 3.1 Report Methodology
As with all models, the inputs and the assumptions are critical. Many models exist with varying details, sources and biases. NACFE has made a good faith effort to provide unbiased realism, accuracy and transparency.

The process for this report consisted of synthesizing fuel economy data from Run on Less – Messy Middle with numerous public studies and deep-dive interviews with experts in the field. The study team calculated the TCO over a five-year ownership period, and to reflect the costs across three distinct timeframes: 2025 (Now), 2028 (Soon), and 2035 (Later).

As the study team approached the later years of the study, the model naturally carries more uncertainty. To mitigate this, the study team relied on established technical studies that assume S-curve technology adoption trends. See Figure 5. These trends account for the volume leverage, manufacturing cost reductions, and efficiency improvements that historically occur as new technologies scale from early adoption to mass-market maturity.

In its analysis, NACFE took a very conservative approach by assuming zero incentives for its TCO calculations. This provides a worst-case scenario that reveals when parity occurs organically through technology improvements and operational savings.

Importantly, while the Run provided critical real-world operational insights, the sample size of 14 vehicles over 18 days was not large enough to be statistically representative for a universal financial model. In addition, some of the vehicles in the Run achieved exceptional fuel efficiency — such as diesel units exceeding 11 MPG — that would have disproportionately skewed the TCO results if applied as a universal benchmark; more is discussed on these performance variations in the Fuel Efficiency Methodology section. As a result, NACFE used US national averages for its baseline TCO calculations instead of purely data from the Run, to ensure the results are broadly relevant to the wider industry.

### 3.1.2 State-by-State Variations
While this report uses national averages to provide a consistent benchmark, fleet managers must account for significant regional variability that can shift a TCO model from unattractive to highly profitable, and vice versa. A fleet’s specific geography introduces several local multipliers that include but are not limited to the following.
* **Maximum Vehicle Speed Limits:** Aerodynamic drag is a squared function of speed; therefore, efficiency is highly sensitive to regional limits. A fleet operating in California, where heavy-duty vehicles are limited to 55 mph, will achieve significantly better energy/fuel efficiency than a fleet running in regions with 75 or 80 mph limits, but may conversely have lower daily mileage
* **Maximum Weight Allowances:** Weight limits dictate the revenue-generating potential of the asset. While federal limits generally sit at 80,000 lbs. (with an 82,000 lb. allowance for ZEVs), some states allow specialized CNG/RNG configurations to pull three trailers with gross vehicle weights reaching 120,000 lbs. to 140,000 lbs. Conversely, fleets must weigh the 2,000 lb. ZEV allowance against the actual weight of heavy battery packs to ensure they don't sacrifice payload.
* **Localized Incentives:** As established in the methodology (section 3.1), the study team did not include incentives at the vehicle level for the baseline study. However, many states offer substantial purchase vouchers and make-ready utility infrastructure credits that can slash upfront CapEx by 20% to 50% in the near term.
* **Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) Credits:** States like California, Oregon, and Washington offer recurring credits for the use of renewable fuels and zero-emission electricity. These credits act as a fuel subsidy, which helps to lower operating costs for ZEV fleets.

### 3.1.3 Study Limitations
Modeling the financial future of the commercial freight industry involves inherent challenges. With that in mind, NACFE acknowledges the following limitations in the analysis.
* **The 2025 vs. 2035 Confidence Gap:** There is a significant difference in data confidence between the timeframes. While 2025 conditions are based on current market invoices and observed performance, the 2035 outlook relies on projections of technology maturity.
* **Uncertainty vs. Variability:** This model deals with both uncertainty (data that is not yet widely available or measured, such as long-term battery second-life value) and variability (data that has inherent, unpredictable swings, such as diesel fuel prices or electricity demand charges). Figure 3 categorizes the data by confidence level to provide a sense of the reliability and accuracy behind NACFE’s assumptions.
* **Asymmetric Technological Maturity:** There is a disparity in how well the industry understands the inputs for each powertrain. Legacy diesel has decades of actuarial and maintenance data, making its inputs highly certain. Conversely, inputs for BEVs and FCEVs — particularly residual values, long-term maintenance, and insurance premiums — are less certain because the technologies have not yet completed multiple full lifecycles at scale.
* **The Wild Card Factor:** This analysis assumes a relatively linear progression of technology. However, major market disruptions — such as a step function breakthrough in battery density or a massive manufacturing pivot by a high-volume entrant — could rapidly accelerate the timeline to parity beyond what a linear model can predict.
* **Operational and Environmental Variability:** While the study team categorized fleets into standard duty cycles, real-world operations contain massive variability in payload weight, specific terrain, and driver behavior. Furthermore, a static TCO model cannot fully capture edge cases — such as extreme weather conditions or mountain-grade hauls — that might make a specific powertrain viable for one fleet but unviable for another.

## 3.2 Why TCO?
TCO is a common and powerful way to compare different powertrain technologies on a level playing field. TCO aggregates every expense incurred over a defined period of vehicle operation. This includes the initial purchase and infrastructure build-out, as well as the ongoing daily costs of energy, maintenance, and insurance over that period. At the end of the period, the model factors in the projected resale value to offset a portion of those expenses, revealing the true all-in cost of the asset. Lastly, the resulting net cost is divided over the total miles driven during the period to arrive at a final TCO-per-mile. Ultimately, TCO is helpful because it allows a fleet to see past the initial purchase price and identify which investment will result in the lowest total cash outflow per mile over the vehicle's useful life.

However, a standard TCO calculation has one significant deficiency — it often ignores the timing of the financial recovery and differences in ownership periods. For example, while one powertrain may offer the most attractive TCO over a 10-year horizon, the high upfront costs might mean the break-even point occurs when the fleet plans to trade the vehicle after five years. This is why Return on Investment (ROI) and payback period are important complementary parameters. In an industry where many major fleets traditionally trade their vehicles every four to five years (and some as frequently as every two), the speed of the return is just as vital as the total amount.

<!-- page: 33 -->
Every fleet operates under its own capital constraints and trade-cycle criteria; therefore, for fleets with shorter trade cycles, the time to parity often matters more than the eventual TCO.

## 3.3 The TCO Equation
Fleets must start by estimating all the component costs of the vehicle over its lifetime. Fleets must then combine and boil these costs down into the one metric that matters most to the bottom line: Total Cost of Ownership Per Mile ($/mi).

The basic math looks like this:
TCO Per Mile (/mi) = [Total Capital Costs + Total Operating Costs] div Total Lifetime MilesTo further break this down into the specific components discussed in this report, the equation looks like this:textTCO Per Mile (/mi) = ([Total CapEx (Vehicle Cost - Residual Value + Charging & Fueling Infrastructure - Incentives & Grants + Financing Fees)] + [Total OpEx (Fuel & Electricity + Maintenance & Repair + Insurance + Regulatory Penalties)]/Total Lifetime Miles)

To get an accurate financial picture, fleets also should consider the "time value of money" through a Net Present Value (NPV) analysis. Before dividing by lifetime miles, future costs and savings should be discounted to their present value to account for the time value of money. This is a critical step for ZEVs, where fleets pay a higher price up front today in exchange for operational savings that accrue over subsequent years.

In practical terms, once a fleet has this single discounted total cost (the numerator), it divides it by the total lifetime miles (the denominator) to find the true TCO per mile. This step matters because ZEVs carry heavy upfront CapEx and require a high volume of miles to spread out the initial investment and allow the lower OpEx to generate a return. If the final discounted TCO per mile is lower than the fleet’s diesel baseline, the investment is technically paying for itself, even after accounting for the cost of the money spent upfront.

Note: Integrating charging and fueling infrastructure costs into this calculation is necessary to address the significant maturity gap between established diesel networks and nascent alternatives. NACFE recognizes that fleets may fuel at private depots, third-party stations, or a combination of both; in every scenario, the CapEx and OpEx of infrastructure are fundamental components of the TCO equation. Diesel’s incumbent advantage is that the true costs of its extensive global supply chain are often baked into the fuel price and obscured. Conversely, for emerging technologies, these infrastructure costs are explicit and identifiable, making a direct accounting essential for an accurate and transparent comparison.

The rest of this section describes the individual TCO variables that make up the calculation in both the CapEx and OpEx buckets.

### 3.4.2 Residual Value
Residual value is defined as the estimated resale value of the vehicle at the end of its first life or trade cycle. It represents the true depreciation cost of the asset — the difference between what a fleet paid for the vehicle and what it sold it for.

This matters because the resale value represents a key part of the large-fleet business model. Because major fleets often purchase new equipment every four to five years, they rely on selling used trucks at a high price to reduce the net cash out-of-pocket for their next replacement cycle. An inaccurate assumption can destroy a TCO model, making it a high-risk variable for early adopters of emerging technology.

## 3.4.2.4 Residual Value Trends & Forces
This section looks at the residual values trends and forces acting on residual value for both mature markets and

## 3.4.3 Charging & Fueling Infrastructure
Charging and fueling infrastructure relies on upfront CapEx to install depot charging stations, hydrogen fueling 
equipment, biodiesel or renewable fuel tanks, or natural gas compressors, including depot infrastructure, 
hardware, software, and necessary utility grid upgrades. This includes two types of infrastructure.

## 3.4.3.1 Charging/Fueling Infrastructure Trends & Forces
This section looks at the trends and forces acting on charging and fueling infrastructure for both mature markets 
and emerging markets for diesel, CNG/RNG, BEV and FCEV trucks.

* **The Mature Market (Diesel & CNG/RNG):** Diesel infrastructure is ubiquitous and highly commoditized, 
requiring virtually no infrastructure CapEx for fleets using public fueling. CNG/RNG infrastructure is 
mature but requires significant upfront investment if a fleet chooses to build a private depot compressor 
and tank system. Much like ZEV infrastructure, these assets often have technical lifespans of 20 years or 
more.
* **The Emerging Market (BEV & FCEV):** Infrastructure costs represent a massive, highly variable CapEx 
burden. For BEVs, while charging hardware costs are stabilizing, make-ready utility upgrades, trenching, 
and installation can add unpredictability and delays to a project. For FCEVs, fueling infrastructure costs 
remain exceptionally high and represent a major bottleneck to widespread adoption. A major risk for 
early adoption is the first-mover penalty. If a fleet owner loads the total cost of site preparation and 
electrical/hydrogen upgrades onto the first few ZEVs purchased, those vehicles will be 
disproportionately burdened compared to subsequent ones. Success requires recognizing that 
infrastructure is a scale play; once the initial switchgear or compression capacity is installed, every 
subsequent vehicle uses that capacity at a much lower incremental cost.

## 3.6.7.4 Maintenance & Repair Trends & Forces
This section looks at the trends and forces acting on maintenance and repair of the various powertrain options.

* **The Mature Market (Diesel & CNG/RNG):** The diesel engine is a highly complex system of pistons, 
valves, and injectors that demands constant attention, from frequent oil changes (every 10,000 to 
15,000 miles) to fuel system filters and pumps. Furthermore, maintenance costs are spiking as a result of
the heavy burden of exhaust aftertreatment systems (DPF, SCR, and DEF). These systems require 
periodic regeneration, and failures here can trigger limp mode, stranding rigs and causing expensive 
downtime. Brakes, suspensions, and transmissions further add to the routine labor and parts costs. In a 
recent survey conducted by Northwestern University, fleet managers and owner-operators alike cited 
maintenance costs as the primary consideration when determining when to turn over their vehicle [33]. 
While a longer lifespan does help reduce TCO by increasing miles traveled, it can put the vehicle out of 
warranty and into a higher maintenance cost-per-mile bracket, paid out of pocket by the fleet manager. 
A recent Fleetio benchmark study estimated that service costs average about $0.20 per mile for vehicles 
between zero to five years old, compared to $1.10 per mile for vehicles more than 10 years old [34]. 
* **BEVs:** The BEV maintenance landscape simplifies dramatically. No engine means no oil changes, spark 
plugs, or valve adjustments, allowing some BEVs to log up to 21 more days between service intervals 
than ICE equivalents [35]. Furthermore, regenerative braking recaptures energy while easing the load on 
traditional friction brakes, extending pad and rotor life by up to double that of ICE trucks. However, BEVs 
are not maintenance-free. They require specialized battery cooling systems, software updates, and high-voltage safety checks. While there are fewer overall shop visits, downtime per visit can occasionally be 
longer because of specialized repairs and a current lack of high-voltage-certified technicians in dealer 
networks, as well as additional wait times for spare parts that may not be readily available. Finally, while 
the lack of engine vibration reduces wear on some cabin systems, tires have emerged as one component 
requiring more frequent replacement on BEVs, driven by higher vehicle weight and instant torque.
* **FCEVs:** The maintenance profile of FCEVs sits between BEVs and diesels. While FCEVs share the low-wear electric drivetrain and regenerative braking benefits of BEVs, they introduce unique complexities.

## 3.6.8 Insurance
For purposes of this report, insurance is defined as the annual premium paid for primary liability, physical 
damage (collision and comprehensive), and cargo insurance.

This matters because insurance is often overlooked in TCO models, but it acts as a hidden premium for emerging 
technologies. Because insurance rates are closely tied to the replacement value of the asset, the high upfront 
CapEx of ZEVs leads to higher premiums in the near term. For a fleet manager, this means that even if an 
alternative fuel vehicle saves money on fuel, some of those savings are offset by higher monthly insurance 
premiums. 

NACFE used a hybrid modeling approach for determining insurance’s impact on TCO to avoid the national 
average trap and reflect the higher capital value of ZEVs.

* **Liability Baseline:** For the liability portion of the premium, which is driven more by safety records and 
mileage than vehicle type, the study team used the ATRI 2025 Operational Costs of Trucking benchmark 
of $0.102 per mile [32].
* **Value-Based Collision/Physical Damage:** To account for the higher replacement costs of ZEVs, the study 
team applied the Argonne National Laboratory methodology [11]. This logic estimates collision 
insurance at a rate of $2.00 to $3.00 per month per $1,000 of vehicle value. By using this value-based 
calculation instead of a flat per-mile rate, NACFE’s model accurately captures the insurance penalty 
associated with a $425,000 BEV compared to a $155,000 diesel tractor.
* **Self-Insurance Note:** The model assumes traditional third-party insurance. NACFE acknowledges that 
many mega-fleets are self-insured or use high-retention captive insurance companies, which can 
mitigate some of these upfront premium spikes.

## 3.8 TCO Components: Payback Window 
The definition of a payback window is the specific length of time over which a fleet owner calculates the total 
cost of the asset.

## 3.9 Beyond TCO: Revenue & Total Operating Economy 
Revenue is defined as the income generated per mile or per load. While TCO calculates the strict cost of running 
the truck, subtracting TCO from revenue determines the TOE — the actual profitability of the asset.

This matters because TCO only tells half the financial story. If an emerging technology costs slightly more to 
operate but unlocks exclusive, high-paying freight contracts, guaranteed volumes, or additional operational 
time, the overall profitability of that truck might exceed that of a cheaper diesel asset.

NACFE’s baseline modeling focuses strictly on the expense side of the ledger (TCO), and does not incorporate 
potential revenue premiums, green freight incentives from shippers, or volume guarantees associated with a 
TOE analysis.

## 3.10 Beyond the Spreadsheet: Soft Costs & Benefits 
In addition to the many cost and benefit components of various powertrains detailed above, some costs and 
benefits that may merit consideration are not easily quantified or are traditionally excluded in cost calculations. 
Some of these components have direct implications for fleet operations, while others provide benefits to or 
impose costs on society at large. 

This section identifies a range of such soft costs and benefits, organized by powertrain, that a fleet may wish to 
consider. Note that both criteria air pollutants and GHG emissions will be discussed in detail in NACFE’s Messy 
Middle Emissions Report, to be published later in 2026.

## 3.10.1 Diesel 
There are both soft benefits and soft costs associated with operating a diesel-powered vehicle. This section 
looks at both factors.

### 3.10.1.1 Diesel Soft Benefits
Soft benefits are non-monetary in nature but still add value and are a positive factor in determining whether a 
particular powertrain makes sense for a fleet. Below are some of the soft benefits of diesel.

* **Extensive Fueling Infrastructure:** As the long-term incumbent powertrain, diesel has the advantage of 
ubiquitous fueling infrastructure. In most operating environments, diesel fuel can readily be found on or 
near a route, or at a depot via on-site fueling, including wet fueling. Easy availability of diesel fuel allows 
operations to be optimized with minimal consideration of fueling operations. Furthermore, the time 
required for fueling with diesel is significantly shorter than for other Class 8 powertrains. This advantage 
can reduce one factor in the logistics decision-making process.
* **Energy Density:** In addition, as diesel is the most energy-dense powertrain fuel, it adds the least weight 
to the vehicle for the same amount of energy. Class 8 BEVs, even with shorter range batteries, are 
significantly heavier than a lighter weight, long-range Class 8 diesel tractor. This allows a diesel tractor-trailer configuration to carry a heavier payload than the other powertrains. In addition, it helps prevent 
situations where a particular load may not be able to be hauled by a heavier tractor due to the resulting 
total GVW.

### 3.10.1.2 Diesel Soft Costs
Soft costs are indirect or intangible expenses. Below are some of the soft costs associated with diesel 
powertrains.

* **Driver Fatigue:** Drivers of diesel trucks have long complained about the fatigue they experience from the 
combination of noise, fuel odors, and vibrations (including from transmissions). The high noise levels 
and vibrations relative to other powertrains can hinder a driver’s ability to perceive the environment in 
which the truck is operating, potentially requiring additional time to carry out maneuvers safely.
* **Downtime from Engine Faults:** While diesel engines remain significantly more complex than BEV 
powertrains, recent 2026 EPA guidance has mitigated the unpredictability of aftertreatment failures by 
introducing a 4,200-mile operational buffer [41]. This shift transforms formerly disruptive sensor faults 
into manageable, scheduled maintenance, though it does not eliminate the inherent reliability 
advantage of a simplified electric architecture.

## 3.10.2 CNG/RNG
There are both soft benefits and soft costs associated with operating a CNG or RNG vehicle. This section looks at 
both of those factors.

### 3.10.2.1 CNG/RNG Soft Benefits
Soft benefits are non-monetary in nature but still add value and are a positive factor in determining whether a 
particular powertrain makes sense for a fleet. Below are some of the soft benefits of CNG and RNG.

* **Quieter Operations:** According to Freightliner, natural gas engines are up to 10 decibels quieter than 
comparable diesel engines [36]. This approximate 50% reduction in perceived noise levels reduces driver 
fatigue and benefits the surrounding neighborhood during urban P&D shifts.
* **Reduced Downtime:** Meeting emissions standards requires simpler technology for CNG/RNG engines 
compared to diesel. These spark-ignited systems lack the extreme complexity of DPF/SCR 
aftertreatment, making them less prone to the sensor-failure downtime that plagues modern diesel.

### 3.10.2.2 CNG/RNG Soft Costs
Soft costs are indirect or intangible expenses. Below are some of the soft costs associated with CNG and RNG
powertrains.

* **Loss of Maneuverability:** Because the volumetric density of CNG is significantly lower than diesel, these 
vehicles require much larger fuel tanks. To accommodate these bulky tanks, CNG tractors often require 
a longer wheelbase, which can adversely affect maneuverability in tight urban locations or older loading 
docks.

* **Weight Penalty:** High-pressure fuel systems (specifically Type IV storage cylinders) add significant

landfills and dairy farms that would otherwise go directly into the atmosphere. This methane capture 
credit can make RNG operations carbon-negative from a societal perspective, a benefit not currently 
captured in a fleet’s per-mile TCO.

# 4 SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS 
While the TCO framework establishes the baseline costs, it is important to recognize that not all components 
carry equal weight. In reality, certain variables exert a far greater influence on the total cost-per-mile than 
others. To understand which components move the needle most significantly, NACFE performed a sensitivity 
analysis — modeled as tornado plots — for each powertrain. This analysis answers a critical question for fleet 
managers: Which variables, if changed by 10% or 20%, will have the most disproportionate impact on my 
bottom line? By analyzing the difference between the high- and low-impact scenarios, NACFE can identify the 
most influential TCO components for each powertrain and where fleet managers should focus their attention to 
get the best financial results for their fleet. 

To provide a balanced and realistic baseline for these charts, NACFE averaged the cost data across four duty 
cycles: long-haul, linehaul, RTB, and drayage. (The study team deliberately excluded the urban P&D cycle from 
this calculation, as its uniquely low-utilization profile acts as a statistical outlier that would otherwise skew the 
average and mask the true influence of cost drivers for the majority of Class 8 operations.) 

In the real world, the relative influence of these variables can shift depending on the duty cycle. For example, 
fueling costs are more important to TCO in high-mileage long-haul OTR operations than in stop-dense urban 
P&D routes. Fleet managers should consider how their duty cycles may change the relative importance of TCO 
components for their fleet compared to NACFE’s sensitivity analysis.

## 4.1 How to Read These Charts 
The sensitivity analysis uses tornado charts to show which factors have the most power to change a fleet’s total 
cost-per-mile. 

* **The Baseline (Center Line):** The vertical line in the middle is the starting point. It represents the 
standard cost-per-mile NACFE calculated using national average data.
* **The Blue Bars (The Range of Possibility):** Each horizontal bar represents one specific cost factor (like 
fuel price or mileage). The wider the bar, the more that factor can move the needle on total costs.
* **Which Side Is the Bar On?**
  * **To the Left of Center:** This represents a Lower TCO and the Optimistic Scenario. It shows how 
much a fleet manager could save if that variable comes down in cost — such as a lower vehicle 
purchase price through incentives or driving more annual miles.
  * **To the Right of Center:** This represents a Higher TCO and the Pessimistic Scenario. It shows how 
much costs might spike if costs worsen — like fuel prices rising or the truck being used less than 
expected.
* **The Tornado Shape:** NACFE has ranked the factors that contribute to TCO from top to bottom. The most 
influential factors are at the top, creating the wide mouth of the tornado. The factors that do not affect 
the bottom line as much are at the bottom of the chart. As a fleet manager, the widest bars at the top of 
the chart are the priority levers. A small improvement in a top variable may do more to improve TCO 
than a huge improvement in a bottom variable.
* **Sensitivity Assumptions Tables:** Below each tornado chart is a Sensitivity Assumptions Table, which 
lays out the metrics NACFE assumed in the Baseline, Lower TCO, and Higher TCO scenarios. NACFE chose 
these metrics with care to ensure these ranges represent the realistic swings a fleet might encounter in 
the real world, from securing a large grant to facing an unexpected jump in electricity rates.

## 5.1 Overall Themes

This section looks at the themes that emerged from NACFE’s TCO analysis by powertrain, duty cycle, and year.

1. **The Diesel Escalator Is Real:** The most striking universal trend — across all five duty cycles — is that the TCO for diesel goes up over time. While legacy technologies typically see cost reductions, NACFE’s modeling shows the Net TCO for diesel rising steadily from 2025 to 2035. The reasons are growing vehicle costs from emissions regulations and rising operating costs from increased maintenance and insurance. Taken together, these rising costs gradually erode diesel’s incumbent advantage.
2. **CNG/RNG Offers a Bridge:** CNG/RNG consistently presents itself as a stable middle ground in the near term timeframes (2025 and 2028). Because CNG/RNG avoids the steep maintenance and insurance escalators projected for legacy diesel, it frequently undercuts diesel’s TCO in the immediate future. However, because its vehicle and operational costs remain relatively flat over the next decade, it is eventually overtaken by the aggressive cost-reduction curve of BEVs by 2035 in most duty cycles. In addition, while CNG/RNG offers a near-term financial bridge, it does not provide a regulatory one — it still produces tailpipe emissions, meaning it will not shield fleets from strict zero-emission mandates, and its fuel prices remain subject to historical natural gas market volatility.
3. **The BEV Crossover by 2035:** Across the board, BEVs show the steepest improvement trajectory. While BEVs are consistently the most expensive or second-most expensive option in 2025, BEVs achieve the lowest TCO of any powertrain in every single duty cycle by 2035. This drop is driven primarily by shrinking upfront vehicle costs as battery and production costs decline, as well as relatively stable, low electricity and maintenance costs. In some duty cycles, parity will be achieved well before 2035, as a result of the combination of higher daily miles and lower infrastructure costs, as is discussed further in section 5.2. Incentives and grants will also accelerate the parity timeframe for fleets who take advantage of them.
4. **Utilization Dictates the Economics (The Denominator Effect):** Across all charts, annual mileage acts as the ultimate denominator, drastically altering the TCO makeup. Because ZEVs currently carry a massive upfront purchase price, they require high daily mileage to spread out that initial CapEx and allow their cheaper fuel and maintenance costs to generate a return on investment. Conversely, in low-mileage applications, that high sticker price cannot be amortized as quickly, making it difficult to achieve TCO parity without aggressive incentives or finding creative ways to artificially increase utilization (e.g., operating dual-shifts) or extending the vehicle’s trade cycle.
5. **The Maturation of ZEV Residual Values:** A key driver of the 2035 results is the establishment of a secondary market for ZEVs. In the 2025 and 2028 charts, the residual value offsets (the light green bar dropping below zero) for both BEVs and FCEVs are negligible, meaning early adopters are absorbing the full depreciation risk. By 2035, the residual values for both powertrains expand significantly across all duty cycles as secondary buyers, parts markets, and recycling scales emerge, pulling the Net TCO down and making the financial case much stronger.
6. **Hydrogen Faces an Uphill Battle:** FCEVs are consistently the most expensive powertrain in 2025 and 2028 across all duty cycles, heavily burdened by massive vehicle costs and expensive fuel. While FCEV costs plummet by 2035 — narrowing the gap significantly in high-mileage scenarios like long-haul OTR — it still struggles to match the per-mile economics of BEVs or legacy diesel in almost every application.

## 5.2 Duty Cycle Themes

During its research, the study team found themes based on vehicle duty cycles, inducing long-haul OTR, regional linehaul, regional RTB, urban P&D, and Drayage.

### 5.2.1 Duty Cycle Themes for Long-Haul OTR

This cycle remains a tight race. Diesel maintains its crown in 2025 and 2028, though CNG/RNG offers a compelling near-term hedge for long-haul fleets looking to avoid diesel's rising costs while using established fueling networks. Looking toward 2035, the high fuel consumption of this cycle allows BEVs’ cheaper operational costs to finally overcome diesel's lower upfront CapEx. The emergence of 500+ mile range BEVs from manufacturers like Tesla and Windrose fundamentally changes the math for this cycle.

While true long-haul routes still require public fast-charging to complete a full day's work, these extended-range assets significantly reduce the amount of time and expense spent stopping to charge, allowing fleets to maximize the use of cheaper depot power and reach cost parity much faster. However, the major assumption underpinning the 2035 crossover is that public charging infrastructure and production-ready long-haul BEVs will become widely available over the next decade.

Meanwhile, this high-mileage cycle represents FCEV's best opportunity. While FCEVs do not quite reach parity by 2035, the massive drop in hydrogen fuel and vehicle costs significantly narrows the gap, offering a viable path for long-haul fleets that absolutely require fast fueling times and zero payload penalties.

### 5.2.2 Duty Cycle Themes for Regional Linehaul

This cycle sees the most dramatic shift. By 2035, BEVs go from being the most expensive option today to the cheapest overall. The strongest financial case for BEVs in this segment is the combination of high mileage and minimal public charging. As longer-range BEVs enter the market, more linehaul lanes can be completed using only depot-sourced energy, maximizing infrastructure savings while still amortizing the CapEx over high annual miles.

Meanwhile, the per-mile costs for diesel, CNG/RNG, and FCEV all end up virtually tied. Because their economics are so similar, a fleet's choice between these three won't be based on cost alone, but rather on local factors like grid capacity, fueling infrastructure availability, and state incentives.

### 5.2.3 Duty Cycle Themes for Regional RTB & Drayage

These two duty cycles offer Goldilocks scenarios for BEVs during the Messy Middle. They rack up enough daily miles to generate significant fuel and maintenance savings, but their predictable, return-to-base nature avoids the public charging infrastructure hurdles that keep diesel leading in long-haul OTR through 2028. By 2035, BEVs aren't just achieving parity; they are decisively beating diesel. Because the upfront purchase price of a BEV is projected to drop below diesel by this timeframe, these operational savings go directly to the bottom line.

According to the 2025 ATRI Operational Costs of Trucking report, regional hauls (100 to 500 miles) increased to 41% of all trips in 2024, while national long-haul trips (1,000+ miles) dropped to just 13% [32]. This shift toward regional hub-and-spoke models aligns well with the current range of BEVs, and is largely driven by fleets’ efforts to improve driver retention by allowing drivers to be home daily.

* **The Metric ($/mile):** All variables are boiled down to a single, standardized metric: Net Total Cost of Ownership per mile. This levels the playing field, allowing fleets to accurately compare vehicles with drastically different sticker prices and daily operating costs.

The study team grouped the TCO components into six cost categories.

* **Powertrain Costs:** The study team split the vehicle purchase price into two parts to highlight how much of a fleet’s investment is going toward new technology. The Powertrain Costs include the cost of the specific propulsion system (e.g., the engine, batteries, or fuel cells).
* **Chassis Costs:** The Chassis Costs represent the rest of the vehicle, including the frame, cab, and tires. Combined, these first two categories represent the total vehicle purchase Price.
* **Fuel & Infrastructure:** This is a composite cost that combines the price of the energy (diesel, electricity, etc.) with the amortized cost of the infrastructure required to deliver it (such as charging stations).
* **Maintenance:** Includes all scheduled and unscheduled service, parts, and labor over the vehicle's life.
* **Insurance:** Represents the estimated annual premiums for the asset based on its replacement value and risk profile.
* **Residual Value:** The estimated market value of the vehicle at the end of its first life. Because this capital is recovered upon sale, it acts as an offset that lowers the overall TCO.

Here is a simple guide to understanding the visual elements on each chart.

* **The Stacked Bars Represent Costs:** Each column is made up of colored blocks stacked on top of each other. These blocks represent the most significant cost categories that influence TCO. The taller the entire stack of blocks, the more expensive the truck is to purchase and operate. (Note: total vehicle purchase price is comprised of two categories: powertrain costs, which cover the specific propulsion technology, and chassis costs, which represent all other vehicle components.)
* **The Bar Below the Line Represents the Residual Value:** The light green bar that drops below the zero line at the bottom of the chart represents the vehicle's Residual Value. Because this capital is recovered at the end of the asset's life, it offsets a fleet’s expenses and pulls the overall cost down.
* **The Dot Represents the Net TCO:** The dot hovering over each column is the most important number on the chart. This is the Net Total Cost of Ownership per mile. It is the final math after you take all the stacked costs and subtract the residual value. When comparing powertrains, the lowest dot wins.

### 5.5.4.1 What Drives the Economics of Urban P&D

There are a number of factors that play into the economics in the urban P&D sector of the trucking industry, including miles driven, maintenance, and regulations.

* **Low Mileage Effects:** Low average mileage (~80 miles per day) makes it difficult to recover high upfront sticker prices on a per-mile basis. In this environment, the denominator effect works against BEVs today. On the flip side, low utilization means higher residual values (due to lower wear and tear), which helps to pull down net TCO. BEVs only win once their purchase price falls below diesel in the mid-2030s.
* **Maintenance in Stop-and-Go Cycles:** Urban delivery is the most punishing duty cycle for diesel powertrains, leading to frequent aftertreatment failures and high brake wear. BEVs thrive in this environment; regenerative braking captures energy while drastically reducing mechanical brake maintenance, creating a wider OpEx gap than the mileage alone would suggest.

* **Regulations:** Urban centers are the most likely geographies to implement zero-emission zones or nighttime noise ordinances. For fleets in these areas, the TCO for diesel must include the cost of non-compliance, whereas BEVs offer 24/7 access to restricted residential or stop-dense corridors.
* **Multi-Shifting:** To achieve parity sooner, urban fleets could shift from treating BEVs as a single-shift asset to a multi-shift asset. By slip-seating the vehicle for both a daytime delivery shift and a nighttime quiet zone shift, fleets can increase the annual VMT and accelerate the ROI.

### 5.5.5.1 What Drives the Economics of Drayage

There are a number of factors that play into the economics in the drayage sector of the trucking industry, including route predictability, regulation, and resale value.

* **Route Predictability:** Drayage typically involves high-mileage days (~100 to 250 miles) within a very narrow geographic radius. This provides the best of both worlds for TCO: the high utilization needed to recover capital costs, combined with the security of always being within reach of a central depot or port-based charging hub. Taken together, predictable routes and depot charging allow BEVs to generate steep operational savings that offset the initial battery premium.
* **Diesel Regulations:** Drayage is the first sector where diesel is becoming a liability rather than an asset. With port registries increasingly closing to new internal combustion entries, the cost of diesel must factor in the potential for early ICE asset retirement or exclusion from high-volume lanes.

* **Resale Markets:** Drayage has historically relied on a secondary market of used diesel tractors. While the upfront price of a new BEV is a barrier, the concentration of state and regional grants (such as HVIP) is specifically designed to bridge this gap. By 2035, a robust used-BEV market will likely emerge, allowing smaller owner-operators to electrify with lower capital exposure.
* **Shared Infrastructure:** Because drayage routes are highly repetitive and corridor-based, infrastructure investment is more efficient. A single high-capacity hub at a terminal can serve dozens of fleets, reducing the first-mover penalty associated with private depot construction. As a result, fleets may find more public and shared charging options in port cities.
* **Terminal Dwell Time:** Long dwell times at port gates or rail heads, traditionally a productivity drain for diesel trucks, represent a strategic advantage for BEVs. Additionally, many ports now offer fast-lane or priority gate access specifically for ZEVs, allowing these trucks to bypass congestion and complete more revenue-generating turns per shift.
* **CNG/RNG as an Alternative:** For fleets operating in regions with established natural gas infrastructure, CNG/RNG offers a mature, immediate path away from diesel. It provides the high uptime and fueling speed required for intense drayage shifts while avoiding the power delivery delays and vehicle weight penalties associated with large-scale depot electrification.
* **FCEV for High-Uptime Drayage:** While currently the most expensive option, FCEVs provide a zero-emission solution for the most demanding drayage lanes — specifically those requiring 24/7 multi-shift operations or maximum payload capacity. As fuel costs decline by 2035, FCEVs offer a critical pathway for fleets that cannot accommodate the charging dwell times of BEVs.

## 6.1 Step-by-Step Analysis Exercise

What follows is a step-by-step process fleets can use to analyze which powertrains make sense in their various duty cycles.

### 6.1.1 Step 1: Establish a True Diesel Cost Baseline

* **The Goal:** Quantify exactly what it costs to move freight today to have a meaningful benchmark for comparison. This means calculating the true average cost per mile, including the total out-of-service dwell time for repairs. This step provides the control group for a TCO comparison.
* **How to source the data:** Extract 12 months of telematics data and cross-reference it with maintenance management software and fuel card records.

### 6.1.2 Step 2: Map High-Opportunity Geographies

* **The Goal:** Pinpoint regions where the price gap between diesel and electricity is widest and where local subsidies for alternative fuel vehicles are most aggressive. In the Messy Middle, geography is often the deciding factor; a vehicle that fails to reach parity in one state may be highly profitable in another because of stacked grants and lower fuel costs.

# APPENDIX A – DETAILED METHODOLOGY

NACFE’s TCO model was developed in Python to calculate the lifecycle costs of Class 8 trucks across five duty cycles and four primary powertrains (including fuel splits for biodiesel and renewable diesel) across three target years: 2025, 2028, and 2035.

**Calculation Framework:** The model determined the Net Present Value (NPV) of a vehicle's lifecycle by aggregating two one-off capital components — upfront purchase costs and end-of-life residual value — with three annually recurring operating costs: fuel, maintenance, and insurance. All future recurring costs and the residual value were discounted back to the base year using industry-standard rates. The resulting NPV was then divided by the total projected mileage over the ownership period to arrive at the final TCO per mile.

**Capital Costs:** Vehicle purchase prices are considered upfront expenses at the start of the ownership period; financing or loan repayments were excluded to maintain a direct technological comparison. Total vehicle purchase price consists of two categories: Powertrain costs, which cover the specific propulsion technology (engine, battery, or fuel cell), and chassis costs, which represent the remaining vehicle components. These costs were segmented by duty cycle, powertrain, and target year.

**Operating Expenses**
* **Fuel/Energy:** Annual fuel expenditure was derived by multiplying vehicle efficiency (based on duty cycle, powertrain, and year) by annual mileage. For electric vehicles, charging costs were calculated on a levelized basis, making them inclusive of infrastructure payback and maintenance.
* **Maintenance:** Costs per mile were segmented by powertrain and vehicle age. These per-mile rates were multiplied by annual mileage over the analysis period for each duty cycle.
* **Insurance:** NACFE used a consistent base rate for all scenarios, with total premiums varying based on vehicle purchase price and annual mileage. Insurance was split into liability (applied per mile) and comprehensive (calculated as a percentage of upfront purchase costs). The model includes a 3% annual escalator for insurance premiums.

**Residual Value and Depreciation:** Residual value was calculated using powertrain-specific depreciation methodologies that account for duty-cycle-specific mileage.
* **Diesel & CNG/RNG:** Depreciation was determined via an equation developed by Argonne National Laboratory considering vehicle age and mileage. These results were scaled using national average depreciation factors from JD Power to reflect recent market volatility.
* **FCEVs:** Due to the nascent state of the current and near-term market, NACFE modeled zero residual value for 2025 and 2028. For the 2035 analysis, a 30% annual depreciation factor was applied.
* **BEVs:** Depreciation was split between the chassis and the battery. The chassis follows the diesel depreciation curve. Battery value was determined through a two-step process: first, calculating the battery state of health (based on duty cycle size, age, and annual cycles), and second, applying the projected future replacement value of batteries in the vehicle’s retirement year. Finally, a 25% "second-hand market discount" was applied to reflect the risks inherent in the used ZEV market.

**Federal Level:** *Note: Federal support for zero-emission equipment has been effectively phased out.*
* **(Terminated) IRA Section 45W Commercial Clean Vehicle Credit:** This credit was terminated for all vehicles acquired after September 30, 2025. Fleets that finalized vehicle acquisition prior to this date qualified for a tax credit of up to $40,000 per Class 8 vehicle.
* **(Terminated) IRA Section 30C Alternative Fuel Infrastructure Tax Credit:** This was repealed for new equipment installations, effectively removing federal capital support for private depot construction.

**State Level:**
* **California (HVIP):** The Hybrid and Zero-Emission Truck and Bus Voucher Incentive Project. Base vouchers for Class 8 tractors can exceed $120,000 to $150,000, with significant multipliers for small fleets or those operating in disadvantaged communities.
* **Oregon (ZEV Rebate):** Provides rebates of up to $120,000 for the purchase or lease of Class 8 zero-emission trucks.
* **Washington (WAZIP):** The Washington Zero-Emission Vehicle Incentive Program provides point-of-sale vouchers for medium- and heavy-duty electric vehicles, including delivery vans and trucks, as well as a sales and use tax exemption that can cover up to 50% of the purchase price for qualifying vehicles.
* **New Jersey (NJ ZIP):** Offers vouchers for medium- and heavy-duty zero-emission vehicles, with Class 8 incentives often matching or exceeding $100,000.
* **New York (NYTVIP):** The Truck Voucher Incentive Program provides buy-down amounts for both BEV and FCEV Class 8 trucks.

**Ongoing Operational Revenue (Clean Fuel Credits):**
* **California (LCFS):** The Low Carbon Fuel Standard allows fleet owners to generate credits for every kilowatt-hour of electricity or kilogram of hydrogen dispensed. These credits can be sold on a liquid market, providing a recurring revenue stream that effectively lowers the net cost of fuel.
* **Oregon & Washington (Clean Fuels Programs):** Both states have implemented programs similar to California’s LCFS, allowing fleets to monetize the carbon-reduction value of their fueling operations.
