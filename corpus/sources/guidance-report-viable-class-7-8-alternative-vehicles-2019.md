<!-- page: 4 INTRODUCTION -->
NACFE's fourth Guidance Report on electric vehicles evaluates the potential for alternatives to the traditional diesel for Class 7/8 tractors in regional and long haul duty cycles. Innovation and significant investment have brought viable candidate technologies into the realm of production and near production reality. Fleets are rapidly approaching a point where they can choose from competing technologies based on their specific duty cycle and operating needs. Figure 1 illustrates a spectrum of choices, the more promising near-term ones being:

* Commercial battery electric (CBEV)
* Fuel cell hybrid electric (FCEV)
* Compressed natural gas (CNG)
* Renewable natural gas (RNG)
* Liquefied Natural Gas (LNG)
* Propane
* Hybrid diesel electric
* Renewable diesel

The optimum duty cycles for each of these technologies likely differ, trending towards specializing vehicle purchases for specific uses with ramifications on resale into second, third and fourth markets.

The rapid pace of technology development creates a level of insecurity for fleet investors wondering how fast their vehicles may become obsolete or unserviceable.

Complicating fleet choices seems to be an underlying decision bias that one technology should win out over all the competitors. This bias influences traditionally risk averse fleets into delaying investment or gaining experience in any of the new technologies, waiting on the victor to be eventually crowned based on market share. NACFE believes that vehicle segment specialization and regional factors are pushing the market to what former senior director of supply chain sustainability for Walmart and now Nikola’s Head of Business Development, Elizabeth Fretheim, aptly labelled a “poly-fuel future” where many of these technologies will coexist with no single national domination [183]. Scott Phillipi, Senior Director Automotive Maintenance & Engineering at UPS, agrees that “our alternative fuel fleet globally is really diverse, we don’t have a silver bullet per se because there (are) different applications for different types of fuels [182].”

The delays in investment by the more timid fleets rob them of the opportunity to be significant influencers in these fledgling technologies and to optimize technologies, operating models and policies for their own future use.

The purpose of NACFE’s report is to provide an unbiased look at the current progress, trade-offs and key considerations for fleets in these alternatives to traditional diesel powertrains. This report summarizes available research, interviews and first-hand experiences to assist fleets in taking steps to be participants in the on-going development of these technologies rather than waiting to see how the market sorts them out.

## 5 SCOPE

This report concentrates on the primary near-term drivetrain choices for the Class 7/8 regional tractor market. NACFE defined factors impacting this freight segment in its April 2019 report, *More Regional Haul: An Opportunity for Trucking?* [4]. NACFE’s focus is on dry goods and refrigerated van trailers which are the majority of trailer types with the greatest number of miles in North America. The information presented in this report is likely applicable to other trailer types and to other world regions. The report highlights, where relevant, data from other world regions. While references to specific manufacturers and products are included, NACFE’s purpose is to focus on the technologies, not the companies, and NACFE provides no endorsements of them.

Alternative fuels are options for economic reasons and because of differences in emissions levels, both critical factors for today’s fleets in the ever present face of competitive elements and the growing regulatory forces heading towards zero emission requirements. ## 8 WHAT IS THE CLASS 7/8 REGIONAL AND LONG HAUL MARKET?

NACFE defined “regional haul” in the report *More Regional Haul: An Opportunity for Trucking?*, published in April 2019 [4], as an operation where the truck stays within a 300-mile radius of a home base. This may include trucks that return to a home base every day or ones on a route for multiple days but that stay within that 300-mile radius. Long haul are routes are beyond regional ones, where drivers return infrequently to their home base, and distances exceed 300 miles per day.

<!-- page: 21 -->
These vehicles are Class 7 and Class 8 day cabs and sleeper tractors pulling dry van or refrigerated van trailers, or containers on chassis. While straight trucks, tankers, flatbeds, etc. may also be used in regional haul, the focus of this report as shown in Figure 3, is on tractors pulling van or container trailers. The tractors may be new models or older ones. Trailers can vary in age considerably. The majority of the operations use drop-and-hook [4]. Tractors can be 4x2 or 6x4 with or without lift axles. The tractors may be aerodynamic or traditional in styling. Trailers may or may not be equipped with aerodynamic devices. Tires may be wide base or duals. Payloads tend to cube out rather than gross out (at max allowable weight), so the majority of these are below 80,000 lbs. gross vehicle weight. A fundamental aspect of regional haul is that both the tractor and the driver return to base on a somewhat predictable schedule.

## 9.1 FREIGHT

The freight, or cargo, generally doesn’t care what tractor is pulling it. Freight fits inside the trailer as shown in Figure 4. It gets loaded and unloaded usually at a dock. The amount of freight that can be carried may at times be limited by weight of the tractor or by the tractor’s axle ratings and configuration, but as NACFE has reported previously, some 70% of freight loads cube out before ever reaching weight limits. Beverages and bulk products like paper are exceptions and likely care about tractor weight and configuration because these factors directly impact the amount of paying freight that can be carried per trip.

## 9.2 TRAILER

The trailer generally doesn’t care what tractor pulls it. The ubiquitous 53’ dry van or refrigerated trailer is generally tractor agnostic because of standardization. Alternatives such as 28’, 38’, 48’, 57’, double 28’s, double 48’s, and double 53’s, for example, may care about tractor configuration due to axle loading, gross vehicle weight or maneuverability requirements. However, these represent smallercentages of the over 6 million dry van and refrigerated trailers in use in North America [222]. The trailer is designed to be coupled through a standard king pin to any tractor equipped with a fifth wheel as shown in Figure 5. Even in the case of doubles, the dollies are generally equipped with fifth wheels. Better performing trailers do matter to the tractor’s performance, and lighter weight trailers can potentially carry more freight. Trailer loading may impact tractor fifth wheel settings and axle loadings which are limited by bridge law. However, the majority (70%) of freight cubes out before reaching weight limits [222]. The trailer also needs an electrical connection, which is similarly standardized with some exceptions.

## 9.3 SHIPPER

Shipping customers, termed shippers, if paying market rates with fuel surcharges, in a high demand shipping market as shown in Figure 6, generally have no choice about picking the tractor powertrain. These customers just need to move product and, from their perspective, all trucks may cost the same to them in the spot market. Where long-term shipping contracts exist, they similarly are primarily concerned with moving the freight. Unless the contracts specifically state powertrain types, they probably live with whatever tractor shows up to haul their freight. Those more selective customers risk not making deliveries while waiting for specific tractor types. Shippers have many business models - shippers that own no transport assets, shippers that own their own tractors and trailers, but still contract out for some amount of their business, shippers that just own tractors, shippers that just own trailers. Market conditions can cause combinations of models to be required to meet shipper demands.

When shipping demand is lower, what’s called a softening market, shipping capacity is more readily available, and customers can exercise some discretion in selecting tractors on either the spot market or through contracts. These companies may have corporate environmental sustainability goals that demand particular alternative fuel tractors be used. Fleets in these instances may be able to win bids over their competition and/or command a premium for greener tractors.

## 9.4 DRIVER

The tractor’s driver, if paid by the mile, doesn’t financially care which tractor powertrain is driven. Paid by the mile, all tractors likely look the same to the driver’s paycheck as long as uptime and operational hours are similar during their shift. If, however, the driver is paid with some version of performance incentivize tied to vehicle efficiency, the driver has financial reasons to want the best performing drivetrain. It’s dollars in pocket each trip. If the duty cycle requires fueling mid-shift, then differences in fill times may become important to daily paid miles driven under hours of service limits. The drivers also may care about quality of life factors and may prefer specific tractor drivetrains based on noise levels, or minimizing handling lubricants, fuels and DEF, or for performance reasons such as acceleration or regenerative braking. NACFE reported on a number of these tertiary driver factors in the April 2019 report *Regional Freight: An Opportunity for Trucking?* [4]. Drivers may also have pride in a fleet’s green positioning, or excitement in driving the latest technology. NACFE reported that fleets that have traditionally experienced high driver turnover and challenges in hiring are focusing now more on improving these tertiary factors to attract and keep drivers.

## 9.5 FLEET OWNER

Fleet owners and/or operators care which tractor drivetrains power their vehicles. It’s about profit margin. It’s about achieving corporate green goals. It’s about return on investment and total cost of ownership. It’s fleet brand image. It’s competitive positioning. It may be a regulatory requirement. Whether a large fleet with hundreds of tractors or an owner/operator with one, investment in the tractor’s drivetrain is a major decision with many ramifications.

## 9.6 CONSUMER

Consumers are growing more engaged seemingly by the day. They may want to know from where their goods came, how they were produced, and how they got to them. And more and more they may want to feel empowered to make eco-conscious decisions about which products to buy. Because of these trends, some companies are starting to offer consumers information that embodies emissions of products – data providing transparency about how much carbon dioxide was emitted to design, source, produce, and deliver that particular item. Technologies such as blockchain as an example are enabling this sort of credible data collection and sharing. As these trends continue, consumers may start to prefer goods that are shipped using low- or zero-emission vehicles. These goods may then, in turn, be able to out-compete competitors and/or demand a premium from consumers.

## 9.7 REGULATOR

Government regulators at the federal, state, regional and municipal levels enact rules for fleets. Fleet owners have a choice of complying with the regulations or not complying. Examples include regulations for zero emission vehicle use in specific cities and regions, or the increasing stringency in emissions

<!-- page: 26 -->
placed on ICE engine technologies. Those that choose not to comply face fines, probable legal costs, and perhaps eventual elimination as viable carriers in those regulated markets. Return on investment may not be the primary deciding factor where regulations force hard choices on whether a fleet will compete or exit a market. Regulators have goals and roadmaps for adopting technologies to meet environmental and public health objectives using a combination of stick and carrot methods for influencing tractor purchase decisions. Succinctly summarized by Mike Samulon, Sustainability Senior Policy Analyst, City of Los Angeles Office of Budget and Innovation, “The way to effect policy is through money [13].” Funding can incentivize the desired technologies and while legislation can make the competing non-desired technologies progressively more expensive to use, ultimately effectively banning their use.

## 9.8 PUBLIC

Regulators, especially elected ones, make policy in the perceived interest of the public. Politics and vested interests may factor into attempting to interpret and represent the public. In a perfect world, the public is well informed on issues, and the majority advocate for policy improvements. In the real world, establishing consensus on what is and is not desirable is challenging. Non-governmental organizations (NGOs) including non-profit and for-profit ones become advocates or opponents of various technology choices, claiming they represent the public and/or industry voices. Whether an advocate or opponent, the criticality of certain technologies to the public discourse is easily determined from the volume of media coverage, social media, conferences, webinars, and publications. The public, or their self-appointed surrogate agencies, clearly care about which technologies tractors use for power.

## 9.9 INVESTOR

Vested interests abound with technologies. The current push into electric vehicles, hybrid vehicles, autonomous vehicles, etc. is fueled by investors with billions of investments. Wall Street definitely cares which technologies win the competitive fight for market share. Investors also care hedging against climate risk, which may impact their investment’s profitability due to changing policies, liabilities, and technologies.

## 10 HOW ARE ALTERNATIVE FUELS ALIKE?

The alternatives to traditional diesel powertrains share common factors such as:

* Unproven Reliability
* Unproven Maintainability
* Unknown Obsolescence Rate
* Unproven Product Life
* Unknown All Season/All Region Capabilities
* Higher List Cost
* Unknown Lifecycle Costs
* Need for Infrastructure Investment
* Unknown Insurance Costs
* Unknown Residual Value
* Maturation and Adoption S-Curves

<!-- page: 27 -->
Alternative fuels are defined under the Energy Policy Act of 1992 (EPAct) as: “methanol, ethanol, and other alcohols; blends of 85% or more of alcohol with gasoline (E85); natural gas and liquid fuels domestically produced from natural gas; propane; hydrogen; electricity; biodiesel (B100); coal-derived liquid fuels; fuels, other than alcohol, derived from biological materials; and P-Series fuels, which were added to the definition in 1999 [6][7].”

Alternative fuel commercial vehicles have been in some use since before diesels were invented. In recent years, CNG and LNG are perhaps the most mature of the alternative technologies for Class 7/8 tractors, primarily day cabs, having approximately a 10 year head start with commercial vehicles in use by fleets with EPA 2010 compliant 15 liter GX heavy duty truck engines by Westport Innovations entering early use in calendar 2009 [8][9]. An 11-liter CNG engine meeting EPA 2010 and Euro5 standards went into production in 2011 by Doosan Infracore [8][10]. The technology has had on-going innovation and refinement based on actual fleet field history provided by companies like Canada’s Bison and Robert, who recounted their early experiences in a September 2014 summary in Today’s Trucking [11], and later summaries by AJR Trucking and UPS in a 2018 article by David Cullen in Heavy Duty Trucking [12].

Other alternative fuel technologies such as battery electric and fuel cell electric commercial vehicles have only recently entered the heavy-duty market in 2018-2019 as prototypes or pre-production units.

## 10.1 FLEET OPERATIONAL EXPERIENCE

Fundamentally, all these alternative fuel technologies suffer from a lack of fleet miles and the inevitable product improvement that comes from actual field experience. Diesel, in comparison, has been extensively field tested and steadily matured through high volumes of vehicles and miles in the hands of fleets. That inexperience also applies to the fueling infrastructure for the alternative fuels which have not yet seen significant use or benefitted from significant operational experience. Fleets will in time develop extensive field history on maintenance, reliability, repair, fueling, regional and seasonal performance, etc., but right now they are largely projections from limited testing in somewhat ideal conditions or extrapolations from automotive or bus product use. While experience with automotive use and bus use is relevant, requirements and duty cycles for heavy-duty trucking are significantly more demanding.

#### 10.1.1.1 Fuel Cell Vehicle Numbers

The Hydrogen and Fuel Cell Technical Advisory Committee (HTAC) to the U.S. Department of Energy estimated that, “through the end of 2017, nearly 6,500 FCEVs were on the road world-wide, half of those being in California. This is roughly double the number of FCEVs from 2016 [16].” However, NACFE estimates the quantity of Class 7/8 freight vehicles as of July 2019 at fewer than 50. These include a number of prototypes in use in South Coast Air Quality Management District port drayage testing at the Port of Los Angeles and Port of Long Beach [26][27][28].

Fuel cell vehicles were planned for the 2012 Zero Emission Cargo Transport Project (ZECT 1) but as of 2014, the builders had withdrawn [27]. A second drayage test program funded by DOE in 2014 (ZECT II) directed TransPower and Ballard to produce six fuel cell range-extending vehicles for drayage testing [26][28]. The Clean Air Action Plan 2016 annual report published in March 2017 highlights ZECT II direction to develop five fuel cell range-extended vehicles for port drayage testing through the Center for Transportation (CTE), BAE Systems, TransPower and US Hybrid. As of the February 2018 report on 2017 progress, TransPower was continuing to develop two vehicles and had fielded one into testing.

US Hybrid showed a prototype at ACT EXPO 2018 while continuing work on the two contracted trucks. CTE and BAE systems continued work on a prototype with Kenworth. Hydrogenics entered into work

<!-- page: 29 -->
with Daimler with a goal of availability in 2018 [29]. The March 2019 report on 2018 accomplishments indicates TransPower’s first prototype accumulated 3,700 kilometers and the second truck is expected in 2019. US Hybrid’s first truck entered testing but had significant challenges needing repairs. The CTE/BAE/Ballard Kenworth effort entered testing in Washington state accumulating some 12,000 miles primarily on a test track but also encountered challenges and was repaired. The Hydrogenics Daimler prototype continued work on two units [30]. The 2018 report estimates that “all project trucks are expected to achieve regular demonstration service in 2019.”

Ballard reports in its 2018 full year financials that “Within North America in 2019, Ballard expects increased FCEV market activity in California for FCEBs and fuel cell-powered trucks, which can be expected to result in additional module purchase orders.” However, they did not publish specific quantities of Class 7/8 freight units in production use. They estimate by 2030 “500,000 commercial trucks” to be in operation [31]. Future work includes two demonstration vehicles for Bison in Canada in 2019 pulling double 53’ trailers [20].

TransPower states on their website that “Since 2013, over a dozen trucks with the ElecTruck™ drive system have completed successful public demonstrations with over 100,000 miles of real-world experience including short haul drayage, school buses, yard tractors, and heavy port lifting equipment [32].” How many of these miles are directly attributed to fuel cell Class 7/8 freight trucks is not described.

Nikola revealed its second fuel cell vehicle in the spring of 2019 at an industry event. They have reported orders with companies such as Anheuser-Busch for “up to 800 trucks,” translating to an estimated 30% of their fleet [33]. They stated they have “nonbinding orders for more than 13,000 of (their) hydrogen-electric and battery-electric trucks [34].” Production is slated to begin in 2022.

At the October 2019 North American Commercial Vehicle show, Hyundai’s Commercial Truck division unveiled a hydrogen fuel cell Class 8 tractor it calls the HDC-6 Neptune concept vehicle. [220] The Korean car, truck, and bus builder hopes to use this truck, combined with its reputation as a global leader in fuel cell technology to enter the North American Class 8 market.

Brian Lindgren, head of Kenworth’s Research and Development (R&D) lab and its efforts on the Kenworth/Toyota fuel cell grant trucks, estimated at ACT EXPO 2018 that production launch for fuel cell vehicles at PACCAR would be no earlier than 2026 [35]. At ACT EXPO 2019 he estimated the market for fuel cell heavy trucks in 2022-2025 to be 50 to 200 per year [36].

Fuel cells are accumulating miles in cars such as the Toyota Mirai, Honda Clarity and others as documented in March 2019 by Jennifer Kurtz et al in on-going evaluations by the National Renewable energy Lab (NREL) dating back to 2005 [55]. Fuel cells have also seen mileage in buses going back to 2003, as documented on NREL’s on-going tracking on their website Fuel Cell Electric Bus Evaluations [54].

Fuel cell Class 7/8 freight trucks are clearly a major area of investment by manufacturers and investors and have significant interest from fleet operators. However, NACFE concludes actual production level vehicles in real-world operational use hauling freight is extremely limited to date, and likely will not begin ramping up in volume of trucks and miles of operation for some years.

#### 10.1.1.2 Commercial Battery Electric Vehicle Numbers

Commercial Class 7/8 battery electric trucks were also part of the 2012 Zero Emission Cargo Transport Project (ZECT 1) with a 2012 goal of building nine [26][27][28]. TransPower was to build four, US Hybrid two, and a third company was to build three but dropped out of the program by 2015 [27]. The South Coast Air Quality District June 2015 DOE Annual Merit Review presentation highlights that TransPower’s first unit accumulated 3,000 road test miles, and then 325 miles in actual hauling with SA Recycling, only 303 miles pulling a trailer [27]. TransPower’s second, third and fourth units deployed sequentially to TTSI for operational testing in early 2015. The 2016 DOE Merit Review indicates units 2, 3 and 4 accumulated in total 209 days of operations and 9,440 miles travelled. In contrast, the two baseline diesel trucks used in the testing accumulated in total 166 days of operation and 23,590 miles [37]. The 2017 DOE Merit Review states the three TransPower units logged 22,700 miles in 2016 with 3Rivers, Cal Cartage and NRT fleets [38]. The 2018 DOE Merit Review indicates units 2, 3 and 4, since being deployed, have together accumulated 579 days of operation, a total of 25,786 miles averaging 44.6 miles per day [39]. The future plans presented were to rebuild three of the four TransPower battery electric trucks as fuel cell vehicles for further testing.

The first two US Hybrid units were deployed to TTSI in Q3 2016 [38]. Per the 2018 Merit Review report in June 2019, the two vehicles accumulated 48 days of operation and 1,479 miles of usage [39].

Tesla introduced two prototype battery electric trucks, a day cab and a sleeper cab, at a November 2017 industry event [40]. Since that time, the two prototypes have toured fleet locations and made some operational runs hauling Tesla EV batteries from Tesla’s Gigafactory in Nevada to its Fremont, California factory [41][42]. There may be a third prototype travelling the roads [43]. The total miles accumulated and how many actually are operationally representative has not been shown in public. In a 2019 investor meeting, Tesla announced the semi-truck production launch would slip to 2020 from 2019 [44]. At one point, Tesla estimated production would be targeted at 100,000 trucks per year [40]. An optimistic number considering the total production for Class 7/8 semi trucks peaked in 2018 at around 300,000 units per year [4]. NACFE works with several fleets that have Tesla semi trucks on order and is unaware of any that have used them in operations other than demonstrations, although they prepared to do so once they are available.

Nikola announced in 2019 that they would also produce and market a pure battery electric truck in parallel with their fuel cell hybrid ones [33][34]. The two models, the Nikola Two and the Nikola Tre for Europe can be configured for either hydrogen fuel cell or pure battery electric. Neither prototype in either configuration of powertrain has been supplied to fleets for operational testing as far as NACFE has been able to discern. Production start for Nikola is estimated as 2022.

BYD offers a production T9 cab-over electric truck capable of up to 80,000 lbs. gross vehicle weight rating and configured for hauling container chassis, dry van and refrigerated trailers [45][46]. This is a vehicle listed as in production, unlike offerings from every other manufacturer, which are described essentially as prototypes. The SCAWMD 2018 annual report states BYD “will develop 25 T9 battery electric drayage trucks for SCAQMD’s GGRF project. The T9 truck is optimized to serve near-dock and short regional drayage routes within a range of 100 miles, supported by 300 kWh batteries on hand [26].”

Lion displayed the Lion8, a Class 7/8 prototype battery electric truck configured for refuse at a Montreal Impulsion conference in June of 2019 and is currently taking pre-orders [47][48][49]. The company has

<!-- page: 31 -->
discussed with NACFE that the Lion8 could be configured as a tractor equipped with fifth wheel for freight hauling. NACFE is not aware of any such configurations yet in use by fleets.

Daimler Trucks North America is in the process of deploying the eCascadia, a “Class 8 tractor designed for local and regional distribution and drayage [50].” The truck is scheduled to enter series production in 2021 at the Portland, Oregon factory, which is being reconfigured to focus on electric platforms [51]. Daimler’s CEO Roger Nielsen stated at ACT EXPO 2019 in April that “unfortunately, there is no business case today for a sane, sober customer to buy a battery electric truck [52].” Nielsen estimated that DTNA should have “nearly 50 battery electric trucks and buses running everyday across this country.” It’s estimated that less than half will be Class 8 tractors. Volvo Trucks, under their Volvo LIGHTS project, is similarly deploying “all-electric Volvo VNR regional haul demonstrators in California later this year, with vehicle sales planned to begin in 2020 [53].” “The Volvo VNR electric vehicle will be based on the proven technology currently being used in the North American model Volvo VNR, the Volvo FE Electric, and builds on the Volvo Group’s experience in electrified solutions. ## 10.2 ENERGY STORAGE AND CONVERSION SYSTEMS

The term “fuel” in context of commercial vehicles implies a physical substance typically altered in a process that converts the chemical energy stored in the fuel into mechanical energy, moving the vehicle down the road. The inclusion of the term “electricity” in the definition of alternative fuels is somewhat misleading labelling.

Electricity is technically its own form of energy rather than a substance or fuel. All the other alternative fuels can be used to create electricity which can then power an electric drivetrain as an electric hybrid. This means that diesel hybrids, CNG hybrids, hydrogen fuel cell hybrids, propane hybrids, etc. are all technically viable. They share the capability to translate chemical energy in the fuel into electricity and then have the electricity power electric motors for driving the wheels. Put in perspective, all alternative fuels can be viewed as energy storage systems combined with energy converters for powering motion through electric motors as illustrated in Figure 11.

## 10.4 COOLING SYSTEMS

Every alternative fuel powertrain system for heavy-duty trucks has cooling requirements, just as diesels require cooling modules. The conversion of chemical energy stored in the fuel or battery packs into useful motion of the vehicle creates heat that must be managed and dissipated. The requirements and thus the size and performance of the cooling modules vary depending on the alternative fuel and operational considerations, but they all have them, a trait shared with diesels.

<!-- page: 37 -->
## 10.5 HEATING SYSTEMS

Alternative fuel powertrains, just as with diesels, have to pay attention to cold weather conditions. Diesels have fuel heaters, block heaters, winter fronts, special winter fuel formulations, the need to idle, etc. to deal with severe cold weather conditions. Alternative fuel powertrains have varying low temperature challenges and ramifications. In some cases, as with diesel, the heat created during conversion of the chemical energy into vehicle motion can be used to help maintain temperature in other chassis systems. Thermal management of alternative fuel powertrains requires managing for both high and low temperature extremes.

## 10.6 CABIN AND ENVIRONMENTAL SYSTEMS

Concern for the driver’s health and well-being means that cabin temperatures need to remain between moderately low and moderately high. This requirement is based on the human body – the tractor must be designed to maintain the cabin interior in an acceptable range based on the seasonal and regional operational expectations. Air conditioning and heating, the HVAC systems, are needed where tractors are expected to see both severe cold and severe heat. In more moderate climates, cabin cooling systems may not be required, but heating systems tend to be universally needed. Alternative fuel drivetrains can create heat as a byproduct of the conversion of chemically stored energy into useful vehicle motion, similar to diesels. The “waste heat” can then be used to heat the cabin compartment. In others, the alternative fuel powertrain must create the heat to warm the cabin, taking additional energy. In general, cooling systems all place an additional load on the powertrain, requiring energy and reducing efficiency.

## 10.8 INFRASTRUCTURE

Alternative fuel powertrains are universally challenged by a lack of relevant transportation fueling infrastructure on the scale of diesel. While the production is mature for electricity, natural gas, propane, and renewable versions of fuels like natural gas and diesel, the distribution for the express purposes of use in trucks is often limited in comparison to the ubiquitous diesel truck stops and terminal filling stations. This limited deployment of truck energy replenishment (“filling” really is not descriptive enough) stations is often characterized simply as the “chicken and the egg” conundrum – how can you sell alternative fuel trucks without a national infrastructure first? How can you roll out a national infrastructure without alternative fuel trucks first? These two questions are over-simplifying and somewhat red herring issues. Capital investment in required infrastructure nearly always follows market demand for product support. Light bulbs and telephones rolled out before there were hydroelectric dams and telephone poles dotting the U.S. Automobiles were rolled out long before there were interstate highways. Airplanes were developed long before there were airports. Cell phone networks evolved after cell phone demand pushed for expansion of coverage.

# 11 HOW ARE ALTERNATIVE FUELS DIFFERENT?

Comparing alternative fuels on a true apples-to-apples basis means comparing identical vehicles. Since alternative fueled vehicles are inherently not identical, each factor that is not kept similar has ramifications that impact the validity of the comparison. Alternative fuel vehicle equivalent comparisons are difficult. Trying to oversimply comparisons for media, advertising, and public consumption can bias the information, making it misleading.

Apples-to-oranges comparisons abound in the discussion of alternative fuel powertrains. As an example, NACFE reviewed comparisons based on gallons of each fuel, disregarding that each fuel has a different energy content or that each fuel must be converted into useful motion using systems with differing efficiencies. The example in automobiles is the comparison of a car’s range using regular gasoline versus E85 or E10, blends of alcohol and gasoline. The government clarifies that “Due to ethanol's lower energy content, FFVs (flex fuel vehicles) operating on E85 get roughly 15% to 27% fewer miles per gallon than when operating on regular gasoline. Ethanol contains about one-third less energy than gasoline. So, vehicles will typically go 3% to 4% fewer miles per gallon on E10 and 4% to 5% fewer on E15 than on 100% gasoline [103].” So comparing solely on the price of a gallon of E85, E10 or 100% gasoline is misleading because the range of a gallon of each is different. Comparisons can be easily biased by trying to oversimplify the discussion.

## 11.1 ENERGY CONTENT OF FUELS

Each alternative fuel system invariably requires an energy conversion system, as outlined in Figure 11 and Figure 12. These vary significantly in efficiency. So comparisons based purely on energy content of each fuel are misleading because the ability to convert that stored energy into useful motion is different for each powertrain. The storage system for the different fuels also leads to confusion since energy density can vary considerably depending on fuel storage system pressures. Where diesel operates at standard atmospheric pressure, CNG may be stored between 2,400 and 3,600 psi, and hydrogen may be between 5,000 and 10,000 psi. It’s common for energy to be compared in one vehicle in terms of gallons of diesel fuel, another in kilograms of hydrogen, and yet another in kilowatt-hours of stored electricity. These apples-to-oranges-to-plums comparisons can be improved by using standard, comparable units and stating the conditions at which they apply.

## 11.2 VEHICLE TARE WEIGHT COMPARISON

Weight comparisons are even more complex than energy content, as storing alternative fuels at different pressures – ranging from unpressurized at 14.7 psi sea level atmospheric pressure, in the case of renewable diesel, to 10,153 psi (700 bar), in the case of one of the hydrogen options – has to be done safely, requiring well-engineered pressure vessels. Even for diesel fuel tanks, whether they are round cylinders or rectangular ones, whether aluminum, steel or other material, and how they are secured to the vehicle all have total vehicle weight ramifications. Fundamentally, like-for-like comparisons require actually weighing vehicles built for the same duty cycle and range, fueled, but without freight. These true comparison tare weights are critical to validate predictions and to establish the actual allowable freight capacity on tractors designed for comparable duty cycles.

NACFE has found that total vehicle weight comparison estimates that mix and match requirements and specifications, biasing analyses, are common. For example, one analysis compared the vehicle weights by just looking at the fuel weight and tanks, but disregarded the vehicle systems that are removed as not needed or that must be added with one alternative versus the other. A past diesel example was when DEF tanks were just being introduced and there were different sizing, urea dosing, and regen mileage choices made by OEMs. This made true like-for-like comparisons of fuel economy difficult for the same duty cycle mileage, leading to the creation of recommendations for measuring total vehicle

## 11.3 PAYLOAD CAPACITY

Payload capacity is critical to the economics of freight hauling. The net weight of a vehicle has to factor into performance. As the E85, E10, and 100% gasoline example shows, the same tank of fuel has different ranges depending on what fuel is used. If the comparison were reversed, looking at maintaining the same range, the E85 vehicle would need to weigh more than E10, and the E10 would need to weigh more than the 100% gasoline version because both would need more fuel to go the same distance. That added fuel and tank has weight, which means the vehicle has to also work harder to go that same range, needing even more additional fuel.

Maximum weights of heavy-duty commercial vehicles are regulated, which means that each vehicle technology has to be within a total gross vehicle weight limit. Subtracting the weight of the unloaded vehicle (with fuel load, driver and possessions) leaves the available freight weight that can be carried.

NACFE has identified that stating comparisons of alternative fueled vehicles at just the 80,000 lbs. GVWR vehicle weight is an over-simplification of real-world freight and biases the conclusions. Freight weights cross the spectrum from zero when deadheading to loads as high as 45,000 lbs. or more for beverage and bulk material haulers. NACFE estimates 70% of freight trucks operate below 80,000 lbs.

While vehicle weight is important in some duty cycles, it is not as critical in others. The analogy here is with being a passenger on a commercial aircraft. While physics requires the net aircraft weight to not exceed the maximum weight capability of the plane in order to fly safely, individual passengers can vary in weight, all paying similar fares to go the same distance, and resulting in the total plane weight varying from flight to flight. In essence, the plane cubes out on passengers long before it reaches its max weight limit. While air travelers are now more likely to encounter full airplanes, there are flights where a percentage of seats are empty. The majority of freight trailer loads are like that, cubing out before weighing out, and sometimes partially loaded.

### Propane Engine Energy Conversion
Propane for Class 7/8 freight vehicles is not yet demonstrated. There are a number of somewhat conflicting reports on the efficiency of propane versus diesel and gasoline. NACFE has concluded that for automobiles, light trucks and buses, that purpose-built propane powertrains could be equivalent to or more efficient than gasoline vehicles, but less efficient than current diesels. The energy content per gallon expressed in terms of Btu per the DOE Energy Information Administration (EIA) is 1 gallon of diesel fuel (with sulfur content less than 15 parts per million) = 137,381 Btu and 1 gallon of propane = 91,333 Btu [138]. Per the DOE AFDC, the DGE of propane is 0.656, which begs the question how the fuel efficiencies of propane fueled buses are nearly equivalent on a DGE basis.

The key is that LPG is a liquid, like gasoline and diesel, and used in the powertrain fuel lines as a liquid. It is maintained in a liquid state through moderately high pressure with the fuel system [140]. The Propane Education & Research Council released a guide in 2011 on converting vehicles to Autogas (LPG), stating “One gallon of propane will produce 91,502 Btu’s of heat energy, compared to 114,264 Btu’s for one gallon of gasoline. By weight, one pound of propane produces 21,548 Btu’s, which is almost the same as gasoline. Although propane produces almost as much heat energy as gasoline on a per-pound basis, propane weighs about two pounds less per gallon than gasoline. An engine’s horsepower output depends on the quantity (mass) of fuel burned, so even though propane requires a leaner air/fuel mixture than gasoline, the net result is that it takes more propane than gasoline by volume to achieve the same power output. Gasoline engines converted to propane will generally consume 15-25 percent more fuel, in terms of miles per gallon [139].” However, the guide also states, “Many engines perform better on propane than on gasoline.”

Two factors favoring propane’s combustion efficiency are that it can operate at high compression ratios because the fuel enters the intake manifold cold as a vapor, perhaps as cold as -40°F, not requiring heating. The air/fuel mixture is also denser which promotes higher power output [139]. However, the compression ratio, while possibly higher, is limited by knocking – pre-combustion before the piston is at optimum compression.

### Hybrid Vehicle Energy Conversion Efficiency
A hybrid combines two or more systems into one vehicle. Fundamentally, the choice to power a vehicle with two distinct systems instead of one, the core decision of hybridization, stems from an analysis that one technology is insufficient to address the range of duty cycle and operating conditions for that vehicle. Adding a second power system generally increases the vehicle complexity and multiplies the number of possible failure modes, which can have ramifications for maintenance, reliability, uptime and repair. However, NACFE concludes a well-engineered and manufactured hybrid vehicle can be equivalent to or better than an equivalent ICE vehicle in terms of uptime, maintenance, and repair, based on recent years of automotive experience with large volumes of mature production systems.

Hybrids are usually categorized as mild or strong. In reality, this is a spectrum rather than one or the other. The spectrum includes variations such as a). ICE driven powertrains with diesel, CNG, propane, or renewable forms of these combustion fuels, b). fuel cells with hydrogen or other fuels as the on-board stored energy, and c) plug-ins with electricity as the primary on-board stored energy. A variety of permutations are imaginable, beyond the handful that have made it into production, a spectrum as shown in Figure X.

#### 11.5.1.1 Hybrid Definitions
Mild Hybrid – Uses a starter/generator and a small battery pack to augment the power from the main engine under fairly limited circumstances. This kind of system can be used to absorb power during the braking cycle, but the amount is generally limited by the size of the electric machine. Power addition to the powertrain is a small percentage of the total needed, and the electric machine is never used

---

<!-- page: 63 -->

Viable Class 7/8 Electric, Hybrid & Alternative Fuel Tractors

63

exclusively for propulsion in lieu of the engine. Generally, operating voltage is 48V or less. Fuel economy benefits are generally modest – 10% improvement or less. Vehicle transmission and drive modifications are minimal.

Strong Hybrid – Also uses a starter/generator, but this is a more radical modification of the powertrain. A larger electric machine is paired with a smaller engine and a larger battery pack. The electric motor will have enough power to drive the vehicle down the road at modest speeds while the engine remains off. These vehicles may have the powertrain configured to work in series, parallel or both. There is a fairly large variation in the powertrain configurations. Some use a conventional transmission, some use some form of continuously variable transmission (CVT), and others use a two-motor arrangement with no transmission. In any case, the engine can not only drive the vehicle down the road at modest speeds, but it can charge the batteries at the same time. Therefore, these vehicles have unlimited range as long as they have fuel. Batteries are not charged by plugging in the vehicle. Generally, the engine is required to facilitate reasonable acceleration rates or to climb hills at highway speed. Operating voltages tend to be in the 300-500V range. Fuel economy benefits are much better, particularly in city driving and generally equal to highway numbers. Combined mileage is on the order of 30-50% better than similar conventional vehicles.

Two variations inside this spectrum include the plug-in hybrid and the fuel cell hybrid.

Plug In Hybrid – This configuration uses a much larger battery pack to drive the vehicle electrically so that it has significant range before the internal combustion engine kicks in. Driving range without using the ICE varies from perhaps 20-75 miles. After this, the ICE is required to drive a generator to power the electric machine. These configurations are mostly ‘series,’ meaning that the only way to propel the vehicle is electrically. Of course, this forces the electric machine to have enough output to power the vehicle under all circumstances. Plug in hybrids will generally have a much smaller engine than the ones above that run at more constant power levels and RPM to optimize fuel economy.

Fuel Cell – The fuel cell arrangement is technically a hybrid since the conversion of fuel to electrical power occurs on-board the vehicle. The fuel cell converts stored fuel into electricity. The electricity can be used to drive the vehicle wheels directly, or to charge the batteries, which then can drive the vehicle wheels, or can be used in combination. The division of labor between the fuel cell and the batteries can vary significantly depending on how the manufacturer tunes the design of the entire system for specific duty cycles and operations. For this report, NACFE has listed fuel cells as a distinct topic.

The term “duty cycle” is used by NACFE to describe how a vehicle is operated in the real world. NACFE recognizes that there are other interpretations and applications of the term. Duty cycle must be qualified by stating a period of time to which it applies — a single trip, a single work shift, a full 24-hour day, a five-day week, a seven-day week, etc. At each moment of that time period, the vehicle is either moving or not moving; engine off or idling; accelerating, decelerating, or travelling at a constant speed; in traffic or not; at highway speeds or urban street speeds; fully loaded, partially loaded, or empty; etc. All of that is the duty cycle and it can be captured with data loggers and sensors that constantly record parameters from the vehicle.

When comparing performance, the duty cycle needs to be comparable for the comparison to be valid. Focusing on too short a period might skew the true comparison. For example, on the SuperTruck program, the net energy use of new prototypes were evaluated against a current baseline vehicle, first over a single trip, then over a duty cycle of 24 hours and then finally over a duty cycle of one week. The single trip was not sufficient to capture comparing overnight idling to on-board idle free solutions. The 24-hour period, while capturing hotel loads and such, was insufficient to capture driver hours of service ramifications, with stops, required rests, refueling, etc. At one week, the comparisons were felt to be valid for a single driver. Seasonal effects require a year’s comparison. Seasons may be a factor for alternative fueled vehicles where performance may be differentially sensitive to environmental conditions versus diesel.

Comparing a plug-in battery electric to a diesel, for example, looking at only one shift of operation skews the comparison because the battery truck must be recharged while the diesel likely can immediately go into another trip without refueling. Comparing a fuel cell vehicle to a diesel on a 24-hour basis similarly probably does not capture comparable distances between refueling. Comparing CNG versus diesel over a week likely captures enough data to be a valid A-to-B comparison.

## 11.7 FREIGHT TON EFFICIENCY

Freight ton efficiency is a significant step towards comparable comparison of two alternative tractors. It’s the ton weight of freight carried in the vehicle multiplied by the average fuel economy in comparable units of diesel gallon equivalent DGE (or gasoline gallon equivalent GGE). For example, a diesel tractor may haul 15 tons of freight 500 miles requiring 62.5 gallons of diesel at 8 mi/gal fuel efficiency, or 8 mpgₔₑ. The electric truck hauling the same 15 tons 500 miles might use 1100 kWh of electricity. This uses a heavy truck representative consumption factor of 2.2 kWh/mi [56]. So 500 mi/1100 kWh = 0.45 mi per kWh. A common energy content conversion factor is 1 diesel gallon equivalent = 38.1 kWh of electricity [56]. Putting that electricity used into diesel gallons means 0.45 mi/kWh = 17.3 mi/diesel gallon.

Freight Efficiency Calculation:
* Diesel Tractor: 15 tons freight x 8 mpgₔₑ = 120 freight ton mpgₔₑ
* Electric Tractor: 15 tons freight x 19 mpgₔₑ = 285 freight ton mpgₔₑ

In 2010, the National Academy of Sciences recommended load-specific fuel consumption (LSFC) as a primary metric measured in gallons per ton-mile for comparing vehicle performance [152]. The mathematical inverse of this consumption is termed efficiency, measured in freight ton-miles per gallon [116].

## 11.8 FREIGHT CUBE

Freight ton efficiency as a single comparison metric overlooks that freight comes in different package sizes, called the cube. The amount of payload is limited by the physical volume of space in the trailer not by the max weight. UPS and others package delivery fleets have started to use form factors in addition to weight when pricing parcel shipments, recognizing the cube is important [4]. NACFE expects that evaluation metrics will eventually multiply freight ton efficiency by the volume of the actual payload carried. This will provide a single composite metric that compares the volume of the freight carried along with its weight and the efficiency of the tractor.

For tractors hauling the same trailers, the cube comparison is identical. But some alternative fuel system comparisons might create tractor/trailer combinations that allow use of different length trailers. The challenge for fleets is that while it is very easy to measure cost and volume of energy at the dispensing point, the pump, it is infinitely more difficult to figure one vehicle’s real total portion in the entire energy supply chain. Only aggregated estimates are currently possible.

## Well-to-Wheel Comparison

Well-to-wheel evaluations are complex and abound in literature. The sheer volume of complexity leads to differing conclusions by analysts who may simplify the analyses differently. Renewable alternative fuels in particular depend on well-to-wheel comparisons to diesel to evaluate the amount of displaced petroleum use, a major factor in establishing these fuels as better choices. For example, using straight petroleum based diesel versus renewable diesel (essentially a synthetic fuel made from renewables) still uses the same engine powertrain in the truck. The major benefit is that renewable diesel is displacing petroleum diesel with one that’s a byproduct of renewable stocks, earning Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) credits, for example, in California [165].

CNG is already a Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) fuel versus petroleum diesel. In the case of CNG versus RNG, additional credit factors can be earned. ## Pump-to-Wheel Comparison

Pump to wheel generically describes the final delivery of the energy to a vehicle. The word “pump” is unfortunately an overly simplistic way to describe the variety of alternative fuel powertrain charging systems or high pressure fueling systems conceived for transferring energy to a vehicle. It also wrongly implies a static point of energy transfer, but pump can also include mobile modes such as overhead or in-ground catenary power, wireless charging and solar on-board charging.

The delivery point generally records the physical amount of energy transferred for a price. Units can be volumetric or power or even chemical energy. NACFE’s position is that equivalent comparisons need to be put into DGEs as previously described, with similar duty cycles, weather conditions, traffic conditions,

## Tank-to-Wheel Comparisons

Tank-to-wheel comparisons focus on the efficiency of the vehicle to convert energy into useful motion. Again, NACFE’s position is that energy use should be described in comparable units, such as DGEs. Vehicles should have similar duty cycles with comparable freight load and operating ranges. Avoid rationalizing away differences. If a difference, like the range from a diesel fuel tank can go multiple trips before refueling while a battery electric has to recharge after each trip, then the comparison is flawed and needs to be done more comprehensively looking at similar multiple days of use or the diesel tank should be resized to only go one trip. The farther the two vehicles stray from identical use and requirements, the less valid the comparison.

What is the fundamental difference between a fuel cell hybrid electric vehicle and a pure battery electric vehicle in terms of energy efficiency? Both use electric drivetrains. All other factors aside, converting water or other hydrogen based sources into useable hydrogen uses electricity, then compressing it to extreme pressures to make it portable and useable in a vehicle uses more electricity, and then having the vehicle convert it back again into electricity with some imperfect conversion efficiency uses even more electricity. All those conversions waste energy, not using it to move a vehicle. In essence, those conversions are middlemen each taking cuts and reducing the net that can be applied to movement. In total, hydrogen as a fuel source for a vehicle is fundamentally much less efficient than just using the electricity directly from the power grid to power a battery electric vehicle. NACFE’s review and interviews with experts indicates that in some cases, an advanced diesel ICE truck may even be equivalent in net energy efficiency to a hydrogen fuel cell one, in the range of 40%-50% efficiency.

The fuel cell vehicle is just one example of a hybrid. There are many permutations possible combining alternative fuel powertrains that can be combined with battery electric systems. Diesel, propane, CNG/RNG, renewable diesel, and hydrogen fuel cells all can be combined with battery electric systems to become technically viable hybrid vehicles. The net energy use efficiency of any of these combinations will be worse than a pure battery electric vehicle because they each introduce inefficiencies in converting energy. Any combination that employs CNG, RNG, renewable diesel, diesel, or propane will also have GHG and particulate and other emissions like methane from the vehicle, however low, so none of those hybridizations are actually zero emissions, even before looking at the original production of the fuel.

NACFE concludes that from an energy use perspective, battery electric vehicles are the most fuel efficient. All other alternative energy fuels are less efficient in converting raw sources into vehicle motion. Other factors do matter to the comparison, such as weight, fill times, etc. But in terms of which technology will eventually win out, it will be battery electrics. The qualifier here is that catenary powered electric vehicles are likely even better than pure battery electric ones, as they remove some of the weight and some of the losses with charging batteries.

Additionally, many of the alternatives cannot be considered zero emissions, only pure battery electric and fuel cells are zero emissions capable WTW when combined with solar and wind power generation. These two are also the only true zero emissions powertrains possible in TTW. If the long-term future is a zero emissions world, then NACFE concludes that only pure battery electric, catenary electric and fuel cells are viable.

In the “messy middle” time between now and that zero emission world, the transition from current diesel trucks will likely see all of these alternative fuels in use as interim bridges, as all have potential to improve versus current diesel technology. That messy middle will likely be decades long.

Fill times as an issue are artifacts of specific technologies, essentially byproduct issues of technology choices. Fill times are inherently biasing a discussion by force fitting new technologies into the model for existing ones. For example, does fill time in any way factor into whether someone chooses Uber versus driving their own car? Both have equal fill times, but the fill time factor is only relevant to one.

Operations tend to optimize around a new technology rather than the technology optimizing for the operation. A classic example is the advancement in wine bottle cork screws, with ever-more sophisticated solutions, but becoming moot in the face of screw top bottles. Or advancements in typewriter technology completely obsoleted by computer word processing and printers. Or evolution of film cameras completely obsoleted by digital cameras – remember when you had to send film in for developing? Developing time became irrelevant to seeing your photos and one-hour photo centers disappeared. Fill times may or may not be relevant to the future of freight transportation because future operations may not value fill time as a key performance indicator.

A transport case in point is a freight operation today where trucks sit overnight at a warehouse where the overnight staff already fuel, service and load the vehicle. In the morning, the driver gets in and drives his route, returning at the end of shift to start the process all over again. NACFE showed in its Guidance Report, Battery Electric Trucks – Where They Make Sense, that the actual human labor time spent charging an electric truck was about the same as the labor time spent fueling a diesel vehicle [56]. The truck sits in the yard the same amount of time unattended.

There are duty cycles and operations where fill time is relevant and unavoidable as a differentiator. Multi-shift operations with slip seat or team drivers is definitely one. NACFE estimates that the majority of North American truck freight operations are single shift, but multi-shift team and slip seating are still significant segments of operations. OOIDA estimates that 95% of owner-operator drivers are solo [167].

Regarding CNG fill times, the National Academy of Sciences documented “there are two basic types of CNG fueling systems: fast-fill and time-fill. Both are capable of filling the on-vehicle tank to approximately 3,600 psi, but as the name implies, the fast-fill can refuel a passenger vehicle in a few minutes by supplying gas from high-pressure storage vessels that are maintained full by compressors. The time-fill system in essence refuels the vehicle over a long period (hours) by the CNG compressor’s relatively small direct output, with small storage tanks used only for buffering. The time-fill stations are generally less costly to set up and are likely to be most appropriate for fleets that can refill overnight. In some applications – most notably refuse – this isn’t a problem as the vehicles return to their yard every evening and can be fueled simultaneously overnight [170].”

NACFE has found in interviews with some fleets that diesel fuel tanks are topped off before trips to ensure there are no late deliveries due to fuel issues, as the cost of a delayed delivery is greater than the cost of the driver’s time to fill up each day.

NACFE asserts that fill time comparisons are relevant but the importance is very dependent on operations and duty cycles. Fill time is not a universally critical factor as some fleet operations are not impacted by long fill times or vehicles sitting idle on lots overnight. Comparisons should be based on actual duty cycles over comparable periods of operation.

## 11.12 SYSTEM COST

System cost comparisons are difficult when comparing diesels to newer technologies. Comparable cost estimates for emerging new technologies versus well established ones are inherently biased because the state of the maturity and field experience are significantly different. For example, the Kenworth/Toyota fuel cell grant trucks in use in California are built using two existing automotive fuel cells available from the Toyota Mirai production vehicle car program. Fuel cells optimized for these heavy trucks have not been purpose built because market volumes are not there yet for the investment to be made in purpose built systems.

Actual cost to the end customer versus the list price are also greatly impacted by factors such as grants, incentives, trade-ins, residual values, LCFS credits, etc. that may make actual purchase prices paid equivalent to diesels. NACFE discusses these factors in detail in Medium Duty Electric Trucks – Cost of Ownership [57]. Argonne National Laboratory’s Yan Zhou et al suggested that a new cost metric be used, “adjusted retention rate,” that reflects the post subsidy vehicle price paid by a consumer [172]. The reason for this is primarily that the incentives generally only apply to the new vehicle purchase, where the vehicle value in the second market is largely market based pricing.

## 11.13 MAINTENANCE

Auditable and relevant maintenance data on new alternative fuel powertrains is largely lacking. Alternative fuel powertrains differ in complexity and failure modes. Some operate very similarly to diesel engines while others are entirely different. While some have seen commercial use, many have only limited field history as prototypes or early production vehicles that generally are not representative of production vehicles in terms of maintenance. Projections of theoretical maintenance costs are frequently overly optimistic. Experience with significantly different duty cycles like buses or passenger cars are not all that relevant to heavy truck use. Fleets interviewed by NACFE consistently reinforce that new technology has a learning curve and stress not to make decisions based on early performance, which can be below expectations.

An example is the introduction of CNG and LNG heavy trucks after 2010 where fleet maintenance experience was consistently higher than expectations, sometimes double as reported by Bison and Robert in their lessons learned in early operations [11]. NACFE interviews with other fleets have confirmed similar early experience and also that the current generation of vehicles has improved significantly.

Battery electric vehicles have significantly fewer moving parts for friction and vibration to cause wear. There are fewer major components, fluids, filters, etc. Theoretically there should be less maintenance, but the complexity of the operating systems for battery electric vehicles may involve software coding issues that are time consuming to resolve. These vehicles have wiring, connectors and sensors, three high failure prone areas also typically found with diesel trucks in the field. They tend to have environmental performance differences versus diesels. Maintenance and repair may theoretically be better with some alternative fuel powertrains, but until a volume of production mature vehicles are in operational use with fleets, assumptions on improvement should be tempered.

As an example, NACFE reported maintenance and repair cost trends from automotive experience in Electric Trucks – Where They Make Sense that service costs were similar between comparable diesel, gasoline and battery electric automobile models [56]. Clearly the types of maintenance and repair activities differ, but net downtime, technician time and replacement part costs may not see a reduction, especially in the early years of vehicle models. Preliminary indicators from automotive experience suggest that these costs are average or slightly better than typical internal combustion alternatives. Feedback from medium-duty electric truck operators is that after separating out early failures, these vehicles over the long run do have lower maintenance costs versus diesel. Feedback from transit bus experience has shown reductions in maintenance from battery electric use after initial learning curves.

NACFE concludes that maintenance and service cost reduction is an open question at this time for most of the new alternative fuel powertrains. In theory, some vehicles should see significant improvement over diesel. The industry is still at the early stages of development where designs have not yet matured through significant field experience. CNG and LNG use has been sufficient in fleets to capture reliable costs, however they went through significant learning curves so only more recent data should be considered.

## 11.14 INCENTIVES

List price is typically not what fleets pay for vehicles. The out-the-door price actually paid by the buyer is impacted by a range of adjustments. Alternative fuel vehicles have access to grants, tax incentives, special loans or leases, rebates, exemptions and other types of price reductions. The goal of these adjustments is to spur purchase of desired low emissions technologies over less desired ones by helping bring the prices into parity.

Built into the use of incentives is a common assumption that new technologies do not yet have the economies of scale and design-to-cost maturity seen by established technologies, so they need financial incentives to offset that disadvantage. Later, when the market is established, and high volumes of vehicles are in production and use, the incentives are no longer needed and are phased out.

An automotive example of this is the case of electric car federal tax credits, regulations set a cap of 200,000 qualified vehicles for when the credits would cease to be available by manufacturer. Per Edmunds, “Tesla hit the milestone first in July 2018. As a result, Tesla vehicles delivered from January 1 to June 30, 2019, are only eligible for a federal credit of $3,750. Tesla vehicles delivered from July 1 to December 31, 2019, are eligible for a federal credit of $1,875. There are no federal tax credits for Tesla after that [180].” Edmunds also states, “You might be thinking: Don't hybrids and clean-diesel cars qualify? They once did, but no more. The federal tax-credit clock ran out on December 30, 2010, for clean-diesel vehicles and conventional hybrids, meaning gas-electric models such as the Hyundai Sonata Hybrid or the Toyota Prius. These vehicles don't have batteries that can be plugged into the power grid.”

## 11.15 OTHER OPERATION FACTORS

Alternative fuel powertrains have different infrastructure requirements. With some, venting systems must be built into service facilities. Some require special equipment beyond standard laptop computers and service tools. They all require unique training for service technicians. In many instances, the expertise is contracted to support alternative fuel systems. This is especially true in the early stages of implementing alternative fuel vehicles where the number of trucks may be small.

Disposal of waste products from servicing vehicles is a differential cost as alternative fuel vehicles differ in the amount of coolants, lubricants and other hazardous materials handled during servicing. There are costs to these, whether recycled, salvaged or disposed of.

Safety is a topic where alternative fuels vary because of differences in storage pressures, combustibility, energy content, temperatures, voltages, etc. Each requires training and different safety equipment and procedures, and training must include drivers, technicians and emergency response personnel.

There are a number of what NACFE terms “soft costs” buried in overhead of running fleets that may differ between choices of alternative fuel powertrains.

## 12.1 RENEWABLE ENERGY

The definition of renewable per Merriam Webster is “capable of being replaced by natural ecological cycles or sound management practices”, by Oxford as “a natural resource or source of energy that is not depleted by use, such as water, wind, or solar power,” and Cambridge states as a counter point that “oil, natural gas, and coal are non-renewable fuels [198][199][200].” Branding and marketing have created the terms “renewable natural gas” and “renewable diesel” to differentiate from fuels produced directly from fossil coal, gas and oil. Sometimes the term “synthetic” is used to describe the fact that the fuel is created rather than mined.

In some cases, the renewable fuel is a byproduct of another critical industry, such as dairy, where the production of milk produces volumes of raw manure known as biomass that can be converted into fuel. ANL did a case study on renewable natural gas from cows as a transportation fuel in 2017 [196]. The report outlines that waste can be used directly through anaerobic digesters to create natural gas for use in on-site power generation systems. The natural gas so produced can also be used to fuel heavy trucks. The report defines anaerobic digesting as “a process employing heat and beneficial bacteria in an oxygen-free environment to break down organic materials and produce a methane-rich gas mixture (biogas), which can be purified to produce a renewable form of natural gas (RNG) [196].”

ANL’s report cites the government’s Renewable Fuel Standard RS2 which has many definitions for renewable fuels as the topic gets more complex. The original EPA regulatory definition of renewable fuel was ‘‘any motor vehicle fuel that is used to replace or reduce the quantity of fossil fuel present in a fuel mixture used to fuel a motor vehicle” with separate definitions for cellulosic biomass ethanol, waste derived ethanol, and biodiesel [197]. This clarifies that “renewable” does not mean zero emissions. They are better than current fossilbased fuels, but not perhaps only 50% better at reducing emissions. There are well-to-wheel questions as well as far as the extent of renewability since there are fertilizers and pesticides involved in farming, and there are fuels associated with the farm equipment itself. Renewable may mean in some cases that there are rationalized levels of use of non-renewable sources in the well-to-wheel supply chain.

## 12.2 CLEAN

“Clean” as an adjective describing fuels rarely includes definitions in marketing and advertisements. Clean diesel, clean coal, clean oil, clean hydrogen, clean natural gas, etc. are all terms where links can be readily found in volume using internet search engines. Truth in advertising rules would suggest that lawyers assisting marketers have found some nuanced solid legal ground to stand on in using the term. Still, the wide use of this term as a marketing phrase without qualification confuses comparison of the fuels. Marketing is also careful to clarify where tank to wheel is clean, but may not mention that well to tank may not be, or qualify with the statement, “if the electricity to create the fuel originates from solar or wind power, then well to wheel is clean.” That statement is rarely followed in advertising with details of when and how that sourcing will dominate in a region.

The use of the phrase “clean” is problematic. NACFE concludes that apparently, all alternative fuels can be clean, but some may be cleaner than others. NACFE recommends avoiding use of the term since it has no consistent meaning. The same can be true for the expression “near zero emission.”

## 12.3 NEAR ZERO EMISSION

As with the adjective “clean”, “near zero emission” (or “partial zero emission”) is another vague and potentially confusing term. Clearly 99 is nearer to zero than 100, but so is 1. “Near” is a very relative evaluation. As with the term “renewable,” being based on a 50% reduction in current diesel GHG emissions, at some future time, those near zero emission vehicles may represent 100% of the transport emissions, so a “nearer to zero” definition may be needed in the future. Every percentage requires a baseline off which it is relevant. In transportation emissions and efficiency, that baseline is a constantly moving and improving target.

The math involving using percentages demonstrates that percentages without context of a baseline can be deceiving. For example, a 50% improvement in fuel efficiency for a baseline of 6 mpg means an improvement of 3 mpg. That same 50% improvement for a baseline of 10 mpg would be an improvement of 5 mpg. The baseline matters when stating percentages.

Emission reductions tend to register off of specific years, for example the California Air Resources Board uses 1990 [203]. EPA’s Phase 1 GHG regulations reference heavy truck emissions to a 2010 model year and engine without idle shutdown [205]. In 2014, EPA published a Corporate GHG Goal Evaluation Model where individual companies and states can choose their own baseline starting point for stating and tracking improvements [204]. NACFE finds that baselines, when stated, can differ significantly.

Zero emission, on the other hand, is pretty explicit. The DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center defines a zero-emission vehicle as “a vehicle that produces no criteria pollutant, toxic air contaminant, or greenhouse gas emissions when stationary or operating [201].” No relative reference to a baseline is needed, zero is zero.

A search of regulations can find statutory definitions for near zero emission and partial zero emission through the state of California Air Resources Board and state regulations [206].

Similar to the use of the term “clean,” “near zero emission” is in all practicality a questionable term without qualifiers. NACFE recommends that advertising use of the term includes a clear statement of those qualifiers and specifically a baseline value and time frame for any percentages stated. Fleets should request the details of any claims using this terminology.

## 12.4 MONEY INFLUENCE

Alternative fuels represent a significant opportunity for long-term cash flow and profits. Existing energy supply systems face risks of obsolescence and so are actively looking to repurpose their existing infrastructure for the long-term and extend the life of their existing profit channels. Some energy suppliers see new market opportunities for existing capacity and are eager to capture long-term new market share in the evolving transportation marketplace. Vehicle manufacturers and their supply chains are seeing opportunity to capture new market share in a largely undefined zero emission vehicle new market, a sense of tremendous opportunity not unlike the Oklahoma Land Rush of 1889 or the dot.com boom of the 1990s, brought on by the current lack of production of zero emission product and growing regulatory and environmental demand for that product.

# 13 SUMMARY FINDINGS

Alternative fuel powertrains are developing because fossil fuel-based energy streams are increasingly problematic under environmental and regulatory pressures. All alternative fuels discussed in this report represent potential improvement versus the existing fleet of primarily diesel heavy-duty trucks. Diesel technology is not static and continues to improve, and will continue to be an option both as primary powertrains and for use in a number of hybrid variations. NACFE finds there is no single solution technology, no silver bullet, to replace existing diesels. Rather, the variety of duty cycles and fleet operations will lead to market share for all these technologies, at least in the near- to mid-term timeframe. In the extreme long-run, however, if the world migrates to requiring only zero emission vehicles, only battery electric, fuel cell electric, and catenary electric vehicles have the ability to be zero emission from well to wheel, when power originates exclusively from wind and solar generation. That future is at least decades away.

Fleets are being presented with a range of alternative fuels and powertrains for commercial Class 7/8 trucks. These alternatives are in the early stages of maturity in comparison to the diesel powertrains they may replace. Natural gas powertrains are significantly more mature, having entered production in significant numbers over the last decade. Some alternative fuels, such as renewable versions of natural gas and diesel may go directly into existing powertrains with little or no additional truck modifications. Others, such as hydrogen fuel cells, battery electrics, and a variety of potential hybrids, require greater vehicle redesign. Overall, the volume of production level alternative fuel vehicles is very limited today. While many manufacturers are ramping up initial production lines for the 2020-2025 timeframe, significant production volumes are likely not feasible until the latter half of the decade, after designs have been through a few iterations based on field experience and market demand has increased substantially.

# 14 RECOMMENDATIONS

Trucking technology is constantly adjusting to new demands. For over two decades, the focus has been on getting improved performance while reducing emissions from diesel engines and the vehicles that house them. The future of trucking is facing growing demands for near zero and ultimately zero-emission solutions. Responding to this demand is a growing list of alternative fuels and related powertrains which are now starting to reach the point of production-quality vehicles. While natural gas powertrains have the lead in maturation, the bulk of the current investment is going into the newer options like fuel cells and battery electric vehicles.

NACFE concludes there will not be a single solution to replace diesels, nor will diesels be entirely replaced in any short timeframe, if ever. Required duty cycles will drive further development of ever-more optimized and specialized powertrain solutions. Gone will be the paradigm that the same tractor will be transferred through a series of ownership over 20 years with consistent utility in every different vocation it meets, from on-highway freight hauling, to regional use, to local use, to bulk material hauling, and finally to farm use. Instead, tractors will be purpose built, optimized by OEMs for specific duty cycles, with specific ranges, payloads, routes. These duty cycles will also be optimized around local, regional, national, and international regulations, as required to legally operate. Regional access to certain energy types will also factor into this specialization.

Return on investment will still drive decision makers, but new factors will need to be included in future investment decisions beyond just the familiar and easily quantified factors. Hard decisions will need to be made where zero and near zero emission zones eliminate the option of pure diesel vehicles. In that scenario, alternative fuel technologies will be weighed on their own merits and against each others’ rather than against diesel’s.

Comparison between technologies will become clearer in time as each alternative fuel and related powertrain tend to perform better in different scenarios. The poly-fuel future combined with greater duty cycle specialization will likely mean fleets may have several alternative fuels in their operations, just as today they may have gasoline delivery vans, CNG regional day cabs, and diesel long haul units.

What is largely missing in the debate on alternative fuels is significant production vehicle operational experience with most of these alternatives. The ability to compare choices requires a solid foundation of factual information on uptime, maintenance and repair costs, efficiencies, training costs, energy costs, infrastructure costs, etc., all factors well quantified for diesels.

Obtaining this required data means having actual production trucks, not prototypes hand-held by OEM engineers and service technicians. This data requires that fleets document actual operations to a comparable level of detail to their diesel units, and perhaps even further, including all the hard and soft factors that go into actual business costs today.

In order to satisfy accountants and managers tasked with comparing alternatives, approaches will be needed for how to quantify in dollars soft factors like driver fatigue or retention differences due to quieter and cleaner tractor operation. Other soft factors needing to be quantified in dollars include handling and disposal of toxic materials, training costs, changes in uptime, changes in things like tire wear and replacement, true costs of downtime, etc.

Actual tractors in use on actual routes with real drivers hauling real freight will quickly replace the often-inappropriate extrapolations from automotive experience and bus experience. This real data will also allow factual information to replace optimistic or biased marketing claims or biased competitive conjecture.

The availability of production trucks in operations will challenge standards and guidance organizations to improve and publish comparable methods of real-world performance evaluation, with a focus on all three segments of the systems, tank-to-wheel, pump-to-wheel, and well-to-wheel. Selective cherry-picking of results will still occur, but the growing availability of real-world data will permit unbiased agencies to offer up consistent reporting and evaluation for fleets seeking good information.

While freight by truck occurs nearly everywhere in the world, each market is unique, a point often lost in simplistic comparisons between nations.

Insecurity in investing in alternative fuel vehicles is largely the result of a lack of solid information on which to base decisions. The vast amounts of investment money going into different alternative fuels tends to create vested interests that can color and bias analyses and decision making. Having real-world data evaluated by non-vested third parties will help investors and management focus on actualities and cushion the negative aspects induced by financial speculators.

# 15 CONCLUSIONS

Summarizing a report of this magnitude requires identifying the key takeaways. NACFE concludes there are six major findings supported by the research for this report.
* North American freight movement is becoming more predictable with dedicated routes enabled by e-commerce and other technologies, offering better duty cycles for alternative powertrains.
* Each alternative fuel powertrain offers benefits in the short term compared to current diesel and may have enough duty cycle scale to offer TCO and emission savings.
* CBEVs and Fuel Cell trucks will be capable of lower total cost of ownership in the 2030 timeframe.
* Vehicle specifications will be more optimized for the duty cycle and technology of the first user, limiting the applicability of the equipment for second or third users.
* There will be a “messy middle” until CBEVs and FC vehicles alone power these trucks, as alternatives offer significant improvements over the diesel and gasoline baselines.
* A future zero-emission freight world will only have electric based vehicles (CBEV, FCEV, or catenary electric) powered well-to-wheel from truly renewable sources such as hydro, solar and wind.

The frenetic pace of investment combined with innovation and environmental demands are increasing the number of viable alternative fuel choices. North American fleets that in 2011 could choose between diesel and CNG are now faced with the tyranny of choice, as psychologist Barry Schwartz outlined in his 2005 Scientific American report. That research showed that “more choice is not always better than less”

and “making a decision becomes increasingly daunting as the number of choices rises. Worse, after making a selection, they (buyers) are nagged by the alternatives they have not had time to investigate. [188].”

An Economist article from 2010 summarizes Schwartz. “At this point, choice no longer liberates, but debilitates. It might even be said to tyrannise [189].” There is an expectation that a perfect choice exists. The absence of a clear silver bullet winner in the alternative fuels arena leads some to delay decision making in hopes of better future information.

Fleets want to be able to jump to the end of the book to see which technology wins out, to avoid the “messy middle” as coined by Steve Hamburg, Chief Scientist for EDF – that long period of transition. NACFE believes the book on alternative fuels is not yet written. The messy middle will be marked by a good deal of trial and error through significant real-world experimentation. When the transition is mostly over, hindsight, having 20:20 vision, will identify some technologies as “bridges” between the present and the future. These bridge technologies will have provided timely needed trucks for hauling freight but ultimately will become less desirable in the long run or relegated to specific duty cycles.

Diesel has been the predominant fuel for powering heavy-duty commercial freight trucks and likely will maintain extensive use for some time into the future for a range of practical reasons, chief of which is annual production rates of OEMs (perhaps 300,000 vehicles/year) and the slow attrition rate of the existing pool of vehicles (2,000,000+) in the field. However, the growing list of alternative fuel powertrains are becoming commercially available, progressing from the prototype and idea stages into early production and real-world use.

These alternatives all too easily get cast as opponents, in some winner take all match. In reality, just as today’s diesel trucks span a considerable range of manufacturers, vehicle variations including engine sizes and option content, tomorrow’s fleets will likely see alternative fuel vehicle types operating together, each optimized for specific duty cycles and regional factors. Fleets that see long haul, regional and urban uses out of the same facilities may have three different alternative fuel powertrains side by side at loading docks, and each of those may be tailored to specific duty cycles. OEMs have repeatedly pointed out that today’s diesel trucks may have millions of optional permutations based on the variety of fleet needs.

There are no “standard” vehicles today in North America, so why expect them tomorrow? The interim future is a poly-fuel one, with ever more specialized vehicles optimized for best performance on specific duty cycles. The alternative fuel powertrains each may perform best under different conditions. There are no silver bullet solutions, no single Super Bowl champion, nor should fleets expect any based on their experiences with endless optimizations of diesel trucks.

The alternative fuel powertrains come in a spectrum of configurations. There is not one type of battery electric truck, not one type of fuel cell vehicle, or hybrid, or even CNG or propane. There is no one lithium ion battery, no one fuel cell, no one hybrid configuration. Just as with today’s diesels, the permutations possible are immense, trading off weight, range, complexity, cost, etc. to arrive at any one particular configuration.

Though NACFE concludes that in the long run, electric powertrains will predominate the marketplace. The fundamental reason for this is that battery electric powertrains are the most efficient use of energy

much more significant losses. It is actually more efficient to burn natural gas at a large power plant to generate steam for a turbine generator to generate electricity for an electric vehicle than it is to combust the energy directly in an engine in a vehicle. Another example is that converting water or anything else for that matter into useable hydrogen for a fuel cell has significant conversion losses and requires significant electricity, and then the fuel cell has to convert the hydrogen back into electricity again. The least losses, fewest conversions, occur through electric charging of batteries from the grid. Today’s batteries are not mature or proven for heavy truck use, but they have the potential in terms of meeting needs for many duty cycles.

The total cost of ownership for alternative fuel vehicles is a moving game. Reliability builds from field experience and continuous improvement in production. The production level of alternative fuel trucks in any appreciable volume, are just now starting to enter the market for Class 7/8. The exceptions are CNG/RNG, which has about a decade head start on the electric and fuel cell market, and renewable diesel, which can operate with no changes to current diesel powertrains. Assessment of TCO at this point is largely projection, estimate, extrapolation and educated guess in the absence of field history. NACFE documented 20 significant unknowns on predicting the future of electric trucks in its Medium-Duty Electric Trucks – Cost of Ownership report [57]. Many of these unknowns apply across the spectrum of Class 7/8 alternative fuel choices. While automotive and bus experience has some applicability, the duty cycles, requirements, and tradeoffs for those are significantly different than those of Class 7/8 freight.

Purpose-built designs for Class 7/8 trucks are needed, rather than being built from existing parts designed for cars and buses. These require sales volumes for OEMs to make capital investments in new production designs. Of the new powertrain alternatives, only CNG/RNG has seen any appreciable production volumes to date.

Force fitting alternative fuels into the diesel model is short sighted. Each alternative fuel and related powertrain likely can be better optimized outside of the constraint of artificially back-fitting to diesel’s century of choices. Purpose-built designs for Class 7/8 need to be allowed to emphasize positive performance characteristics and to minimize the effect of deficiencies on hauling freight. Just as diesels have pushed changes that accentuate their attributes, alternative fuels need to be permitted room to innovate over the diesel model. That may mean operational changes that can improve asset utilization.

Freight weight possible with each technology requires real production trucks in real operations to validate projections. There is a tendency to assume all trucks operate at 80,000-lbs. gross vehicle weight, a poor assumption that biases comparison of alternatives. NACFE sees a spectrum of operating weights from empty to max weight, with some 70% of runs below max weight and most loads cubing out rather that weighing out, as NACFE documented in Battery Electric Trucks – Where They Make Sense [56].

Range sensitivity for alternative fuels is based in on-board energy storage capacity. Diesel is particularly efficient at being a storage mechanism for energy on vehicles, having perhaps the smallest chassis space requirements when not considering the emissions, exhaust and engine systems to be necessary parts for using the fuel. Other fuels like hydrogen, CNG/RNG, LNG, propane, each require special pressurized tanks, some being capable of 10,000 psi pressure. These tanks can dictate additional frame length for tractors for equivalent ranges.

It may be that equivalent potential range to diesel is never achieved as operators may regularly carry excess diesel fuel for their duty cycles. Two 120-gallon diesel fuel tanks, a common configuration in the past, equates to ranges of 1,680 miles at 7 miles per gallon. A fleet averaging 300 miles per day in regional haul would only see 1,500 miles in a week before prudently refueling for the next week. Modern diesels can see efficiencies of 10 miles per gallon, for a range of 2,400 miles between fill ups. Alternative fuel choices may not be required to carry this extra margin of safety on fuel by tailoring on-board stored energy to actual duty cycles.

That excessive margin of safety on fuel comes from unknowns in route and load planning. Growth in connectivity, real time asset tracking and improved asset utilization – the tractor, the trailer, the driver and the load - may lead to much better pre-planned routing as outlined in NACFE’s More Regional Haul – An Opportunity for Fleets [4].

Fill times are manifestations of operational factors, and are not necessarily black-and-white decision factors. A fleet caring about fill times likely has the driver filling the trucks, inherently trading off the driver’s hours of service driving in exchange for flexibility in fueling the vehicle. Other fleets assign depot personnel to do the filling when the tractor and driver are not in service, allowing the driver to focus on moving product – the fundamental reason the fleets operate the vehicles.

Fleets may be overwhelmed with too many choices and not enough factual information. The trucking industry is in a very transitionary time with respect to alternative powertrains. NACFE feels that fleets that plan to wait to see market leaders emerge will likely miss opportunities to influence the trajectory of these technologies, denying OEMs valuable fleet feedback necessary to improve the product offerings. Major decisions that span years will occur before these fleets may get involved. Internally, these cautious fleets will also miss out on accelerating their learning curve versus their competition. Being late to the alternative fuels party may compound competitiveness challenges as internal factors such as maintenance, repair, supply chains, drivers, managers, etc. are well behind the learning curve on new technologies versus their more forward-postured competitors. The tyranny of choice should not prohibit experimentation and familiarization. Fleets have a long history of doing their own evaluations on technologies. The alternative fuels arena should be no different.

Predicting innovation is tough due to the complex interaction of market forces, regulation, investment, geo-political issues, the environment, and disruption from outside developments. It’s been over 20 years since the first significant steps were implemented in 1996 on EPA improving emissions from diesel trucks, back when expected fuel economies hovered in the 5-mpg range. Today, production trucks in real freight operations can exceed 10 mpg while concurrently meeting today’s aggressive emissions constraints and while fleets and OEMs alike are seeing profitability. All along the way there have been parties arguing fervently that changes to diesel were not technically or economically feasible.

History shows frequently that “impossible” is an opinion, where “possible” is a commitment. Alternative fuels and their related powertrains are just taking their first steps in production. Where will they be in 20 years’ time? NACFE has faith in the trucking industry to work out the details for these alternative fuels and powertrains for a future where they will coexist along with improved diesel ones. Each will be optimized and specialized for specific duty cycles and regions. Each will benefit the environment over today’s vehicles. And fleets and OEMs will remain profitable.

### 17.9 RENEWABLE DIESEL & BIO DIESEL

Shelley Ernst succinctly summarized the difference between bio-diesel and renewable diesel in a Government Fleet article in Mar 2016, stating

**Biodiesel:** Unlike petroleum diesel, biodiesel does not rely on fossil fuels. Instead, it is made from vegetable oils and/or animal fats, which are renewable. It is also processed differently than petroleum diesel, using a process called transesterification. This process introduces oxygen into the fuel, which can cause issues with freezing temperature, separation during storage, algae growth, and higher emissions. Biodiesel is generally blended with petroleum diesel at a 5% to 20% ratio.

**Renewable Diesel:** Like biodiesel, renewable diesel is not a fossil fuel. Instead, it is made of nonpetroleum renewable resources such as natural fats, vegetable oils, and greases. “Renewable diesel, much like biodiesel, is derived from waste agricultural products, particularly waste vegetable oils and waste animal fats,” said Allen Schaeffer, executive director of the Diesel Technology Forum. “As long as we grow soybeans and produce livestock, the waste derived after these products have been processed into food can be refined into a clean, low-carbon fuel. Renewable diesel is processed similar to the way petroleum diesel is produced, which makes it chemically the same as petroleum diesel. 3.Because it has the same chemical structure as petroleum diesel, renewable diesel can be used in engines that are designed to run on conventional diesel fuel — with no blending required. Renewable diesel comes with a number of benefits: It’s made of renewable sources, it burns clean, and it works just like traditional diesel. Fleets making the switch won’t encounter performance issues, nor will they need to modify equipment or fueling infrastructure to adopt the fuel. [93].”

### 17.11 DIESEL BASELINE

The baseline for all discussions of alternatives for Class 7/8 tractors is the ubiquitous diesel engine.

#### Infrastructure

Diesel as a fuel has been developing its infrastructure for a century. While much of the original capital investment in these facilities likely is a less critical factor in promoting use, on-going maintenance, operation, delivery, and environmental management are part of the price paid in each gallon of fuel purchased. Fleets should consider that this infrastructure has a number of built in subsidies.
