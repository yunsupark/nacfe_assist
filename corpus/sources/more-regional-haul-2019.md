## 4 INTRODUCTION

The term Regional freight has many interpretations and expectations, and they are evolving. NACFE sees
Regional freight as a proving ground for new technologies, new operational models and business
practices. There are a number of trends interacting to define the future of Regional freight hauling. This
report focuses on NACFE’s assessment of current and projections of the future of Regional haul as a
result of interacting trends:

* Driver hiring and retention
* Growth in e-commerce
* The push towards immediate delivery
* Growth in GPS based asset tracking systems (tractor, trailer, load and driver)
* Advances in technologies such as electric and hybrid vehicles
* Vehicle automation
* Innovations in load matching systems
* Increased use of long combination vehicles
* Data collection and mining
* Vehicle specialization

## 5 SCOPE

A simplistic characterization of freight moved by commercial tractor-trailer divides the population
hauling freight as Over-The-Road (OTR) or Regional. OTR implies drivers and loads on any given trip may
be routed anywhere in North America. Regional implies some constraint on geographic operation.

Although the scope of the term “regional” can vary significantly by fleet and driver, one consistent factor
in many of the interpretations is that the driver returns home on some predictable basis. NACFE’s report
defines the scope of Regional as operations that regularly return the driver home in a predictable time
frame.

NACFE limits its definition of Regional in this report to Class 7 and 8 tractor-trailer operations, those
vehicles with Gross Vehicle Weight Ratings (GVWR) above 26,001 lbs., as shown in Figure 1.

a load — at a point of origin, then routes it on a lane to deliver to a destination within a prescribed time
for a specified cost as illustrated in Figure 3. Freight routing is both an art and a science, manipulating
these variables in a profitable way when scaled over multiple loads, locations, drivers and equipment.

making fleets more efficient focused first on making the tractor more efficient, then the trailer, then the

capital equipment investments are tied to trucks that are moving, so are not the whole story on freight.

A critical element of freight is the driver, where hours of service rules control the useful period of work,
whether the truck and trailer are moving or not. The other critical element is the density of the load. A
driver pulling a 55,000 lb. gross vehicle weight (GVW) tractor trailer is less freight efficient than one
pulling a 75,000 lb. GVW one. Put in terms of hours of service, optimizing the driver’s time on road and
freight efficiency are the primary areas of opportunity for fleets looking to improve performance.

During transport, the driver has hours of service constraints that combined with detention time and
traffic congestion, effectively limit the daily miles possible. For example, a driver held up 30 minutes
waiting for a load at a warehouse, and then able to average only 45 mph due to traffic conditions, and
then held up in line at the delivery warehouse for 30 minutes has a daily range potential of 450 miles
over an 11-hour day. The Journal of Commerce (JOC) highlighted data for 2015 from telematics provider
PeopleNet which “found in more than 200,000 records, drivers averaged 6.09 hours a day [128].”
PeopleNet recognizes this is relatively small sample of the entire truck driving population, stating, “That
data could include daily duty hours for various types of drivers, including short-haul or regional or less-
than-truckload drivers with shorter routes and more non-driving duty time, as well as long-haul
truckload drivers, but the average number still points to a problem [129].”

Once the load has been delivered, asset management becomes even more critical, independently
determining what to do next for the driver, the tractor and the trailer. Ideally, fleets want their assets
hauling paid freight at all times. Return loads or back hauls may not be possible at that location, so the
assets may need to be repositioned to a new location where a paying load exists. Predicting where
assets may need to go over the course of a week turns into a game of probabilities. Load matching
becomes critical to maintain driver freight efficiency.

The following sections of this report examine the variety of challenges and opportunities for improving
the efficiency of delivering freight. Many of these are interrelated.

NACFE finds Regional length of haul has many interpretations ranging from a 100-mile to 500-mile
radius from a point of origin. Net distances can see over 2,000 miles in a week before the tractor and
driver return home. Trailers may never return.

Trailers primarily exist to move freight. Fleets generally do not care which trailer is used, only that the
freight gets delivered on time. Only in recent years have fleets started using GPS tracking of trailers, and
recording which trailers are mated to which tractors on each run. This growing database will aid in
optimizing future trailer asset utilization, but at this point in time, NACFE believes the state of the
industry is that fleets are not focused on pairing tractors and trailers based on performance. In the
current state of freight movement, fleets are just scrambling for drivers and any tractors.

New optimized tractors and trailers migrate into fleets typically in portions. Some fleets NACFE talked
with replace a third of their units each year. Rarely do fleets replace all of their equipment at one time.
The pool of vehicles then tends to have a variety of model year tractors and trailers. These assets tend
to also get distributed over time across several locations. The ability to intentionally pair the best
performing tractor with the best performing trailer in a fleet is then constrained by what vintage of
technologies is available at a location at that moment.

Estimating the benefits of new tractor and trailer technologies requires modeling routes and duty cycles.
While national averages can be estimated, there are significant variations by fleet and by region. A
trailer may be dedicated to an A-B-A type route as shown in Figure 6 between St. Louis and Denver.
However, that trailer may see an A-B-C-A type route, or it may see an A-B-C-D-E-F type route where the
trailer never gets back to its point of origin. NACFE feels this latter is more representative of the majority

An anecdotal example of the unpredictability of routing involved an engineering assessment of the
benefits of a complete aerodynamic package on a specific shipping route. A supplier and a shipper were
identified where the loads were consistently shipped from Point A to Point B on a dedicated route with
dedicated tractors and drivers. The shipper committed to provide several new trailers for the testing,
and the engineer’s company agreed to outfit them with the aerodynamic packages. A baseline was
established by using trip recorders installed in the tractors and using the trailers as delivered without
aerodynamic devices for a period of six weeks. Then the engineer installed skirts and boattails on all the
trailers. The second phase of testing began, and the first week’s data was obtained. Then massive winter
storms hit the eastern U.S. The test trailers had to be diverted by the shipper to cover other
commitments, and never returned to the original route.

This example is echoed by others who have tried similar testing and was demonstrated in the NACFE
Run on Less testing in September 2017. In that multi-truck, real world testing, the goal was to
demonstrate MPGs in excess of 10 MPG. The trailer usage was affected due to the impacts of two major
hurricanes on trailer utilization. Non-aero rental trailers had to be used by the ROL test fleet on some
routes [75].

central hub, such as Atlanta. The fleet may operate from any number of supplier manufacturing or

### 8.3.3.1 Inside 80,000 lb. GVW

Increasing weight and/or volume capacity of loads can be done in a variety of ways. At the product level,
reduce the amount of packaging dunnage. Dunnage is essentially the packaging that is needed to get a
product from the manufacturer to its end buyer. It includes pallets, boxes, shipping popcorn, bubble
wrap, etc. to protect the product in shipment, and it also includes the product packaging itself such as
blister packs or plastic tubs, with brand imaging, and instructions. Companies have a cost reduction
incentive to reduce the weight of dunnage at all levels as it reduces paid freight weight and raw material
costs for products. They have a cost incentive to reduce the cube, or volume, of their packages as many
companies now face dimensional billing for shipments. Dimensional billing combines the size and weight
into the cost of shipping [44]. There are environmental incentives as well to reducing waste.

### 8.3.2 Above 80,000 lb. GVW

The two graphs, Figure 33 and Figure 34, on typical vehicle weights highlight that some percentage of
vehicles in the U.S. currently run at GVW weights higher than 80,000 lbs. These are typically under
special permits. Some of these loads are tractors pulling two 53' trailers. Mihelic, Smith and Ellis
documented a comparison of two 53' tractor-trailers in a platoon versus one tractor pulling two 48'
trailers in SAE paper 2015-01-2897 titled *Aerodynamic Comparison of Tractor-Trailer Platooning and A-Train Configuration* [48]. The evaluation shows that two tractor-trailer units in a platoon separated by a
30-foot gap each have reduced drag versus a truck running alone. The report also shows that an A-Train
double has an even greater drag reduction than the platooning pair when normalized to the same
freight weight. The paper poses the question: if platooning trucks are of interest, why not consider
double trailers?

### 8.3.4 Increased Build Out of Warehouses

Freight infrastructure will continue to evolve, with greater use of local warehousing and last mile
delivery. What this does for the driver pool is that last mile delivery can be accomplished with smaller
trucks and even cars more readily safely driven by a larger population of individuals. The data that
supports this trend begins with the massive investment in warehousing in recent years as reported in
2018 by Bloomberg: "Industrial real estate is crushing offices, malls, (and) apartments. Forget your fancy
office towers. The future lies in warehouses [14]." This growth in local warehouse space is thought to be

the largest freight marketplace in America) and by ATRI shown in Figure 5 [15][16].

How does adding a warehouse shorten length of haul? Consider a hypothetical example where a
product is produced at a factory in Nashville for delivery to a home in Dallas. The direct route between
factory and home would be 687 miles (per Google maps) as shown in Figure 44. However, that's not
how freight works. The product is bundled with other packages at the Nashville factory and shipped to
some freight center such as in Houston. It's then separated and combined with other shipments headed
from Houston to the DFW area where it goes to another warehouse in Fort Worth, and finally is picked
up for local delivery with another bundle of packages headed to a neighborhood in Dallas. The actual
length of shipment for that product is 1,149 miles, 60% farther than the direct route. Now if a new
intermediate warehouse is built in Texarkana, the package needs no longer go to Houston first, but
might go directly from Texarkana to Fort Worth, shortening the total miles to 751. The more
intermediate warehouses, the shorter the net package travel, hence a reduction length of haul for the
trucks.

### 8.3.5 Reduction in Driver Detention Time

Drivers as company assets have uptime and downtime, just like machines in a factory. And just as in a
factory, the goal is to keep the production line running all the time, so fleets want to keep their drivers
actually driving. Unfortunately, part of driver's on-road daily time is time spent waiting — waiting in line
to go through a warehouse gate, waiting at a facility for a trailer to be released, waiting at a facility for
an open dock to drop a trailer, waiting because the truck arrived early or late, and more.

Figure 46 illustrates that a driver experiencing a total of one hour of detention time, for example waiting

travel a maximum of 500 miles in 11 hours behind the wheel. Two hours of detention limits that range
to 450 miles. Some large fleets see average speeds in the mid 40's mph due to traffic, weather, and
other factors, and the mid 50's can occur even for fleets running speed limiters at 65 mph.

### 8.3.6 Reduction in Traffic Congestion Impacts to Trucking

Another focus for future technology is improving drivers range potential per shift by reducing the impact
of traffic congestion on driver mileage. As shown in the graph on the impact of detention on driver
range time (see Figure 46), net average speed over the course of a trip impacts driving range per shift.
Weather, traffic conditions and speed limits impact average speeds. A truck with zero detention time
that averages 40 mph has a maximum range of 440 miles versus one that averages 50 mph with a
maximum range of 550 miles, a 90-mile difference. For a Regional A-B-A out and back one day trip, the
average 40 mph truck can only go 220 miles from home, where the average 50 mph one can go out 275
miles and still get home in one day.

The U.S. Department of Transportation stated in its preliminary 2018 Transportation Statistics Annual
Report that, "Road congestion is one of the most visible and challenging characteristics of transportation
system performance. Relieving road congestion has been one of the main incentives for investing in the
nation's road network. Since the early 1980s, when national congestion indices were first applied, the
trend in road congestion was steadily increasing in urban areas of all sizes. The most recent data
suggests that the trend is now mixed, with some areas still seeing increased congestion, while others

### 8.3.8 Driver Pay and Benefits

A key aspect of the driver shortage is probably the sensitive subject of driver pay and benefits. The
traditional business model for trucking freight is that drivers are paid by the mile. As the supply of active
drivers decreases and demand for shipments increases, attracting more drivers through increased pay
and benefits is one solution. The intent of increases is to accomplish several objectives:

* Bring existing licensed drivers back into the industry who have left the active pool for other jobs
* Attract new drivers to the active pool from other vocations
* Keep existing drivers from seeking new employment in other fields (reduce attrition)
* Keep existing drivers from changing fleets (reduce turnover rate)
* Attract new young drivers into their first job instead of other vocations

There are several business trade-offs that result and NACFE is not assessing if this is a viable solution,
only that many fleets in 2018 and 2019 reported compensation changes such as signing bonuses,

### 8.3.9 Uptime

Uptime, and its converse, downtime, are factors that restrict driver on-road time moving freight. A
service issue that takes a truck out of service often also takes the driver out of service and sometimes
impacts on-time delivery of the freight. International, for example, in announcing their new 24/7
customer support Uptime Command Center, highlighted at a 2017 conference that their "mission is to
get vehicles back on the road as quickly as possible and no longer than 48 hours after it is first
diagnosed," according to Michael Cancelliere, president of Trucks & Parts [63]. Stefan Kurschner,
Daimler Trucks North America (DTNA) Senior Vice President Aftermarket stated in a 2018 interview, that
customers want service groups to "Tell me more, tell me sooner, and fix my truck faster." DTNA
announced in an article by Deborah Lockridge in *Heavy Duty Trucking*, "that it's working toward a goal of
24-hour turnaround service times at its dealers. Today about 56% of our dealerships are achieving that.
And 80% in our Elite Support network [64]."

#### 8.3.9.1 Improved Uptime Technology

Vehicle OEM's have warranty departments and track costs for repairs, including labor and parts. In the
past it was less likely that they tracked the true cost to the vehicle operator in terms of downtime. A
leaky air hose requiring a quick snip and reinsertion in a fitting might appear trivial in warranty costs, but
if the vehicle is down for half a day or more waiting for service, that has significant cost ramifications to
both the driver and the carrier.

Technology advances like over-the-air monitoring (OTA) and geo-fencing of service facilities can
accumulate the time the vehicle is actually out of service to help reflect the true fleet cost in downtime.
OTA allows service groups to monitor each vehicle and predict a service need ahead of a failure,
allowing preventive maintenance to be scheduled. Geo-fencing identifies when vehicles enter and leave
a facility, keeping digital tabs on the time on site. Fleets that accurately maintain and monitor uptime
and downtime metrics for their vehicles can use that data to prioritize where opportunities are for

improving driver on-road time. Fleets could, in the back-office applications, tag incident costs from
missed shipments, late deliveries, driver hoteling, etc. to each service event, making it possible to spot
recurring issues and focus on improvements. Such is an opportunity that modern real time digital asset
tracking provides.

Jim Buell, Executive Vice President of Sales and Marketing at FleetNet, a national provider of fleet
maintenance and emergency roadside assistance for medium- and heavy-duty commercial equipment,

also stated that the best in class fleets were seeing one breakdown every 75,000 miles [60]. That
translates to between one to two times per year for the best Regional operators.

Many vehicle repairs require service technicians to physically touch each vehicle. For isolated repairs, a
given tractor must be scheduled for repair at a facility, taking it out of service for some period of time.
Where the problem is found to be endemic to an entire model line, fleets have to schedule each vehicle
to be serviced.

However, many issues relate only to software. Where the problem is one of only software, the ability to
do OTA updates to an entire fleet at one time has significant potential for reducing tractor downtime
and hence improve driver on-road time. A problem found with one tractor can immediately be fixed on
all similar tractors in the fleet without taking them out of service. Jonathan Randall, Mack Trucks' Senior
Vice President of Sales and Marketing, stated, "A lot of dwell time is due to work with software systems,
and we are successfully working to eliminate the need for fleets to bring trucks into fleets for dealers for

those with greater use of digital control systems.

Predictive analytics on maintenance such as with Noodle.AI with the fleet NFI as described by the *Wall

can be designed into new OEM tractor models, or it can be retrofitted, such as offered by Geotab with
Volvo. They partnered in 2017 to provide 24/7 monitoring of new Volvo tractors and retrofit older
models as far back as model year 2010 [61]. Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and virtual imaging
can also help reduce downtime.

Connectivity, GPS based asset tracking, geo-fencing location usage, over-air-monitoring and preventive
maintenance, service prioritization and scheduling, AI, virtual reality imaging, etc., are all technologies
that can help improve uptime and keep drivers on the road. The challenging is integrating the mass of
available information into useful and readily apparent actionable data.

## FREIGHT SYSTEM MODELING

Freight system modeling is complex and described by the term "dynamic allocation." NACFE identified
two publicly available references that provide summaries of mathematical modeling methods: a) *A
Survey of Optimization Models for Long-Haul Freight Transport*, by Crainic (et al), published in 2003
contains an extensive development of theory on modeling [72], and b) *A Framework for Modeling
Freight Railcar Routing Problems with a Time Window*, by Samanta and Mishra, published in 2011 which
contains a succinct literature review on the topic [73].

Crainic (see pages 46-48 of [65]) provides a particularly good description of the challenges in modeling
the freight system that NACFE concludes is relevant to matching particular models of trailers to
particular models of tractors.

Many (fleet management operations) dynamically allocate limited resources to requests and
tasks. For example, empty vehicles, trailers and rail cars are allocated to the appropriate
terminals; motive power tractors and locomotives to services; crews to vehicles or services; loads
to driver-truck combinations; empty containers from depots to customers and returning
containers from customers to depots; and so on. All these (operations) have several common
characteristics:

1. Some future demands are known, but most can only be forecasted, and unpredictable
requests may happen.
2. Many requests materialize in real or quasi-real time and must be acted upon in relatively short
time.
3. Once a resource is allocated to an activity, it is no longer available for a certain duration (the
length of which may be subject to variations as well).
4. Once a resource becomes available again, it is often in a different location than it initially was.
5. The value of an additional unit of a given resource at a location greatly depends on the total
quantity of resources available (which are determined from previous decisions at potentially all
terminals in previous periods) and the current demand.

NACFE concludes that the nature of intentionally pairing specific models of trailers with specific models

know the position of a particle, one can only predict its location with probabilities. An article in *Forbes*
on explaining quantum physics summarizes this as "All we can predict is probability [68]."

To ensure the best tractor is always mated to an appropriate best trailer model on every leg, the density
of SmartWay trailers at each location at all times that the SmartWay tractor arrives must be greater than
zero. The only absolute surety would be if 100% of the available trailers in the network were SmartWay
trailers. Below that point, there is a graduated level of risk that an optimum pairing would not be
possible.

These problems are statistical, and the probabilities can be estimated, but they would differ for each
fleet and all its operations. The ramifications for a fleet's capital investment in new tractors and new
trailers is that as more optimized units enter the system, the net fuel economy would improve,
eventually reaching the maximum fuel economy when all tractors and trailers are SmartWay types.

The methodologies outlined in Crainic and discussed in the literature search in Samanta and Mishra
provide insights on ways to model intentional pairing of specific model tractors and trailers based on
configuration to achieve the best performance. The methods that place a value on time can highlight
where a driver waits for a trailer to arrive so that the tractor is mated to the correct model trailer. This
dwell time is not moving freight, or moving at all, so has no impact on MPG (assuming no idling) but
does factor into net miles a driver could achieve under hours of service rules per day or week, so
impacts paid annual vehicle miles traveled.

A requirement that a SmartWay tractor wait for an appropriate SmartWay trailer at a location would
have a net cost to a fleet because they would move less paid miles annually. The trade-off in improved
vehicle fuel efficiency when paired would be against differences in net annual revenue miles driven.
Rules could be developed that SmartWay tractors and SmartWay trailers are only used on specific lanes,
ensuring that the density at each end of the lanes is non-zero, but the other key factor is that there must
be a load needing to be transported at those times, again, a probabilistic uncertainty.

This example is simplistic to make the point that real world prediction of trailer asset locations
accounting for actual operations, market conditions, etc. is complex, with the only certainty occurring
when an entire network is using specific models.

## PREDICTING E-COMMERCE

E-commerce is defined by the ability to order and receive products through the internet. The bulk of the
transactions are digital, but the physical products still require handling from the manufacturer all the
way to the consumer, and in the case of returns, possibly all the way back again to the manufacturer.
E-commerce tends to be characterized as a quick process for purchasing, but in comparison to buying a
product at a brick-and-mortar store where the consumer experience is instantaneous, e-commerce
requires a delivery either to a home, business or even again a store for pick-up. Reducing that time lag is

## 8.7 ZERO EMISSION MANDATES

The U.S. government has been phasing in stricter emission requirements on diesel trucks since the mid-
1990s [13]. The most recent EPA/NHTSA Green House Gas Phase 2 rules impose requirements through 
Model Year 2027. There is no national direction toward zero emission now. However, state and local 
governments may be imposing stricter requirements that may directly impact Regional operations. 
OEMs have challenging decisions on whether to make 50-state-compliant vehicles able to operate 
everywhere, or to make subset models based on Regional requirements.

Regulatory mandates to adopt zero emission vehicles in specific regions force manufacturers to produce 
viable alternative technology platforms. The U.S. and Canadian rules generally apply directly to the 
manufacturers and their annual product mix. North American vehicle buyers are largely free to make 
their own free market decisions on which vehicles to purchase, but they are influenced by a significant 
number of grants, incentives, tax breaks, and other cost reduction assistances in purchasing zero 
emission technologies. In Europe and other regions, punitive measures are also taken to encourage end 
users to adopt low, ultra-low and zero emission technologies through fees and fines when operating in 
specific urban emission zones.

optimized for residual value, configured option content for the best price in the secondary market. Still 
others have purchased vehicles on the secondary market to use in Regional haul applications. A 
recurring theme in talking with fleets is a concern about responding to market shifts. One approach has 
been to spec vehicles for OTR applications and accept that they may not be optimized for specific 
Regional duty cycles. This keeps these tractors from “being parked on the fence” if the market shifts to 
longer trips. Some of the fleets run both OTR and Regional operations and try to keep the same 
equipment to allow vehicles to slip into either role as needs demand. Maintenance personnel support 
having fewer variations to maintain.

## 9 CONCLUSIONS

NACFE confirms that there is a growth in Regional haul operations versus over the road (OTR) reflected 
by a consistent decreasing trend in annual miles per tractor, and by changes in the driver pools for each. 
This trend is driven by a combination of several factors:

* Driver hiring and retention
* Growth in e-commerce
* The push towards immediate delivery
* Growth in GPS based asset tracking systems (tractor, trailer, load and driver)
* Advances in technologies such as electric and hybrid vehicles
* Vehicle automation
* Innovations in load matching systems
* Increased use of long combination vehicles
* Data collection and mining
* Vehicle specialization

The data rich Internet of Things world is seeing real time asset tracking at the tractor and at the trailer 
level with the incorporation of GPS location systems. Drivers and loads are similarly being tracked, 
through a variety of methods. The adoption of Electronic Logging Devices (ELDs) is providing 
opportunities for optimizing not just the tractor and trailer, but the driver with respect to each load. 
Regional operations will benefit greatly from Big Data advances in data mining, where fact-based
decision making, and effective statistical modeling can supplement and eventually replace past 
experiential based systems.

Growth in Regional operation, with its clear fleet focus on providing quality of life improvements for 
drivers should help with attracting new and experienced drivers and help lead to better retention. There 
should be parallel improvements in attracting and keeping qualified technicians.

Looming forces such as the push towards automation and growth in alternative power sources such as 
battery electric, hybrid electric, and a variety of alternative fuel systems promise to make inroads in the 
Regional market’s more predictable routes where duty cycles may be better aligned with near-term 
capabilities of the technology and supporting infrastructure.

The continued growth of e-commerce is expected to sustain further build out of local warehousing and 
rapid delivery to customers. This trend requires greater distribution of inventory to a wider set of 
locations, which bodes well for boosting demand for Regional haul.

The industry has been focused on optimizing equipment, first the tractor, then the trailer, and then the 
tractor-trailer as a pair. Efficiency gains in hardware have not been matched by efficiency gains in driver 
utilization, where loaded miles per trip, per year and per driver have decreased over time. E-commerce 
is also pressuring greater use of bulky and less dense packaging of trailers as individual home and 
business deliveries increase. These two forces are reducing the amount of freight carried per driver per 
trip. Delays caused by traffic and by detention are similarly impacting the amount of loaded miles 
possible per driver per day. NACFE concludes that the future of Regional haul requires optimizing 
around the driver, and the opportunities abound for improvements.
