So this allows you to just shut off some number of cylinders, and then the remaining number of cylinders—so you're running at 30% load or something—the remaining number of cylinders are each going to have to work harder, which means they're going to run a little hotter. And then the overall amount of air flowing through the engine gets cut by whatever that fraction is that you've shut off. So it's lower flow and hotter flow, so it does keep the masses of all the aftertreatment a little warmer. <!-- timestamp: 12:23 -->
**Steve Ernest:** Well, let's—I mean, first of all, I'd love to geek out with you all day long about it, but um, so we're kind of in that exploratory phase where we're looking at that. And initially we just thought, oh, we'll just make it real simple and cheap and we'll only put it on three cylinders and we'll just shut them off with one solenoid. Boom! That's probably the cheapest and most balanced solution you can have. But then those three cylinders are going to act a certain way, and you know, they may wear differently than the other three cylinders that are firing all the time. One of the things we've learned since running all the time is, well, you know, you got to keep those cylinders a little warm for good combustion. So you really want to just kind of shut them off for $X$ number of cycles—it kind of depends on ambient temperature and all kinds of other stuff—and then you turn them back on once in a while. One of the things that you have to think about is you want to effectively turn each cylinder into a little spring. So you capture air in there, you basically shut off the valves while there's a little exhaust air in there so that it acts as a spring. That way the piston can come up and push against something and then get pushed down. If there's not some pressure in there—like after a little while the air will kind of blow past the rings a little bit; if there's not pressure in there, then you end up as the piston goes down, you're going to pull a bit of a vacuum, and that's going to suck some oil up through the rings going the other direction, and then as soon as you start firing that cylinder, it's going to have a bunch of oil mist and stuff in there. So you really don't want to do that. So as we kind of apply this to different engines, we found that there isn't one rule. It's like, it's not, oh, 18 cycles, that's the magic number. It depends on that particular ring pack, what kind of compression ratio they have, and 20 other factors. So each one of them has to be kind of calibrated with a cylinder pressure sensor to see where that optimal point is. So that's kind of some of the fun of exploring through this stuff, is we're literally—I guess for at least me, I'm learning about base things on the engine that I'm sure the analysis guys know for sure. But for me, it's still kind of a little bit of discovery going through different modes. And like I say, once we throw this stuff in there, other really smart people will kind of find all kinds of new ways to apply it and different parameters.
