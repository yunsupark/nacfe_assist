### 5.6.4 Long Haul

Long haul, irregular routes are the way semi-truck operations are traditionally viewed, however, they 
represent a small segment of the more than 10 million trucks in operation in North America. As 
discussed in Section 5.3, BEVs are not currently a good technology match for these long-haul routes. 
Vehicles equipped for sustainable alternative fuels and hydrogen FCEV are better candidates for these 
duty cycles as discussed in NACFE’s reports *The Messy Middle: A Time for Action* and *Natural Gas’ Role in 
Decarbonizing Trucking* [23][49]. This may change as public charging expands.

## 5.7 DEPOT CHARGING

Estimating energy use at depots requires attention to accurately modeling current fossil-fueled vehicle 
use, even to the point of installing data loggers on vehicles to capture 1 Hz sampling of vehicle 
parameters and geospatial data over a representative time period. The risk in not getting this level of 
detail is grossly overestimating or underestimating electricity needs, charger sizing, and costs. The risks 
from this are identified in more detail in Section 8. Once the baseline operations are fully understood,

# 6 TELEMATICS AND GEOSPATIAL DATA

There are a variety of mechanisms for obtaining vehicle telematics data for commercial fleets. Geotab is 
one of the larger providers of hardware and software for tracking the physical movement of vehicles 
and monitoring key vehicle operating parameters. NACFE has partnered with Geotab on Run on Less 
demonstrations in the past and the company was selected as the primary telematics provider for Run on 
Less – Electric DEPOT in 2023. 

Geotab’s data collection and analysis manages a significant amount of information. In some cases, the 
data is very accurately measured, in others, algorithms are filtering or consolidating information to 
provide a calculation of a value, and those may have assumptions and simplifications built in where loss 
of resolution is felt to be insignificant.

For example, if a vehicle is traveling at relatively steady state speeds on a well mapped road, can the 
average speed be reasonably, accurately, inferred from knowing the time traveled between two known 
points separated by a known distance? If the road is straight and flat, is the assessment even more 
accurate? Conceivably, the same average value can be obtained over a stretch of road traveled at a 
constant speed and one where the vehicle speeds up, then slows, then speeds up again as shown in 
Figure 27. The two drives shown produce the same average speed over 10 miles, but the vehicle’s actual 
travel speeds are substantially different. Speed also may be measured directly from the vehicle, but 
those speeds also are potentially impacted by unknown variables such as tire wear. The GPS and vehicle 
data also may disagree, so software must decide which is more accurate.

also may differ significantly among EVSE manufacturers. For example, in reporting on Run on Less –

energy.

## 7.2 RIGHT TRUCK FOR THE RIGHT DUTY CYCLE

The depots in the Run were in various stages of transitioning to BEVs from fossil-fueled vehicles. A 
critical step in this process is determining which vehicles and their related duty cycles are the correct 
choices to be replaced by BEVs. Putting the right truck into the right duty cycle is critical, and conversely, 
fleets need to avoid putting the wrong truck into the wrong duty cycle.

For example, in 2023, the available BEVs largely were equipped for single charge ranges of 150 to 
perhaps 250 miles, with some exceptions. These trucks are not being designed for 600-mile range, 
irregular route long haul, so should not be put into those roles as they would fail at them. However, 
fleets which have duty cycles compatible with BEVs, can deploy them in those applications. 

An analysis of the OK Produce’s Fresno, CA depot showed that approximately 40 vehicles could be 
replaced immediately with current BEV Class 8 trucks, another 40 would need to wait for OEM offerings 
to be capable of 400-mile ranges, and another 25 would require batteries with a daily range of 500 
miles. The first 40 trucks averaged less than 100 miles per day. The analysis showed that these trucks 
averaged more than 11 stops per day for deliveries or pickups. Lower daily mileage with a high number 
of stops for deliveries and pickups are ideal duty cycles for BEVs, encountering no range or weight 
issues, and generally seeing significant levels of regenerative braking energy recovery.

## 7.4 AERODYNAMICS MEANS BEV RANGE EXTENSION

A common concern of BEVs is collectively captured by the term “range anxiety.” Range anxiety is a bit 
nebulous, but at its root is the concern that a truck will not complete its requisite route, stranding both 
freight and driver. Range anxiety exists with fossil-fueled trucks as well, which may explain some trucks 
having 200 gallons of fuel tank capacity equivalent to being able to drive 1,400 miles at 7 MPG, before 
refueling.

Fleets that understand their routes and duty cycles know how far their trucks need to go each day. Some 
have well established daily routes, such as supplying grocery stores. Matching the truck to the route is a 
matter of knowing the achievable distance for a battery pack, knowing its state of charge at the start of 
the trip, and knowing what environmental and traffic conditions might be expected as these factors can 
use energy or can add energy, impacting achievable range.

## 7.5 REGENERATIVE BRAKING, ENERGY RECOVERY AND SOC

Diesel trucks do not recover energy when braking. BEVs are driven by electric motors and those motors 
can both accelerate and decelerate the truck. The deceleration is called “regenerative braking” or 
“regen braking.” Regen braking occurs when the driver stops pressing the accelerator. This braking 
gradually slows the vehicle while concurrently producing electricity that goes back into the batteries. If 
more braking is needed, traditional friction braking is also on all BEVs.

The amount of regen braking differs from vehicle model to vehicle model, and from route to route 
depending on a range of factors including traffic density, terrain, winds, temperatures, and driver habits. 
Some BEVs also allow different levels of regen braking to be set by the driver or fleet manager.

In Run on Less – Electric regen braking, in some cases, exceeded 30% energy recovery [6]. For a BEV with 
an estimated range of 250 miles, 30% regen extends that range to 325 miles.

## 7.6 VEHICLE TO GRID

Vehicle to grid (VTG) or vehicle to everything (VTE or VTX) describe that the vehicle batteries may be of 
use as energy sources for purposes other than moving the vehicle. Another term is bidirectional VTG 
charging. A common example is school buses where the duty cycle often has large bus fleets sitting idle 
for days or months such as during summer breaks. 

Vehicles need to be designed to allow them to function as energy providers for other systems. The 
majority of BEVs today do not have this capability. Regulators are investigating integrating vehicle 
batteries into bidirectional charging scenarios. Some electric automotive and pick-up truck makers offer 
VTE options to assist with power electronics in remote locations or as back-ups for utility blackout 
situations.

NACFE’s Run on Less – Electric DEPOT did not have VTG or VTE equipped vehicles. Bidirectional charging 
was not demonstrated. Some of the NACFE workshop attendees did provide analysis of the potential 
benefits of tapping unused SOC from parked vehicles during peak demand periods, and then recharging 
them during off-peak utility periods. The intent would be to establish a cash flow stream based on the 
difference in kWh pricing between peak and trough demand times. One estimate in California is that 
intra-day spreads between peak and trough pricing might be $0.09/kWh, as shown in Figure 39 [131]. 
While the price delta might be small, it is multiplied by large power numbers.

## 7.7 TEMPERATURE FACTORS

The Run occurred in September, normally a moderate temperature month for all participating locations. 
Range is known to be impacted by both cold and hot weather. Temperature can impact battery 
performance, but more critically, cabin HVAC loads increase significantly in temperature extremes. 
These tend to demand more energy from the batteries, potentially reducing ranges by 15% to 30% on 
extremely cold or hot days. The Run did not take place during severe weather, but NACFE has reported 
on the effects of temperature on BEVs in prior reports [120][128]. Geotab has published data on the 
effect of temperature as shown in Figure 40. This data is aggregated “from 5.2 million trips taken by

## 7.8 DRIVING SPEED AND CONSISTENCY FACTORS

published the graph in Figure 41 illustrating that higher speeds use more energy than lower ones

move vehicles at higher speeds. Counteracting that trend, somewhat, is that some vehicles can be

## 7.9 EFFICIENCY AND CONSUMPTION

Traditional internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicle performance testing tends to be reported in terms 
of miles per gallon of fuel (MPG) or variations of kilometers per liter. MPG and its metric alternatives are 
technically described as “efficiency,” or how far can you go on how much energy. 

The mathematical inverse of efficiency is defined as “consumption,” or how much energy to go how far. 
In a battery electric world, this is typically described as kWh/mi, or kWh/km depending on what country 
you are in.

Efficiency and consumption often get confused by people comparing the performance of ICE vehicles 
and BEVs. With efficiency, higher numbers are better whereas with consumption, lower numbers are 
better. To reduce the confusion between efficiency and consumption, BEV performance is sometimes 

July 9, 2024 Page 58

<!-- page: 59 -->

Run on Less – Electric DEPOT: Scaling BEVs in the Real World

described in miles per diesel gallon equivalent (MPGde), or miles per gasoline gallon equivalent (usually 
MPGe).

Major testing standards organizations such as SAE have not yet standardized commercial vehicle BEV 
energy consumption measurement test methodologies, although there are standards for ICE vehicles 
such as SAE J1321 Fuel Consumption Test Procedure - Type II and SAE Fuel Consumption Test Procedure 
(Engineering Method) J1526_202205 [33][34]. The lack of a standardized evaluation method for BEVs 
means that fleets should take care in comparing numbers from different sources as they can vary 
considerably in assumptions and methodologies. 

Illustrating this, several participants in the workshop used NACFE’s data as well as their own data and 
third-party data to estimate consumption rates for all the vehicles in the Run. The diversity of results by 
these different groups highlighted that there are many interpretations currently possible for the same 
vehicle. The conclusion is that BEV consumption values from different analysists can differ significantly. 

This is consistent with prior NACFE findings. Summarizing the earlier 2021 Run on Less – Electric event, 
NACFE wrote in the report *Electric Trucks Have Arrived: Documenting A Real-World Electric Trucking 
Demonstration* about how consumption values can vary depending on which factors are and are not 
included, and whether the energy measurement is taken on the vehicle, at the charger or at the meter 
[6]. Figure 45 illustrates the complexity of possible measurement points as each location in the energy 
supply chain will have different values due to losses. Even inside the vehicle, measurements will vary 
depending on where in the circuit the measurement is taken. Ultimately, energy costs and usage are 
tracked by the utility at the meter, so it makes sense to relate consumption to that point of 
measurement. Unfortunately, not every fleet has meters specifically for chargers. Charger data was not

## 7.10 ADDITIONAL DATA NEEDS

A consistent message from the NACFE workshop attendees was the need for more and better-quality 
performance data on BEV operations — not just measured on the vehicle, but also at the charger, at the 
depot site, from a utility perspective.

Creating a BEV depot is a team effort involving the truck OEM, the charger manufacturer, the utility, the 
site management team, and multiple other groups related to permitting. Each of these participants 
requires its own type of data for different reasons. Team members may not know the needs of other 
people on the team. Often planners end up defaulting to worst-case values in the absence of real data. 
This can lead to significantly over-sizing systems, leading to more challenging site builds and excessive, 
unnecessary cost overruns.

In some cases, key information may change based on the complexity of planning the depot by a team 
representing multiple interests. This can lead to schedule delays to redesign the site to accommodate 
“surprise” changes. In some cases, these changes require resubmitting grant or permit applications as 
specifications have changed sufficiently over the original authorizations to warrant revisiting them.

All workshop participants wanted information on costs — costs of trucks, chargers, energy, installation, 
etc. This level of detail gets into proprietary information, but everyone could benefit from accurate cost 
numbers to use for future project planning.

There is also a severe lack of standard reporting of information. Definitions of metrics are not currently 
controlled by published industry standards. Examples are reporting SOC levels — what temperature and 
altitude are they measured at? What is the rated battery capacity that is actually accessible by the user? 

July 9, 2024 Page 61

<!-- page: 62 -->

Run on Less – Electric DEPOT: Scaling BEVs in the Real World

How much of the battery pack is not accessible by manufacturer’s choices? How do you measure energy 
consumption? Does consumption include regen braking energy or not? How much regen is assumed in 
stating ranges on vehicle specifications? Where on the truck is energy consumption actually measured? 
What losses are and are not included at that point? What energy losses actually occur between the 
charger and the vehicle for each configuration? Many factors are not standardized, nor is the 
methodology for measuring them.

Maintenance failure rates and repair data were not part of the short Run on Less demonstration. 
However, this type of data is absolutely critical to estimating total cost of ownership (TCO) for BEVs 
versus fossil-fueled vehicles. The industry thinking is that BEVs have lower maintenance costs, lower 
failure rates, higher up time, etc., because BEVs have much fewer moving parts, much less vibration, 
much less heat and wear that cause failures on fossil-fueled vehicles. Battery life is a big unknown. While 
warranties may indicate useful lives of 5, 8 or 10 years, the actual life of batteries in each model and for 
each user is needed to avoid downstream issues. The jury is still out on BEV maintenance data because 
there are so few commercial BEVs currently on the road and so little maintenance information is being 
made public.

The absence of data is a vacuum which creative people will fill with estimations. The accuracy of those 
estimations may be spot-on or completely wrong. Full transparency on many levels is needed to 
properly guide the trucking industry. Having actual data for real-world operations is critical at this 
fledgling time in the transition to zero-emission vehicles.

Depots do not pay for electricity based on vehicle kWh usage; they pay based on the total power

levels include all the losses between the meter and the vehicle. There is a danger in estimating power

charger and from the charger to the vehicle.

Utilities can provide more reliable electricity when charging is occurring at predictable times and at 
consistent levels. Depots exist in a community of electricity users that include all other electricity 
consumers. Charging cycles may offset troughs or compound peaks for regional electricity demand.

This “community” nature of electricity supply creates opportunities for groups of depots to consider 
working as a consortium, rather than each of them thinking of themselves as an isolated consumer. This 
“group think” approach could lead to partnerships in which two or more companies combine charging 
use to maximize charger asset utilization. For example, a bakery that makes deliveries at night might 
combine with a fleet that makes daytime package deliveries to ensure chargers are more consistently 
operating. That consistency can help utilities provide lower costs and better grid reliability.

Looking at the power use and time of day also may open up opportunities for fleets to adjust operations 
to achieve lower electricity costs by showing flexibility in when vehicles are charged. 

## 7.13 PORTABLE AND/OR TEMPORARY CHARGING SOLUTIONS

Portable and/or temporary charging are fleet solutions for getting their BEVs on the road while waiting 
for the longer term utility infrastructure installations. Temporary infrastructure can be installed quickly 
and later easily removed or transitioned into long-term EVSE.

July 9, 2024 Page 65

<!-- page: 66 -->

Run on Less – Electric DEPOT: Scaling BEVs in the Real World

The quick installation of EVSE infrastructure allows fleets to get rapid, valuable firsthand experience with 
operating their BEVs in their commercial duty cycles. That experience can help shape longer term EVSE 
planning with utilities and guide capital investment planning.

US Foods installed five temporary chargers supporting 30 Class 8 BEVs in daily operations. While juggling 
serial charging of multiple vehicles required some operational fine tuning, US Foods discovered that they 
could successfully operate at much greater than a one-charger-to-one-vehicle (1:1) ratio, actually having 
five vehicles per charger. US Foods’ La Mirada, CA location now has completed its long-term EVSE 
installations and redeployed their portable chargers to another site. The ramifications of demonstrating 
a 5:1 charger-to-truck charging ratio is that US Foods’ new EVSE is now capable of supporting more BEVs 
than initially planned. The increased charger utilization rates also mean the chargers are idle less often. 
For the utilities, the increased charger utilization means a more uniform power demand curve for the 
facility, a key factor in providing more reliable power.

Temporary charging is extremely flexible, with a variety of ways to provide power, including mobile 
battery packs, skid-mounted chargers that can be connected to spare available building power systems, 
or to generator sets (gensets) fueled by RNG, propane, hydrogen, renewable diesel, or more traditional 
fuels.

Existing warehouses and buildings often have gone through multiple efficiency upgrades over time as 
building codes and regulations have evolved. Net electrical energy demands for older buildings have 
been reduced over time by efficiency gains such as the use of LED lighting in place of fluorescent and 
incandescent ones, or increased efficiency requirements for heating and cooling equipment, more 
efficient motors and transformers, added insulation and sealing systems, etc. These changes may mean 
that building owners have spare electricity capacity versus when the building was originally built.

Operating a portable genset to temporarily power BEVs often results in a net reduction in emissions 
versus operating diesel trucks. 

There is a growing list of portable charging systems including Pioneer eMobility, Nikola, ChargePoint, EV 
Edison, Momentum Groups, Amply and mCharging [26][27][28][29][30][31][32].

# 8 UNREALISTIC “WORST-CASE” SCENARIOS AND FACTORS

Fleets in the Run on Less events have learned from their early implementation of BEVs and EVSE. Actual 
real-world operational experience has increased asset utilization for EVSE infrastructure, vehicles, 
drivers, and utilities, while also providing critical insights on better estimating TCO. 

Early planning for BEVs often incorrectly focuses on unrealistic “worst case” scenarios. These scenarios 
can significantly undervalue BEVs. Here are some examples of underestimating capabilities or benefits.
* Overestimating kWh/mi consumption for vehicles based on out-of-date data
* Ignoring energy recovery from regenerative braking
* Ignoring reduced out-of-route miles and driver time made possible from depot charging
* Requiring one charger for every vehicle
* Assuming the highest charger rating for every charging event
* Ignoring the benefits derived from managed charging
* Assuming vehicles require 100% charging at each charging event
* Assuming vehicles require daily charging
* Assuming loads are reaching maximum gross vehicle weight (GVW) on every vehicle for every 
trip
* Assuming vehicles are driven the maximum miles every trip
* Assuming only one charge per day is possible
* Assuming en route charging is not feasible at delivery points

There are a number of BEV factors often mistakenly excluded that also can negatively impact BEV 
planning, overestimating capabilities or underestimating costs. These include:
* Not considering cold and hot weather effects in battery sizing and charging
* Not considering the impact of road grades
* Not considering battery degradation possible over years of ownership
* Overestimating maintenance cost savings from early generation BEVs
* Ignoring charger losses (efficiency) in estimating power needs
* Ignoring increased tire wear from heavier, more agile BEVs
* Assuming the driver has no impact on performance
* Assuming tractor and trailer aerodynamics don’t matter

Fleets find they learn a great deal from early deployments that help significantly fine-tune later decision making. NACFE recommends that fleets deploy these vehicles in their own real-world operations. 
Decisions on BEVs generally are made in comparison to existing ICE vehicles. Unfortunately, many fleets 
have not accurately assessed their real-world operations. NACFE recommends that fleets quantify their 
existing vehicle use by installing data loggers and accurately tracking their current vehicles over a 
representative time period to validate performance assumptions. 

Opportunities for improving BEV deployment planning and operations to avoid overestimating or 
underestimating them are provided below.

advised not to charge daily above 80% or 90% charge levels as a way to extend battery life. The net daily 
charge required was then just 30% to 40% of the battery’s stated capacity. This means that “worst-case” 
power demand estimates grossly overestimate actual daily power needs, and EVSE sizing could be 
significantly reduced to less demanding/less expensive EVSE equipment.

One Run on Less fleet reported that its BEV tire life was roughly one-third that of its ICE vehicles after a 
year of BEV operation. This was considered early experience as the feet had not done anything to 
optimize vehicle parameters for tire life, nor discussed in detail with OEMs alternative tire choices. The 
fleet expected its tire replacement cycle to improve with experience, but noted that BEVs generally are 
expected to need shorter tire replacement cycles.

Vehicle freight weight is another topic where worst-case estimation can significantly impact planning for 
electricity loads and sizing EVSE. NACFE reported that Class 8 trucks frequently operate below 70,000 
lbs. gross vehicle weight as shown in Figure 55 [10]. The statistical distribution resembles the Golden 
Gate Bridge in that it has two peaks like the two orange towers of the bridge, and the graph resembles 
the shape of the suspension cables of the bridge. The two vehicle weight peaks are near 30,000 lbs. and 
70,000 lbs. The 30,000 lbs. peak corresponds to vehicles driving unloaded. The peak at 70,000 lbs. 
represents statistically typical full loads. 

Some fleets have very predictable load weights and may never exceed 65,000 lbs. while others may see 
a variety of weights up to the maximum allowable. Still others operate well beyond normal ranges in 
specific regions where GVWs can be as high as 140,000 lbs. Accurate vehicle weight data is now being 
collected by weigh-in-motion (WIM) systems in many locations. The more exact the understanding of 
freight weights, the better the electricity needs for BEVs can be estimated.

## 10 FINDINGS

The primary Run on Less – Electric DEPOT findings released shortly after the event in 2023 are restated here and are still valid after in-depth analysis of the data. See Figure 101.

* Small depots are ready for electrification now.
* Electrification at large energy depots is gaining momentum.
* There have been big technology and production improvements since Run on Less – Electric in 2021.
* The trucking industry needs cost and weight reductions to improve TCO.
* Range can be extended with multiple charging events during a shift or en route.
* BEVs are empowering diversity and inclusion, and energizing initiative and passion.
* Powering up infrastructure is taking too long, but portable/temporary charging is helping.

Since the Run, the US government released a comprehensive plan called the *National Zero-Emission Freight Corridor Strategy*. Traditional and new OEMs have begun ramping up production of BEVs. Fleets all over North America have initiated deployment of these new BEVs at scale. Accelerating this progress is significant construction of multiple electric depots.

Fleet and facility operators are making this transition to new zero-emission technologies possible for both sound business and altruistic reasons. Companies are making commitments to reduce their environmental impact recognizing that daily operations of fossil-fueled vehicles have real-world ramifications to business growth potential, community economics and society wellness.

As of mid-2024, sites capable of supporting tens to hundreds of BEVs have come into existence. NACFE concludes this is not just a “California thing.” States such as Texas, South Carolina, Nevada, Arizona, Washington, Oregon, New York and Canadian provinces such as British Columbia and Ontario, for example, are operating electric depots with growing numbers of BEVs.

The number of OEMs building trucks for the North American market has more than tripled in the last decade, and nearly all of them have zero-emission products.

Perhaps the most significant Run on Less – Electric DEPOT finding is a universal need for more and better information, more and better data on a variety of factors surrounding performance, price, residual value, infrastructure and more.

NACFE determined that misinformation and the misapplication of information can greatly impact decision-making. Assuming “worst case” values, for example, can significantly skew science-based decision-making by underestimating or overestimating benefits and challenges.

For example, assuming vehicles return to base at near zero SOC at the end of each shift when in reality, BEVs often are returning to base with more than 50% SOC. Additionally, with advanced route planning a

truck may not need to be charged back to 100% to complete the next route. This directly impacts the expected overnight charging amount, cutting it in half. Cutting the amount of charge in half may allow purchasing less costly charging systems. Those less costly, less capable charging systems mean the utilities do not need to plan for as significant a load on the grid.

Early planning for BEVs often incorrectly focuses on these unrealistic “worst case” scenarios. These scenarios can significantly undervalue BEVs. They make assumptions about loads, amount of charging needed, number of chargers needed, impact of regenerative braking and more.

Overestimating kWh/mi consumption for vehicles based on out-of-date data can significantly inflate charging system sizing and grid load requirements.

There are also a number of BEV factors that often are mistakenly excluded that also can negatively impact BEV planning, overestimating capabilities or underestimating costs. They fail to consider several factors including the impact of weather, battery degradation, increased tire wear and tear and more.

NACFE found in 2021’s Run on Less – Electric and reinforced in 2023’s Run on Less – Electric DEPOT that many Class 2b through Class 8 duty cycles could be completed using BEVs built with current technology. NACFE also stated in 2021 and reiterated in 2023 that electric vehicles are only part of the future of freight.

A portfolio of technologies will be employed on the path forward based on many regional factors and required duty cycles. NACFE has termed the next period of freight as “The Messy Middle” reflecting an abundance of technology choices in a rapidly evolving freight market.

BEV vehicles are here, they are real, they are beginning to scale. As with all new vehicles, they need to improve in capability, be less costly, be more reliable and serviceable, and the infrastructure needs to grow in lockstep with the vehicles. But they are well on the path to greater adoption to help fill out freight’s portfolio of lower-emitting freight transport solutions.

In addition to validating the initial findings, the post-Run analysis came to these additional findings. See Figure 102.

1. **Electric vans, trucks and heavy-duty tractors are on the road today and are performing well in many duty cycles.** Despite challenges, many fleets are deploying BEVs at scale in their operations. This list includes not only the 10 fleets that participated in Run on Less – Electric DEPOT but also companies like Amazon, NFI and IKEA.
2. **Infrastructure, both at the depots and strategically placed along freight corridors is needed now.** One stumbling block to depot electrification is the need for a wider charging network. The place to begin is at or between depots and along freight corridors. Fleets can help with this by working in partnerships where two or more companies combine charging use to maximize charger asset utilization. Fleets need to be aware that planning time for utilities is significantly longer than what fleets are used to. Utilities need to give fleets realistic timelines for project completion. The *U.S. National Blueprint for Transportation Decarbonization* and the *National Zero-Emission Freight Corridor Strategy* outline a vision for the vehicle technologies and infrastructure required to transform commercial freight transportation from fossil fuels to zero-emission energy sources.

3. **Heavy-duty tractor OEMs should make cost and weight improvements a priority.** It is still difficult for many fleets to make the TCO case for BEVs based solely on hard costs, but factoring in things like driver satisfaction and achieving sustainability goals can result in a better TCO case. In addition, more information is needed on the cost of trucks, chargers, energy, infrastructure installation, etc. Vehicle weight has a direct impact on how much payload can be carried. Even with the 2,000-lb. weight exemption for Class 8 vehicles, the weight of the batteries is impacting payload as well as range. But it is important to keep in mind that today, even with diesel-powered trucks, not every load reaches maximum gross vehicle weight. The more exact the understanding of freight weights, the better the electricity needs for BEVs can be estimated. This will help fleets better match BEVs to duty cycles. In the meantime, OEMs need to continue to refine batteries with weight reduction as a key goal.
4. **More realistic data on all key performance metrics is needed.** There is a need for better quality performance data on BEV operations — not measured solely on the vehicle, but also measured at the charger, at the depot and from a utility perspective. Information needs to be realistic and not focus on “worst case” scenarios as those do not accurately represent the reality of the current state of BEV development. In addition, it takes a joint effort from a variety of sources to electrify a depot and each of these participants requires their own type of data for different reasons.
