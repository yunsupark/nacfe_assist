# 01
CONFIDENCE REPORT: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY – NATURAL GAS’ ROLE IN DECARBONIZING TRUCKING

Natural gas (NG) is a fuel that has the potential to help reduce total greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, but also has significant advantages in reducing the more immediate and local health effects caused by air pollutants like nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter (PM). PM filters are very efficient and the actual difference in PM output between diesel and NG is very small. The big difference between natural gas and diesel is NOx. Replacing diesels with spark-ignited natural gas engines could improve a fleet’s ability to meet upcoming more stringent emission standards.

The net CO2 benefit of a natural gas engine is in the range of 13% to 18% compared to diesel fuel. Natural gas produces approximately 27% less CO2 per unit of energy on a fuel-comparison basis compared with No. 2 diesel fuel. However, the natural gas engine is less efficient than a diesel and natural gas needs to be compressed for storage on the vehicle. Methane leakage that occurs primarily in the upstream process in the natural gas supply system (pipelines and local distribution systems) also must be considered. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimates the leakage to be 1.4%, and there are some higher estimates. 

According to the American Petroleum Institute (API), natural gas is a form of petroleum most often found accompanying crude oil in oil wells. Most of the natural gas in the US is considered a fossil fuel because it is made from underground sources formed over millions of years by the action of heat and pressure on organic materials. Alternatively, renewable natural gas (RNG), also known as biomethane, is a pipeline-quality vehicle fuel produced by purifying biogas. RNG offers a significant environmental benefit: it has negative carbon intensity when derived from animal waste such as dairy. Currently most of the natural gas used in transportation, around 70%, is RNG.

CONFIDENCE REPORT: EXECUTIVE SUMMARY – NATURAL GAS’ ROLE IN DECARBONIZING TRUCKING

While natural gas has been used in combustion engines for decades, currently there is a renewed interest in using it in transportation because it is a lower-carbon fuel than diesel and is cleaner-burning. In the past few years, the capacity to produce RNG has grown significantly and RNG represents 69% of the natural gas used in transportation in 2022. 

Fleets’ experience with natural gas falls into the following four categories.

1. No desire to experiment or invest.
2. Made investment with no success.
3. Invested and had some success.
4. Invested heavily with success and will continue to do so.

In the first type are carriers that don’t have the time or resources necessary to jump into something they know or that they believe is unproven. The second group of carriers is mostly made up of those that tested natural gas tractors when they gained popularity in the early- to mid-2010s. Unfortunately, that generation of natural gas tractors was plagued with a plethora of failures that are common with new technologies. The third bucket is carriers — like Paper Transport — that found a way to operate natural gas equipment, albeit at an operational cost increase over diesel, but it expanded their total portfolio offerings, decreasing dependency on diesel. The last group of fleets have gone “big” on CNG and are making it affordable in their operations. This is a short list, but it includes UPS, WM (Waste Management) and Frito-Lay.

When evaluating the natural gas option, there are many financial considerations. More specifically, fleets should consider and compare natural gas to diesel and other alternatives, including battery electric, from a variety of standpoints. It can take five to 10 years for fleets to see payback from their investment in natural gas vehicles and infrastructure. During that time there are likely to be significant improvements in battery electric vehicles which will offer many benefits to fleets. Having invested in natural gas, they may not be able to pivot to take advantage of the benefits of battery electric vehicles. 

As with any technology there are both benefits and challenges for fleets when it comes to natural gas as a power source for their trucks. Perhaps the biggest benefit is that natural gas vehicles can be a near-zero solution. Plus, there is already some fueling infrastructure in place. On the challenge side is the fact that they are lower in efficiency in miles per diesel equivalent and there may not be incentives to purchase them. ## Concerns About Natural Gas

Because natural gas is lighter than air, there are some concerns about maintenance. Maintenance facilities historically have been designed to handle vehicles using fuels (gasoline/diesel) that are heavier than air. Because natural gas is lighter than air, it rises to the ceiling which means that it might be necessary to modify some areas of the shop. There are some issues that need to be addressed as shops convert to natural gas capabilities, and shops need to review their specific building safety codes surrounding natural gas.

Tanks for natural gas are significantly more costly and complex than that of a diesel fuel tank. Due to lower volumetric energy density of natural gas, they are larger and consume more space on the vehicle.

There is some concern about the availability of natural gas fueling stations. As of 2022, there were more than 1,400 (772 of which were public) CNG fueling stations in the US. There are approximately 97 (51 public) LNG fueling stations, mostly in areas that service the long-haul trucking industry.

## Renewable Natural Gas

RNG is a form of natural gas that can be used as a fuel to power vehicles. It is fully interchangeable with conventional natural gas and has significant environmental benefits. RNG is generated from various feedstock sources such as livestock (e.g., animal manure), landfills, wastewater, and other sources. Negative carbon intensity (CI) can be achieved through animal waste. See Figure ES2. Feedstock through landfills and wastewater do not produce a negative CI but still offer an advantage over diesel and conventional natural gas. 

Note that this opportunity will not go away in the near future. There always will be dairies, chicken farms, cattle, garbage dumps, rotting, grasses, swamps, etc. All of this will decompose and emit methane into the atmosphere, so this opportunity needs to be seized and RNG is an excellent way to take advantage of it.

## Emissions Mandates and Natural Gas

There is a tremendous amount of governmental regulation created over the last several years with the intention of providing cleaner air and decarbonizing the transportation industry. Natural gas engines can easily meet the Environmental Protection Agency’s 2027 criteria pollutant regulations. Natural gas engines can be used as a tool to help truck manufacturers meet the GHG Phase 3 regulations. Natural gas engines will not comply with the zero-emission vehicle mandates. However, if a natural gas engine is combined with a hybrid, it can qualify as a near-zero emission vehicle.

## Key Findings

After researching natural gas engines for use in commercial vehicles and speaking with fleets and other experts, the study team developed the following key findings.

1. **There appears to be a wide range in perception and results regarding the business case for natural gas.** Some fleets have been able to save money with natural gas fueled vehicles while others select them only for sustainability reasons. Some key factors to make a business case positive for natural gas are: Full utilization of fueling stations, utilizing both medium- and heavy-duty trucks, and taking advantage of all incentives. 
2. **There are several positive environmental aspects of natural gas engines.** These include its ultra-low NOx capability, the fact that there is no need for a diesel particulate filter, and the well-to-wheels CO2 reduction. Additionally, compared to conventional natural gas, RNG is the more attractive solution from an environmental standpoint, which can help fleets, shippers and customers meet their corporate ESG goals.
3. **There are environmental concerns with natural gas.** These include methane leaking from aging pipelines and natural gas infrastructure and outdated equipment.
4. **Sustainability goals, regulations, and the California conundrum.** The CO2, NOx, and PM benefits of natural gas-powered vehicles allow fleets and shippers to improve the carbon footprint and reduce their Scope 2 and 3 emissions. Natural gas also helps OEMs and engine manufacturers meet GHG regulation and NOx and PM standards. However, in California and other states, ZEV mandates are being established, and natural gas does not qualify. 
5. **The new 15-liter Cummins X15N engine seems promising.** In the past, natural gas engines experienced reliability and performance issues, but many of these are believed to be fixed.
6. **Aftertreatment is simple and more reliable.** Natural gas engines do not require a DPF or SCR system, eliminating a major cause of downtime, warranty, and repairs.
7. **Natural gas is very abundant in the US.** The question is, how accessible is it for a given fleet’s routes?
8. **There is a question as to whether there will be an ample supply of RNG.** Natural Gas Vehicles for America (NVGA) has projected significant increases in RNG over the next several years. Given the introduction of the Cummins X15N (and Cummins’ projections), there could be a couple hundred thousand natural gas engines in heavy-duty applications later in the decade.
9. **Because of economics and the environment, there are a few points to consider when comparing BEV vs CNG.** Converting to natural gas is a long-term decision. Capital for fueling stations or charging stations, etc. is a 5 to 10+ year investment. From a CO2 standpoint it is essential on a local level to consider the grid’s dependence on fossil fuel vs. renewable/nuclear fuel. Local consideration of grid and CNG capacity also is a factor. CNG requires installing venting equipment, methane detection, and alarms in maintenance shops, which can be an expensive infrastructure investment.

# 1 INTRODUCTION

The purpose of this report is to explore the viability of using natural gas as a transportation fuel in terms of potential fuel and/or maintenance cost savings and to help truck fleets participate in the green energy use revolution. As the world focuses on decarbonization and reducing emissions, fleets are challenged with understanding new technologies and making business, financial, and sustainability decisions. This report shares key factors that a fleet needs to consider when exploring the use of natural gas as a fuel source for their commercial vehicles.

Natural gas is a fuel that has the potential to help reduce total greenhouse gas (GHG), emissions, but also has significant advantages in reducing the more immediate and local health effects caused by air pollutants like nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter (PM). However, particulate matter reduction may not be significant because of the effectiveness of well-maintained diesel particulate filters (DPFs) on today’s diesels. Replacing diesels with spark-ignited natural gas engines could improve a fleet’s ability to meet upcoming more stringent emission standards. There would then be less concern about increased maintenance costs because of the simplicity of the three-way catalyst, which produces ultra-low NOx emissions without selective catalytic reduction (SCR). 

# 2 SCOPE

This report looks at natural gas, one of the many alternative fuel options, and explores the benefits and challenges of using natural gas as an over the road (OTR) transportation fuel. The goal of this report is to provide information that is comprehensive and objective, avoiding biases that could lead to bad decision making. While no report can guarantee a decision to adopt an alternate fuel will be successful, knowing all the facts and understanding all the variable will help fleet executives avoid making hasty or ill-founded decisions. 

Along with research into the scientific and engineering facts, the study team conducted interviews with many transportation industry participants to provide real-world feedback. Through these interviews, we explored the successes and failures of natural gas with equipment manufacturers, truck OEMs, fuel providers, fleets, shippers, regulators, and others.

Today there is a plethora of fuel choices in the OTR transportation sector. Will we need a combination of fuel sources to solve our needs and, if so, which fuels will be beneficial to fleets? Of course, at this time no one knows for certain. What we do know is that a great deal of effort, investment, commitment, and time will be needed for a successful transition to an environmentally friendly energy source.

# 4 THE ENVIRONMENTAL BENEFIT OF NATURAL GAS VEHICLES 

A primary goal of switching to natural gas is the potential for reducing CO2 emissions — the most plentiful greenhouse gas. Running natural gas instead of diesel fuel in your trucks may also help retain drivers because these engines are quieter. Drivers also frequently complain about the smells associated with diesel fuel that often gets on clothing, gloves, skin, etc. Natural gas fueling doesn’t have the same issues associated with it. Natural gas engines also may reduce some maintenance costs because of the absence of emissions reduction systems found on diesel trucks, while also reducing emissions of primary pollutants like NOx. NOx control on natural gas engines in accomplished using a much simpler and well-proven aftertreatment system.

The net CO2 benefit of a natural gas engine is in the range of 13% to 18% compared to diesel fuel. Natural gas produces approximately 27% less CO2 per unit of energy on a fuel-comparison basis compared with No. 2 diesel fuel. However, the natural gas engine is less efficient than a diesel and natural gas needs to be compressed for storage on the vehicle. Methane leakage that occurs primarily in the upstream process in the natural gas supply system (pipelines and local distribution systems) must also be considered. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) estimates the leakage to be 1.4%, and there are some higher estimates [105]. Efforts are underway to limit leakage and ensure equipment does not leak. Companies can by certified by a non-profit organization called MiQ which works toward limiting methane leakage [106].

Taking all the above factors into consideration (efficiency, estimated upstream methane leakage, gas compression), there is still a CO2 savings that is on the order of 13% to 18% using conventional natural gas versus running standard diesel trucks [23]. 

While the overall CO2 savings is better with natural gas, it is worth noting that from a diesel gallon equivalent (DGE) standpoint, more fuel is consumed with natural gas vs diesel. This is because spark ignited engines are less efficient. Hence more fuel will be burned on a DGE basis. 

In 2019, NACFE conducted Run on Less Regional. Nine of the 10 tractors in this event were diesel, but one unit was powered by compressed natural gas (CNG). One objective of the event was to demonstrate to the industry the art of the possible in terms of MPG for a typical regional haul duty cycle. The average

# 6 OVERVIEW OF NATURAL GAS

Today, natural gas is seen by many as a widely available fuel that can power commercial trucks while significantly reducing the carbon intensive climate warming effects that would have been produced by diesels. This section will explain why the fuel is already widely available with the potential for increased supply in response to demand as a fuel for trucks.

## 6.1 WHERE DOES NATURAL GAS COME FROM?

According to the American Petroleum Institute’s website, natural gas is a form of petroleum most often found accompanying crude oil in oil wells. The vast majority of natural gas in the US is considered a fossil fuel because it is made from underground sources formed over millions of years by the action of heat and pressure on organic materials. Alternatively, RNG, also known as biomethane, is a pipeline-quality vehicle fuel produced by purifying biogas [50].

## 6.2 WHY IS NATURAL GAS IMPORTANT?

While natural gas has been used in combustion engines for decades, there is a renewed interest in using it in transportation because it is a lower-carbon fuel than diesel and cleaner-burning. This quality extends to its ability to be used in spark-ignited, natural gas engines. These truck engines are similar to present day passenger car gasoline engines, meaning they can use a simple, three-way catalyst as aftertreatment, eliminating the diesel particulate filter (DPF) and SCR systems.

Calculating the net reduction in CO2 emissions when natural gas is used in large trucks is complex, however there is likely a measurable reduction. In addition, natural gas recovered from various agricultural sources and landfills, known as RNG, when used in trucks, can reduce the total warming effect of GHG emissions. Using RNG as a fuel converts methane (initially 80 times as potent a GHG as CO2) to CO2 and water vapor. This results in a lower CI, which even can be negative in some cases. This is discussed in more detail in Section 9.3.

While the processing is different, conventional natural gas and RNG are virtually identical in the way they behave in the engine. However, RNG is much kinder to the environment than conventional natural gas. This is because the source of RNG is actually an extremely potent warming agent that is normally simply released to the atmosphere and converted to less harmful greenhouse gases in the engine; and because its carbon is not derived from mineral sources.

## 6.3 WHY NATURAL GAS NOW?

The trucking industry is poised to begin using larger amounts of natural gas as a fuel, but will the fuel be available in the quantities needed? The answer appears to be yes because of the use of hydraulic fracturing in recovering natural gas.

Hydraulic fracturing of sedimentary rock containing natural gas, called “fracking,” has significantly increased the supply of natural gas in recent years. The demand for fracked gas is largely the result of the fact that natural gas has made a large contribution to the reduction of CO2 in power generation.

# 7 FLEET EXPERIENCE WITH NATURAL GAS

It is no secret that the majority of carriers in North America have little to no experience with natural gas. However, there are several carriers — Frito-Lay, UPS, Amazon and Waste Management — that have extensive experience with natural gas. While some experiences have been less than fruitful, some carriers have figured out how to thrive using RNG in their fleets. This is discussed in Section 7.3.

## 7.1 RISE AND FALL OF NATURAL GAS IN HEAVY DUTY TRUCKS

Natural gas is not new to the transportation sector. In fact, natural gas has been used to power vehicles dating back to the early 20th century when many people were experimenting with fuels to power the new internal combustible engine automobile. The first widespread use of natural gas as a vehicle fuel came during World War I when many parts of the world experienced gasoline shortages. 

However, natural gas did not take off as a viable transportation fuel source until the 1930s. Because of the gasoline shortages, early European natural gas vehicles (built mostly during WWI and WWII) ran on uncompressed gas and were known as “gas-bag” vehicles. The Dutch old-timer pictured in Figure 3 carried a gas storage bag of 13 cubic meters, an installation that gave it a range of about 50 km (30 miles) at an energy consumption of 13 liters per km (22 mpg). As you can see, natural gas technology has come a long way since its early adoption [28].

## 7.2 NATURAL GAS SUCCESS

Over the last 50 years natural gas vehicle technology has made tremendous advances, especially in fuel storage, where once the gas was stored at atmospheric pressure. Today the fuel choices for natural gas vehicles include CNG and liquified natural gas (LNG). Natural gas currently powers more than 175,000 vehicles in the US and roughly 23 million worldwide [29]. These US vehicle sales, however, are mostly for passenger cars. Natural gas-powered Class 8 trucks have been very limited because of the problems described in Section 7.3. They are primarily used in day cab and refuse applications. Information provided by ACT Research indicates that sales have been less than 5000 units per year in the 2013 to 2019 timeframe [116].

Today’s natural gas is domestically available, easy to transport, and reduces GHG emissions when compared to gasoline or diesel vehicles. So why haven’t North American fleets adopted natural gas on a larger scale? The answer is a history of early failures in natural gas fleets.

## 7.4 DRIVER EXPERIENCE

One of the most important parts of any successful fleet is the person behind the wheel and satisfying them is of great importance. If customers’ needs aren’t taken care of because drivers have low morale or even quit, the fleet will lose customers because of poor service. And, if fleets don’t take care of drivers, they too will ultimately struggle.

That is why when fleets test an alternative fuel, they must consider how it will affect their drivers. This absolutely applies when converting to natural gas tractors. ## 7.5 SHOP AND FACILITIES

Because natural gas is lighter than air, there are some concerns about maintenance. Maintenance facilities historically have been designed to handle vehicles using fuels (gasoline/diesel) that are heavier than air. If there is a diesel or gasoline spill, it puddles on the ground. Because natural gas is lighter than air, it rises to the ceiling which means that it might be necessary to modify some areas of the shop. 

There are some issues that need to be addressed as shops convert to natural gas capabilities. However, shops need to review their specific building safety codes surrounding natural gas.

* **Methane Gas Detection System:** These systems are designed to alert personnel when methane is detected. They must be able to issue audible and visual warnings. They also must be able to power off electrical systems while maintaining functional exhaust. 
* **Ventilation:** Continuous ventilation from floor to ceiling is required. Natural or mechanical ventilation must extract air 20” from the ceiling.
* **Lighting:** All lighting and electrical equipment must be moved below 18” from the ceiling. 
* **Heating:** No open flame heaters are allowed. Only heating systems with surface area temperatures of less than 750°F are allowed. The auto ignition temperature of a methane air mixture is about 1000°F. The requirements for heating systems state 750°F to be safely below that temperature. 
* **Technician, Driver and First Responder Training:** Technicians, drivers and first responders all need to be trained relative to CNG and the unique aspects of its application as a vehicle fuel. Drivers need to understand fueling and maintenance procedures. Technicians need to be aware of the different requirements for maintaining natural gas engines vs. diesels as well as the special precautions that need to be taken in service facilities. First responders are to be aware that the CNG tank delivery systems are under very high pressure and must be managed properly in the event of an accident. Fleets should ensure that all these areas are accounted for as new CNG vehicles are deployed.

## 8.2 FUEL SYSTEM: THE WESTPORT FUEL SYSTEMS’ SOLUTION

Westport offers an HPDI natural gas combustion system. It is a direct injection system which injects both diesel fuel (as a pilot for ignition) and natural gas. This fuel system allows engines to run with late cycle direct injection, meaning the timing of the fuel injection is similar to what occurs in a diesel engine, so the efficiency is similar to that of a diesel engine. The natural gas fuel is injected under high pressure. This is necessary to allow enough fuel to enter the cylinder and combust during the tail end of the compression stroke and the early stages of the power stroke (when cylinder pressures are high) at a controlled rate, rather than being pre-mixed where the fuel faces only intake manifold pressure. This form of diesel-like injection allows both a higher compression ratio and throttle-less operation, for higher efficiency [19].

The injector has two needles: an inner needle to inject the small diesel pilot and an outer needle to inject the natural gas (in the gaseous state). The diesel fuel also is used in a hydraulic circuit for actuating the needles and providing lubrication to them. Typical fuel tank designs allow for diesel refueling approximately once for every five fuelings of natural gas.

No spark plugs are needed for this system. Westport believes that the late cycle direct injection system is particularly well-suited for long haul applications for the same reasons that Class 8 trucks use diesel engines — efficiency and reliability.

Another aspect of an LNG system is that of venting. When a tractor is at rest, CNG will maintain a constant tank pressure, though that will rise slightly if the tank warms. However, with LNG you must use a heavily insulated tank to minimize pressure rise during periods of vehicle inactivity. This pressure results from the boiling of the liquid fuel because of the much higher temperature of ambient air vs. the temperature of the liquefied fuel. Figure 10 illustrates the impact on LNG tank pressure of parking with an empty tank vs. a full tank. Figure 10 applies to any system with an LNG tank, including HPDI and spark-ignited vehicles. This is relevant because any venting of the tank produces a direct release of methane into the atmosphere and will quickly offset any GHG emissions gains of a natural gas system. 

The hold time depends on tank design, size, fuel mass, fuel temperature, fuel composition and starting pressure. ### 9.3 RENEWABLE NATURAL GAS PRODUCTION AND COST

RNG is a form of natural gas that can be used as a fuel to power vehicles. It is fully interchangeable with conventional natural gas and has significant environmental benefits. RNG is generated from various feedstock sources such as livestock (e.g., animal manure), landfills, wastewater, and other sources.

Decomposition of these organic materials forms a gas. The gas is then run through a biochemical process referred to as an anaerobic digester. The gas is then conditioned to remove water, CO2, and other elements.

The CO2 benefits of RNG are illustrated in Figure 23. Negative CI can be achieved through animal waste. Feedstock through landfills and wastewater do not produce negative CI but still offer an advantage over diesel and conventional natural gas. Negative CI is desirable. Rather than harmful methane gases being released into the atmosphere, the gas is captured and used to power the vehicles. This has a positive impact on the environment and results in a negative CI because methane otherwise would have been released.

### 10.1 STATE AND FEDERAL REGULATIONS

There is a tremendous amount of governmental regulation that has been created over the last several years with the intention of providing cleaner air and decarbonizing the transportation industry.

These governmental regulations are being applied on multiple fronts. First, EPA released a new standard for engines built in 2027 and later. This will reduce NOx from 0.2 g/bhp-hr to 0.035 g/bhp-hr, an 85% reduction. Additional components will need to be added which will add cost and complexity to diesel engines.

The EPA also proposed CO2 reductions in the form of the GHG Phase 3 regulations. This regulation will be imposed at the vehicle level. For medium-duty trucks, a 50% reduction is mandated for 2032. For Class 7 and 8 heavy-duty day cab and sleeper tractors, a 27% reduction will go into effect over this timeframe.

Zero-emission and near-zero emission vehicle mandates also are going into effect at the state level. California has led this effort and 17 other states have signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU). These regulations are being imposed on the OEMs (ACT Rule) and the fleets. The 2024 ACF regulation will have the biggest impact on municipalities and drayage operations. Starting January 1, 2024, fleets can only purchase ZEVs. See Figure 31.

The impact of these regulations on the natural gas engine is as follows:
1. Natural gas engines can easily meet the EPA 27 criteria pollutant regulations, therefore the initial cost difference between the natural gas and diesel engine will be significantly reduced because EPA 27 will add more cost and complexity to diesel engines.
2. Natural gas engines can be used as a tool to help OEMs meet the GHG Phase 3 regulations. The net CO2 benefit of natural gas is on the order of 13% to 18% lower than diesel from a well-to-wheels standpoint.
3. Natural gas engines will not comply with the ZEV mandates. However, if a natural gas engine is combined with a hybrid, it can qualify as a near-zero emission vehicle. Fleets will be able to purchase 50% of their ZEV required vehicles with a hybrid/natural gas product. (Note: a diesel hybrid with enough battery capacity to run 75 miles on electric power alone also can qualify for the NZEV. However, at the time this report was written, the study team was unaware of any such diesel hybrids being offered in the Class 4 to 8 space.)

Industry groups and fleets have voiced concern that natural gas vehicles are not given adequate credit and recognition when it comes to the ACT and ACF rules, but at the time this report was written, there has been no progress toward getting California to recognize this.

### 10.2 THE CALIFORNIA CONUNDRUM

CARB is a state agency with jurisdiction in the state of California. However, history has shown that what CARB mandates has a ripple effect across the entire nation if not the world. ### 12.1 USING AN ALTERNATIVE FUEL IN A COMMERCIAL VEHICLE ENGINE

One of the biggest differences between the alternate fuels is the method of fuel induction into the engine’s combustion chamber. Diesel fuel can be directly injected into the power cylinder at the correct time and at the correct rate to maximize power and other combustion characteristics. This allows the engine to operate at much higher compression ratios than an Otto cycle (spark ignited) engine, thus resulting in significantly better overall efficiency and performance.

Many of the alternative fuels that we will discuss have to be introduced into the combustion chamber as a gas premixed with the intake charge. The ignition properties of this mixture mean that the charge must be ignited by a spark plug and at significantly lower compression ratios to prevent ignition before the proper time in the combustion chamber as well as combustion knock. This results in lower overall efficiency of the engine. Natural gas, as an alternative fuel, falls into this category.

Many of these alternate fuels are sold on a DGE basis. This corresponds to the amount of fuel that contains the same amount of energy as a gallon of diesel fuel. However, to get a true comparison between the fuels in terms of on-road fuel economy, you must include the difference in the efficiency of the engine using the fuel.

Each of the fuels requires specific vehicle components to facilitate fuel storage, delivery, metering, and induction as well as facilitating proper ignition. In addition, infrastructure to facilitate fuel replenishment is required.

### 12.2 DIFFERENT TYPES OF ALTERNATIVE FUELS

Many different types of alternative fuels are available for commercial vehicles and they each offer different benefits and challenges, from creation and transport to storage and emissions.

#### 12.2.1 Biodiesel

Biodiesel is mostly produced using a feedstock mixture of used and/or new cooking oil that has been extracted from plant-based sources. Biodiesel is produced by a process called transesterification. This process uses a small amount of alcohol (usually methanol), a catalyst and heat combined with the vegetable oil to produce the biodiesel. The resulting biodiesel product is then refined to meet ASTM specifications (ASTM D6751) [88].

Once the product is ready for use, it is usually blended with fossil-based diesel fuel up to a concentration of 20% or less (often referred to as B20). Today’s engine manufacturers have approved the use of biodiesel blends of up to 20% in their engines. Running higher concentrations of biodiesel are likely to cause problems including compromised cold performance and increased NOx emissions.

Biodiesel generally has a higher “cloud point” than standard diesel fuel. Cloud point is the temperature at which wax crystals start to precipitate out of the fuel. When these wax crystals form, they can plug fuel filters and increase the flow resistance of the fuel. Therefore, biodiesel blends tend not to perform as well at low temperatures compared to #2 diesel fuel.

Biodiesel blends have better lubricity than low sulfur diesel fuel, so fuel system component wear is less of an issue. However, this is not considered to be a big advantage because most diesel fuels have a small amount of a lubricity-improving additive to mitigate this issue.

#### 12.2.3 Ethanol

Ethanol (CH3CH2OH) has been used as an internal combustion engine fuel for many years by being mixed in relatively small quantities with gasoline. This formula works very well in gasoline engines. However, mixing ethanol with diesel fuel is not as straightforward.

The study team is aware of only a small amount of research exploring the advantages and disadvantages of using diesel/ethanol blends. Advantages include using a domestic renewable resource similar to what is done with blending it into gasoline. Gasoline engines can be configured to use a high percentage of ethanol, like those that can use E85 (85% ethanol). There are roughly 4,200 stations around the country that sell E85 according to the DOE’s Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy website, but many are not configured to be accessible by large commercial vehicles [90]. The referenced website has filters that allow you to sort what types of vehicles can be fueled at the locations. The energy.gov website currently doesn’t publish any data relative to the availability of pure ethanol (e.g., E98). Many of the newest engines that can use E85 feature direct injection of the fuel into the combustion chamber but still use a spark plug to ignite the fuel at the correct time.

Another option is to blend ethanol with diesel fuel. However, adding ethanol to diesel fuel significantly lowers the fuel flash point causing safety concerns and there are barriers to getting engine manufacturers to approve its use.

Another alternative for using ethanol has emerged recently. A company called ClearFlame Engine Technologies is developing commercial vehicle engine alternatives. The engine being developed uses direct injection of ethanol and compression ignition rather than a homogeneous charge model where the fuel is introduced in the intake manifold and mixed with the intake air as is done in a gasoline engine. The engine’s charge air cooling and EGR cooling are reduced to produce a high enough compression temperature to compression ignite the methanol fuel. Direct injection and compression ignition facilitate the use of higher compression ratios and results in improved engine efficiency.

#### 12.2.4 Methanol

Methanol (CH3OH) as an internal combustion engine fuel is primarily used by the motorsports industry in North America. It has combustion characteristics that make it very well suited to that application — in fact, methanol is a required fuel in several racing circuits. The primary reason for this is that the flames in the event of an accident do not produce a cloud of smoke that obscures visibility for approaching vehicles.

In addition, the combustion of methanol has a significant amount of water as a byproduct and a lower flame temperature which helps to keep the combustion process cooler. This makes the fuel suitable for applications where there is very high-power output compared to the engine’s displacement.

There is a small number of trucks operating in China on pure methanol, but it has not gained widespread acceptance in other parts of the world.

Methanol is manufactured by a steam reforming and catalytic process using natural gas and water as the feedstocks. A gallon of methanol has about 45% of the energy content of a gallon of diesel fuel. Therefore, to get the same power output for a given power stroke of an internal combustion engine, you must have more than twice the amount of fuel. It takes less oxygen to facilitate the combustion, so the air-to-fuel ratios can be much lower.

Methanol combustion can be controlled to yield very low NOx and particulate matter. Unburned hydrocarbons and carbon monoxide (CO) tend to be higher than diesel, but the CO can be controlled very well by a three-way catalyst, and hydrocarbon emissions can be partially controlled. Three-way catalysts are not as effective at reducing unburned hydrocarbons except at high exhaust temperatures if they are methane based.

Methanol can be used as either a spark-ignition fuel or a compression-ignition fuel (direct injection). It has most often been used in spark ignition engines. Only a small amount of research has been done to understand all aspects of using methanol as a compression-ignition fuel. It presents some difficulties in engine management at very light load operation when used in compression ignition. Of course, NOx output will be higher in compression-ignition mode.

Methanol is highly hygroscopic — which means that it is prone to attract a great deal of water from the air around it. In addition, because of its water attraction properties as well as other factors, methanol can lead to a high amount of corrosion in aluminum and carbon steel components. Therefore, it would require a great deal of engineering and materials work to make a highly durable commercial vehicle engine that uses methanol as a primary fuel.

#### 12.2.6 Propane

Propane (C3H8) also has been used extensively as a motor vehicle fuel for many years. For example, many forklifts operated in and around warehouse facilities use propane as a fuel. This is the largest single application of propane for internal combustion engines. Propane is a gas at atmospheric pressure and temperature but becomes a liquid when compressed to a relatively low pressure of about 150 psi. The low storage pressure allows the storage tanks to be much less expensive than those for CNG or LNG.

Most engines that have been configured to use propane as a fuel generally mix the propane into the intake system before it is taken into the power cylinder. Ignition is accomplished by a spark plug in the combustion chamber. Because of propane’s volumetric characteristics as a gas, generally an engine will make less maximum power for a given displacement than if configured to work with gasoline. Some recent research has been done on directly injecting liquid propane into the power cylinder to make use of compression ignition vs. a spark plug, but the study team is unaware of any production engine configurations that employ this technology.

A gallon of liquid propane has roughly 65% of the amount of energy of a gallon of diesel fuel, so the same points apply relative to tank volume as the other liquid fuels discussed above. In addition, if the engine is set up for spark ignition and either intake or direct injection, the efficiency of the engine will likely be less than a comparable diesel.

Propane is generally obtained as a byproduct of either natural gas processing or the refining of petroleum products. There are roughly 2,000 public propane fuel stations in the US according to the DOE’s Energy Efficiency & Renewable Energy website — so they might not always be convenient to a particular operation [92].

From an emissions standpoint, like with other alcohol-based fuels, NOx and PM emissions can be controlled very well, and carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons can be controlled very well using a three-way catalyst.

Propane has not been broadly used as a commercial vehicle engine fuel for larger vehicles, although one major engine manufacturer does offer a propane version of one of its smaller engines. It has been used in local delivery trucks, school buses and utility trucks.

Propane is heavier than air, so safety procedures need to be in place to handle potential leaks. Propane generally has a compound called ethyl mercaptan added to give it a “rotten egg” smell, so leaks are easier to detect.

#### 12.2.7 Renewable Propane

Renewable propane comes from feedstocks such as vegetable oils, animal fats and restaurant grease [24]. Renewable propane is made via a process similar to renewable diesel, using a hydrotreating production process. It has identical properties in terms of energy content, NOx, PM, and performance as propane, so it can be used as a drop-in fuel for a propane-certified engine.

It provides a carbon intensity reduction of 60% to 70% relative to conventional diesel. It is used primarily in California, Oregon, and Washington. Today, approximately 4.5 million gallons are produced annually. The EPA Renewable Fuel Standard, Renewable Identification Number (RIN) credits are only available for transportation applications of renewable propane. In addition, California LCFS incentivizes only on-road transportation and forklift applications for renewable propane [42].

#### 12.2.8 Synthetically Derived Fuels

It is possible to create fuels synthetically using feedstocks of compounds high in carbon (like coal and recovered CO2) with hydrogen. The processes vary, but most are not capable of being produced at high volume at this point. The cost of these fuels is not well documented either, so it is impossible to see if they could ever be competitive with some of the other fuels discussed in this report.

All these synthetic fuels use hydrogen as one of the feedstocks. Today, virtually all hydrogen is created via a process called steam methane reforming which uses natural gas and water as inputs. The process has a very high CO2 output and there are currently no viable/widespread means of carbon capture and storage to manage the CO2 output. Until that technology becomes economically viable for very high production, it is likely that synthetic fuel production will have higher GHG emissions than traditional fuel production and use processes.

The study team encourages work on these technologies to continue at very high levels so that the economics can make sense in the coming years.

### 12.3 OTHER TECHNOLOGIES THAT WILL COMPETE WITH ALTERNATIVE FUELS

As new ZEV technologies advance and regulations become far more stringent, fossil-fueled diesel or alternative fueled internal combustion engines will not be an option in many applications and regions. As such, the diesel engine and truck will need to be modified to meet upcoming more stringent emissions regulations. It is anticipated that the cost of a diesel vehicle will increase significantly. Estimates range from $2,300 to $42,000 to meet the NOx thresholds and warranty requirements of the EPA Clean Truck Plan governing heavy-duty emissions [71]. These include NOx requirements announced in December 2022 [71].

These new requirements also will focus on light load and startup conditions where the SCR system is too cool to be effective. Emission control devices will improve the SCR system’s NOx conversion efficiency by improving temperature conditions. Example technologies include close-coupled catalysts, a second doser of DEF at the turbocharger exhaust outlet, SCR system heaters, insulation, cylinder deactivation to increase exhaust temperatures, and combinations of these and other new technologies [74].

#### 12.3.1 Hybrids

Hybrids provide another potential solution in this phase of the messy middle. They can be powered by diesel or renewable fuels and can provide adequate range even with renewable fuels because of their higher efficiency. They provide regenerative braking and thus recover energy while going downhill and in stop-and-go traffic, store that energy, and then use it when needed. They are an excellent solution for many medium-duty and shorter regional haul applications less than 200 miles per day. However, they provide limited benefits in long-haul applications because of the weight of the batteries needed for those distances and the steadier cruising conditions.

As noted earlier, Hyliion planned to offer a new hybrid solution but has discontinued it at the time this report was released. The product had adequate battery storage so vehicles could travel 75 miles on the battery. The vehicle used a 12-liter engine running on RNG that drives only an electric generator. Drive was provided by tandem electric rear axles with motor-generators that could be reversed in order to recover energy during braking. After driving 75 miles on the batteries, the vehicle then used the onboard engine-generator for greatly extended range. The combination of batteries and the electric

#### 12.3.2 Battery Electric Vehicles

These vehicles will run off the present power grid, which increasingly is being made greener via the use of renewable sources like wind and solar power, and zero-emission sources like nuclear power. They will become true zero-emission vehicles when the power grid is entirely free of the need for fossil fueled generation.

NACFE has written many Guidance Reports regarding the applicability of BEVs to trucking [122]. They provide a great solution for Class 3 to 6 vehicles that run in urban stop-and-go, regional haul with return-to-base operations, and travel less than 200 miles per day. Some of these applications today have positive total cost of operation (TCO). Examples include terminal tractors, vans and step vans, medium-duty box trucks and many vehicles involved in short or medium regional haul. Vehicle prices are expected to decrease over time as production volumes increase, and TCO is expected to continue to improve. Federal, state, and local incentives currently are required to bring the out-the-door vehicle price paid by fleets to parity with the diesels they are replacing.

H2FCs and BEVs are the only products which will meet evolving ZEV rules. The BEV provides a zero-emission vehicle solution with the simplest vehicle configuration of all these options. They also are expected to have considerably lower maintenance costs than current diesel trucks since there are fewer moving parts, no highly stressed internal combustion engine parts or emissions systems, easier reporting requirements, etc. The BEV is the best in terms of overall energy efficiency. It has an overall efficiency of about 65% to 85% from the charger to the wheels, as compared with approximately 40% for a diesel engine [72][60].

The three major challenges for BEVs are infrastructure/charging, range and cost. Today, many fleets are finding that the BEV arrives from the OEM, but the charging infrastructure is not available. In some cases, this is just a planning problem between the OEM, fleet, charging company and the utility. However, other problems include that some utility companies are at or near capacity to deliver electricity and additional capacity is years away. NACFE conducted Run on Less Electric – DEPOT in 2023 to explore some of these problems with fleets that have expanded the number of BEVs they are operating. Reports on this Run will be forthcoming in 2024.

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act are providing significant funding and tax incentives to help enable growth in energy generation and delivery capacities [76][77][78][79]. Range and weight issues are preventing the BEV from becoming a solution for distances longer than 200 to 300 miles. Recently some OEMs have introduced products that begin to address this. However, the actual payload capacity still has not been publicly stated for this range. BEVs are given an additional 2,000 lbs. federal gross vehicle weight rating (GVWR) exemption, and thus can weigh up to 82,000 lbs. However, the actual maximum payload of BEV trucks will be less than with diesels which will limit their use in some duty cycles.

## 14 CONCLUSIONS

After researching natural gas engines for use in commercial vehicles and speaking with fleets and other experts, the study team developed the following key findings.

1. There appears to be a wide range in perception and results regarding the business case for natural gas. Some fleets have been able to save money with natural gas fueled vehicles while others select them only for sustainability reasons. Some key factors to make a business case positive for natural gas are: Full utilization of fueling stations, utilizing both medium- and heavy-duty trucks, taking advantage of all incentives. Also, owning the fueling station allows the fleet to take advantage of tax incentives. Fleets that have been successful with natural gas engines have been “all in” and built stations behind the fence, which can offer a lower price per cubic foot. The perceived financial savings of natural gas has varied over time as the price differential between natural gas and diesel varies and system reliability has changed.
2. There are several positive environmental aspects of natural gas engines. It has ultra-low NOx capability. The spark-ignited natural gas engine produces significantly lower NOx than a diesel engine because of the three-way catalyst, which is enabled by the spark-ignited, stoichiometric combustion process. Some engines that meet the 0.02 g/bhp-hr and the California standards already have been released. It does not require a DPF. This means there is no need for DPF regens and cleaning. Also, no DEF needs to be added and there are no filters to maintain. It offers a well-to-wheels CO2 reduction. CARB estimates the well-to-wheels CO2 reduction of conventional CNG compared to diesel at approximately 15%. Compared to conventional natural gas, RNG is the more attractive solution from an environmental standpoint. It has lower CI than diesel and in some cases, it has negative CI because it offsets expected methane emissions from animal waste, decaying plants and other sources. There always will always be animal waste, decaying plants, swamps, etc. and RNG is an excellent way to take advantage of this energy and improve the environment. Considering all of these items, natural gas vehicles will be a good solution to help OEMs upcoming NOx and the proposed GHG regulations which will take place in 2027.
3. There are environmental concerns with natural gas. Methane leakage from aging pipelines and natural gas infrastructure and outdated equipment is a major concern. When released into the atmosphere, methane is 30 to 80 times worse in terms of GHG than CO2 emissions with impacts lasting for several decades. This leakage could offset the reduced CO2 value of natural gas. The EPA estimates this leakage at 1.4%, and there are other studies that show higher leakage rates. This could easily offset the 15% CO2 reduction quoted above. There are ways to curtail this leakage via maintenance and repair, and replacement of outdated valves and pressure regulating equipment that was designed prior to awareness of methane’s warming potential. A certification process can be done to certify such work, and it is referred to as MiQ certification. It ensures upstream quality of equipment to minimize leakage. (Note that while methane leakage is bad for the environment, RNG captures this from animal and other waste converting it to CO2 and water vapor in the engine, and that is the value of RNG.)

4. Sustainability goals, regulations, and the California conundrum are considerations. The CO2, NOx, and PM benefits of natural gas-powered vehicles allows fleets and shippers to improve the carbon footprint and reduce their Scope 2 and 3 emissions. Currently, none of this is regulated, but fleets are monitoring it and making long-term sustainability goals. Natural gas also helps OEMs and the engine manufacturers meet GHG regulations and NOx and PM standards. However, in California and other states, ZEV mandates are being established, and natural gas does not qualify.
5. The new 15-liter Cummins X15N engine seems promising. In the past, natural gas engines experienced reliability problems, but many of these have been fixed. Other changes Cummins has made to its engine include the following. Spark plug change intervals are extended from their current product because of better cooling. Cummins is trying to sync with the oil change interval (60k miles). The engine is believed to be quieter than diesel because of the spark-ignited combustion process. Cummins says the performance of the X15N is almost indistinguishable from the torque and power of diesels (and a major improvement relative to all its predecessors such as the Cummins 12N).
6. Aftertreatment is simple and more reliable. Natural gas engines do not require a DPF or SCR system, eliminating a major cause of downtime, warranty and repairs. Spark-ignited natural gas uses only a three-way catalyst. This will have increased value in 2027 with the new EPA standards. The 2027 EPA standards for both significantly lower NOx as well as better low temperature performance will likely force diesel aftertreatment to become significantly more complex and costly.
7. Natural gas is very abundant in the US. The question is how accessible it is for a given fleet’s routes. Maps are available to show public fueling stations, however if a fleet wants to run natural gas, it will need to work with the fuel providers to see if additional stations are needed.
8. There is a question as to whether there will be an ample supply of RNG. NGVA has projected significant increases in RNG over the next several years. Given the introduction of the Cummins X15N (and Cummins’ projections), there could be a couple hundred thousand natural gas engines in heavy-duty applications later in the decade. If so, they would consume roughly 3 to 3.5 billion DGE per year. NGVA has shared information where there could be as much as 14 billion DGE per year by 2030. The question is: how it will be allocated between transportation, electricity generation, etc. and whether subsidies will remain as well as what mandates might come up.
9. Because of economics and the environment, there are a few points to consider when comparing BEV and CNG. Converting to natural gas is a long-term decision. Capital for fueling stations or charging stations, etc. is 5 to 10+ year investment. From a CO2 standpoint it is essential on a local level to consider the grid’s dependence on fossil fuel vs. renewable/nuclear is essential. Local consideration of grid and CNG capacity is also a factor. CNG requires installing venting equipment, methane detection, and alarms in shops, which can be an expensive infrastructure investment. Once infrastructure is installed, it reinforces buying more CNG.

## 16 APPENDIX A: CARBON INTENSITY COMPARISON AND BENEFITS

### What is carbon intensity?
Many papers and articles make a reference to a term called carbon intensity (CI). There are several definitions of the term.

For example, the Department of Energy (DOE), website defines carbon intensity as follows: “Carbon intensity is the measure of CO₂ produced per dollar of GDP. In other words, it's a measure of how much CO₂ we emit when we generate one dollar in our economy [100].”

The World Bank uses the following definition. “Carbon intensity is the ratio of carbon dioxide per unit of energy, or the amount of carbon dioxide emitted as a result of using one unit of energy in production. Emission intensities are also used to compare the environmental impact of different fuels or activities [101].”

Chevron defines the term as follows. “Simply put, carbon intensity is a measure of carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases (CO₂e) per unit of activity, like generating a product [102].”

The common theme here is that the amount of CO₂ or CO₂ equivalent that results from some kind of activity — be it economic, chemical or transportation related — is compared to something relevant in the denominator of the equation.

The state of California has a slightly different definition of carbon intensity. “Carbon intensity is expressed in grams of carbon dioxide equivalent per megajoule of energy provided by that fuel. [Carbon intensity] takes into account the GHG emissions associated with all of the steps of producing, transporting, and consuming a fuel — also known as a complete life cycle of that fuel.”

So, they define carbon intensity as the carbon that results from the entire process of managing a fuel from the extraction, processing, transporting and using the fuel. This is commonly referred to as well-to-wheels. See Figure 43 which shows these major sources of emissions. However, California also has published a document that compares the carbon intensity score for the various steps in this process as part of its Low Carbon Fuel Standard credit program [103].

## CO₂ Creation Tank-to-Wheels

Why does a natural gas engine release less CO₂ than a comparable diesel engine, especially when the mileage the vehicle gets is less per diesel gallon equivalent (DGE), than the diesel powered vehicle?

The answer has to do with the composition of the fuel that is being ignited in the combustion chamber of the engine. Natural gas, which is mostly methane (about 94%, on average), can create the same amount of heat with many fewer carbon atoms than what is contained in diesel fuel.

Methane has a chemical formula of CH₄ – that is one carbon atom for every four hydrogen atoms. The heat that is generated in combustion is released when the carbon-hydrogen bonds are broken, and the resulting atoms combine with oxygen to produce CO₂ and water. Diesel fuel, on the other hand, has just slightly more than two hydrogen atoms per carbon atom. Therefore, when combustion occurs with diesel fuel, there is less energy released per carbon atom than with methane.

Methane combustion produces about two-thirds the amount of CO₂ compared to diesel fuel for the amount of heat generated [104]. Also, a spark-ignited engine is somewhat less efficient than the modern diesel engine — about 15% to 20% less. So, the advantage of two-thirds of the CO₂ output starts to go away because of lower efficiency.

In addition, methane must be compressed to 3500 to 5000 psi to be stored in a reasonable space on the vehicle. This compression process generally happens at the fueling station before the gas is delivered to the vehicle. Compressing methane to those pressures takes a good bit of energy — about 10% of what is contained in the gas by weight. Of course, depending on where you are in the country, the generation of that energy to accomplish the compression also results in at least some CO₂ output.

If you add up all this, natural gas used in a commercial vehicle still produces less CO₂ than a diesel powered one doing the same job. The range of this number varies, but about 85% is the average. The range of this number can be fairly wide, depending on a variety of conditions and assumptions. It is reasonable to assume this number could range between 75% and 95% depending on the many factors involved. This analysis only applies to the CO₂ generated from the fueling station to the vehicle going down the road doing its everyday work (i.e., tank-to-wheels + gas compression).

Additional factors that are not considered in the above analysis are the CO₂ emissions that come from the extraction, refining, piping, transporting and delivering the diesel fuel to a diesel powered vehicle (i.e., well-to-tank). Also, the above analysis did not consider the CO₂ emissions of extraction, processing, piping and transportation of the natural gas in the same manner as the CARB analysis.

Natural gas extraction and processing has one other effect that has the potential to offset the advantage it has from a CO₂ standpoint — methane leakage. Methane released into the atmosphere has a much stronger global warming effect than CO₂ — 80 times as high just after release to about 20 or 30 times after about 20 years.

Therefore, small methane leaks in extraction and processing can make a big difference in natural gas’ effective CO₂ benefits as a fuel.

## 17 APPENDIX B: SUMMARY OF NATURAL GAS CHALLENGES

We all are familiar with the fuel storage options on commercial vehicles when it comes to diesels. Most fuel is stored in saddle tanks behind the front wheels. Diesel fuel is quite energy dense, and enough fuel can be stored on the vehicle to accomplish well over a day’s work without stopping to refuel.

These saddle tanks are relatively light without fuel in them and mounting to the frame rails is simple. The combustibility range for diesel is relatively narrow, meaning that it has low volatility. Therefore, the tank will not need to keep the fuel under pressure and that vapor need not even be collected to keep it out of the environment. This makes the tankage inexpensive and simple and makes the fuel very safe to transport and use.

Most vehicles must store natural gas in a compressed form in tanks that mount behind the cab on the frame rails. The gas is stored at a pressure of 3500 to 5000 psi, so the tanks must be specially made to withstand the high pressure. They generally are constructed with a substantial metal lining (that is non-porous) and that lining is surrounded by carbon fiber to provide the strength needed to retain that pressure. Because of the danger of failure, tanks must be inspected periodically for damage and replaced if any is found. Because of the danger of tank damage from road debris or accidents, most compressed natural gas tanks are not mounted in the saddle position.

The solution is tank racks that mount behind the cab of the vehicle. There are typically several tanks stacked above one another, taking up about 24” of frame rail space. We are told that up to about 170 diesel gallon equivalent (DGE) of storage can be placed behind the cab giving the vehicle a substantial range. These tanks lengthen the wheelbase of the vehicle compared to a conventional diesel vehicle. See Figure 45. This also makes it difficult to combine a sleeper cab arrangement with compressed natural gas fueling. Extended wheelbases affect the vehicle’s turning radius as well as affecting weight distribution of the load. Reportedly, there are very few sleeper cab natural gas powered units in operation today.

It is possible to store LNG in tanks that are saddle mounted because of its increased density. However, the process of getting the gas to liquified form is quite energy intensive and storage can be somewhat problematic. To keep the fuel in liquified form at near atmospheric pressure requires well insulated tanks. Furthermore, as the fuel warms up, it changes back into a gas and periodically must be vented to the atmosphere or captured in some way for reuse. The vehicle systems necessary for preparing LNG for use on the engine are slightly different from those necessary for CNG, mainly so enough heat will be available to evaporate the fuel. There are very few fueling facilities that can handle the delivery of LNG to vehicles, so choosing this fuel takes careful consideration.

Renewable natural gas (RNG) is chemically equivalent to pipeline natural gas. In fact, most facilities that process RNG just put it into the system at the nearest access to the pipeline. RNG is sold to customers under contract so that gas that goes into the vehicle is generally not pure RNG. Therefore, the engine and vehicle systems are equivalent regardless of the fuel that you happen to be using.

### Methane Leaks

Leakage of methane during the capture and processing of it is known to be a major source of greenhouse gas emissions. Many papers and articles have been published over the last few years that highlight this issue. There are several problems related to leakage. First, methane has a global warming potential that is about 80 times higher than CO₂ for the first 20 years after it is released. This effect decreases over time, but its significance should not be underestimated. Therefore, a very small methane leak can be worse for the environment than a much larger emission of CO₂. Second, studies have indicated that a major source of methane leakage is associated with the extraction, movement and processing of it before it ever reaches the vehicle.

Leaks from fueling stations and from the vehicle itself are a smaller concern since the volume of gas involved is small versus the capacity of the tanks and associated piping. Therefore, the major concerns is the need to vent tanks for repairs or prolonged, significant leakage at a facility.

Based on experts in the field, the study team learned that the majority of leakage occurs upstream of the truck in the supply chain. About two-thirds to three-quarters of leakage occurs where the gas is collected and pressurized for transportation to the fueling station. Some of the key steps to get the fuel are:

1. Remove the raw fuel out of the ground. It contains both CO₂ and methane.
2. Gather and boost. In this stage there are a lot of compressors and valves where leakage occurs.
3. Remove impurities and separate contained liquids from the gas. This is the last part of the process where leakage dominates.
4. Transmission via interstate pipeline, etc. Here operations are more regulated, and equipment serviced more often.

Typically, the older the equipment, the greater the leakage both because newer equipment is better designed to minimize leakage, and because it is less maintenance intensive. Much of the leakage is found to be in the valves and pipelines like those in Figure 46.

First responders must be notified about vehicles that use natural gas and any safety procedures that need to be followed in the event of an incident.

Technicians must be trained on proper safety procedures when working on natural gas vehicles. Special personal protection equipment is not required, but high pressure leaks could present dangers that technicians have not encountered before. Natural gas tanks require periodic inspections that must be completed and documented. Several OEMs offer training classes specifically tailored for natural gas powered engines.

Engine oil and oil drain intervals for natural gas powered vehicles are generally different and more frequent than their equivalent diesel counterparts, though special engine lubes that can be used with either fuel are available. In addition, it is critical that spark plugs be changed on a schedule that is often more frequent than normal diesel maintenance intervals in order to prevent durability issues with the engines.
