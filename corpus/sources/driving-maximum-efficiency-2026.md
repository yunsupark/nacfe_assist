# Overview

## Purpose

This report highlights the existing technologies being implemented by high-efficiency fleets to achieve best-in-class freight efficiency and lower-carbon performance. The North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) and Shell have engaged in multiple collaborations, including Run on Less, with NACFE serving as the reporting partner measuring and verifying the freight efficiency of the Shell Starship. These collaborations demonstrate the freight-efficiency and lower-carbon benefits of technologies in production today that are usable in most, if not all, freight-hauling applications. These technologies include various aerodynamic aids, powertrain options, low-carbon fuels, and low-viscosity lubricants.

## About NACFE

NACFE (www.nacfe.org) works to drive the development and adoption of efficiency-enhancing, environmentally beneficial, and cost-effective technologies, services, and operational practices in the movement of goods across North America.

It provides independent, unbiased research, including Confidence Reports on available technologies and Guidance Reports on emerging ones. These highlight the benefits and consequences of each technology and deliver decision-making tools for fleets, manufacturers, and others. NACFE collaborates with RMI on a variety of projects, including the Run on Less demonstration series, as well as with industry partners, including Shell, on projects such as the Shell Starship initiative.

# Technology and fuel efficiency

## What is "best in class"?

There are several industry benchmarks for average Class 8 freight efficiency. The US Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) publishes its Highway Series dataset, which includes vehicle miles traveled and fuel consumed by vehicle category, including combination heavy trucks, for all motor vehicles that use the public road infrastructure. Given the much larger proportion of over-the-road freight tractors and their greater fuel consumption, NACFE uses the combination trucks category as a reference point for all Class 8 tractors used for goods movement, even though the category includes some non-freight-moving trucks, such as dump tractors and other vehicles.

US average fuel economy has been on a general uptrend for many years (Figure 1). However, this has not always been the case. Average fuel efficiency had just crossed 6.0 miles per gallon (mpg) in 2008 before it began a three-year decline as the need for early exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) engines to meet EPA04 and EPA07 emissions standards became more pressing. Following 2010, most engine manufacturers implemented selective catalyst reduction (SCR) to meet US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) regulations. This technology helped to reduce EGR rates significantly. This change in emissions technology also helped stop the decline in freight efficiency, although it was not until 2017 that the US fleet regained the 6.0-mpg freight efficiency level it had achieved nearly a decade earlier.

In the FFS survey, fleets are asked about the technologies and practices they implemented in the prior year's purchases. The most recent survey was completed in 2024, which means the data apply to new trucks acquired in 2022 and 2023. While this group of fleets has higher average fuel efficiency compared with fleets described by the FHWA data, average fuel efficiency increased rapidly overall with greater adoption of fuel-saving technologies and practices.

The survey indicates that technology adoption has flattened in the most recent five-year period. This is in part due to the changing mix of fleets that NACFE now includes in its survey, which itself is reflective of the overall change to more regional-haul in the overall US fleet. Regional-haul fleets have implemented some technologies, such as tractor and trailer aerodynamics or heavy down speeding, at a lower rate than disparate route long-haul sleeper fleets, due to their differing duty cycles, which can include more urban and slower speed operations.

Another reason why adoption may be leveling off is that it is not possible for a fleet to implement all 86 technologies and practices on a truck, and some fleets are nearing the limit of what they believe they should implement. Several of the technologies listed are alternatives (e.g., low rolling resistance dual tires or wide-base single tires). The fleets in the survey that purchase the most technology have adoption rates of just above 50%.

The FFS fleets with the highest adoption rates have higher average fuel efficiency compared with the other fleets in the survey (Figure 3). While this does not prove causation, it shows that there is a strong correlation between adopting fuel-saving technology and improving freight efficiency. The most freight-efficient fleets are averaging more than 8 mpg, and one fleet in the study averages more than 9 mpg, which means its fuel costs are more than 15% lower than an above-average fleet operating modern trucks achieving 7.5 mpg.

Since it became available in 2016, its adoption rate has increased steadily, reaching 60% in the most recent survey. The latest engine oils include low high-temperature, high-shear (HTHS) products that are designed for enhanced fuel efficiency. Many of today's SAE 30-grade lubricants formulated within the standard 3.5-mPa·s HTHS limit are thicker and closer to the SAE 40 grade. However, the API FA-4 standards allow lubricant manufacturers to formulate within the full SAE 30 range, with a minimum HTHS viscosity of 2.9 mPa·s. This lower HTHS limit means lower-viscosity oils that remain within the SAE 30 grade, providing less resistance to flow for less fuel use while maintaining performance in harsh conditions.

In a Confidence Report on low-viscosity lubricants, such as 10W-30, NACFE found that fleets reported a 0.5–1.5% fuel saving when using low-viscosity engine oil versus traditional 15W-40 oil. Fleets that tested or implemented FA-4 oil found an additional 0.4–0.7% improvement in freight efficiency. Further benefits were gained by fleets implementing synthetic transmission and axle fluids. At the time of the report, NACFE found that improved freight efficiency and longer maintenance intervals offset any challenges related to cost and availability while on the road.

## Cutting fuel costs by up to 3.9% with low-HTHS technology engine oils

Field trials demonstrate that fleets can reduce fuel costs by up to 2% by moving to Shell Rotella T5 10W-30 from a conventional 15W-40 engine oil. Adopting Shell Rotella T6 5W-30 can push that saving up to 2.8%, and using the latest synthetic, low-HTHS Shell Rotella FE 5W-30 FA-4 oil can reduce fuel costs by up to 3.9% while maintaining compatibility with major OEM specifications, such as Cummins CES 20087 and Detroit DFS 93K223, and improving long-term engine durability.

> "Using the latest synthetic, low-HTHS Shell Rotella FE 5W-30 FA-4 oil can reduce fuel costs by up to 3.9% compared with conventional 15W-40."

NACFE lists the benefits and challenges of adopting synthetic transmission fluids on its website. These include:
- improving energy efficiency and fuel economy by maintaining stable viscosity across a wide temperature range, reducing friction and heat losses;
- advances in formulation and increases in production scale, improving performance and reducing the cost gap compared with conventional lubricants;
- higher thermal and oxidative stability, enabling longer maintenance and drain intervals, reducing service frequency;
- improved component protection from more consistent viscosity at high temperatures, leading to reduced wear in engines and transmissions;
- the barrier of higher up-front costs, though these may be offset by fuel savings and extended maintenance intervals; and
- potential limits to on-road availability, particularly outside a fleet's primary service network.

While NACFE has not completed an in-depth Confidence Report on synthetic transmission fluids, the high adoption rates—close to 100% (Figure 4)—by the NACFE Fleet Fuel Study (FFS) fleets indicate that the benefits offered by the technology far outweigh any challenges.

## Extending fuel efficiency across the drivetrain

Shell has extended the fuel-efficiency benefits of low-viscosity synthetic lubricants across the drivetrain. Shell Spirax S6 GXME 75W-80 manual transmission fluid has special frictional properties and high fluidity to reduce energy loss. It also reduces the operating temperature, which, combined with high oxidation stability, extends fluid life.

Shell Spirax S6 AXME 80W-140 and Shell Spirax AXRME 75W-90 synthetic fluids extend the advantages to the axle, with an additional fuel saving of up to 1% for Shell Spirax AXRME 75W-90 when tested against other products for the same application.

# Implementing a change

## Rapid paybacks for technology upgrades are possible

Almost any change in technology or processes requires investment and has the potential for increased risk. Low-viscosity engine oil and other lubricants have been studied by OEMs. They specify the approved oils and lubricants for engines, transmissions, and other components. As long as fleets adhere to these specifications, the risks associated with changing lubricants are very low. Fleets considering a change should consult with their OEM(s) to ensure compatibility with their applications.

Assuming there are no compatibility issues, fleets must also consider cost and return on investment (ROI). Cost differences are specific to each fleet and can vary widely based on what is currently in use, quantities, and other factors. To guide fleets in this decision, NACFE published a confidence matrix for low-viscosity engine oil in 2016 (Figure 5). The matrix indicates that fleets should have high confidence in adopting low-viscosity CK-4 engine oils. Now that more is known about FA-4 oils, if NACFE were to update this matrix, the FA-4 bubble would likely move to the right, indicating even greater confidence in the technology.

> "Fleets should have high confidence in adopting low-viscosity engine oils and should increasingly be looking forward to FA-4 technology"

# Considerations for switching to natural gas and RNG

A switch to RNG is a much larger, long-term decision. Even for fleets already operating CNG trucks and wishing to reduce their emissions, there are many complexities that may impact cost and fuel availability and may require processing of incentives, subsidies, or credits. It is a complex topic, and NACFE goes into considerable detail in its 2024 Natural Gas Confidence Report.

For fleets considering a switch from other fuels, a change to natural gas or RNG requires different trucks and access to fuel stations, or the ability to develop current fueling infrastructure. Considerable investments in vehicles, infrastructure, people, and processes are needed. Additionally, the return on these investments may be influenced by the price of fuel, subsidies, and incentives—which can change. In summary, with respect to the ROI for natural gas trucks, the 2024 NACFE Natural Gas Confidence Report states that the business case for natural gas is fleet-specific. Fleets that fully utilize fueling infrastructure, own behind-the-fence stations, and capture available incentives have achieved cost savings; other fleets adopt natural gas primarily for sustainability reasons. Refer to the full report for the specific details.

A framework (Figure 6) for powertrain decision-making is provided in the 2024 Natural Gas Confidence Report to help guide fleets through this transition along with a large body of work from NACFE on truck electrification, which can be found on NACFE's website.

# Diesel and renewable natural gas

## Diesel optimization

NACFE has been involved with the Shell Starship initiative since its launch in 2018. In its first iteration, the Shell Starship was diesel powered, reflecting the powertrain of 97% of the US long-haul fleets. Over many decades, fleets, manufacturers, and regulators have optimized productivity and improved efficiency while reducing the emissions from freight transportation.

Before EPA GHG phase 1, on-road heavy-duty emissions regulations were mostly concerned with reducing emissions, such as nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter. With GHG phase 1 being approved in 2011 and becoming effective in 2014, engine and truck manufacturers were also accountable for carbon reduction through reduced fuel consumption.

The Shell Starship, using a combination of existing technologies combined with a custom-built aerodynamic cab, showcased the gains in efficiency and emissions reduction that could be achieved using a highly optimized system. Two generations of the Shell Starship powered by diesel engines operated on cross-country runs at near the maximum gross combination vehicle weight (GCVW) of 80,000 lb (36,300 kg). The first achieved just under 9 mpg with a freight efficiency of 173 ton-mpg, while the second achieved an even greater fuel-efficiency, of just under 11 mpg and a freight efficiency of 254 ton-mpg. These results are more than double the freight efficiency of the average Class 8 truck operating in North America.

Similar to the Shell Starship initiative, NACFE's Run on Less events demonstrate and document the achievements of fleets pushing the envelope of freight efficiency and carbon reduction. They focus on one or two trucks from multiple fleets delivering real freight over the course of two to three weeks while achieving meaningful emissions reductions. The most recent iteration was Run on Less Messy Middle (RoL-MM), which was held in September 2025.

"Messy Middle" is a term NACFE uses to describe a transitional phase in heavy truck powertrains between today's legacy diesel and an expected future powered by cost-effective renewable electricity. In this transitional period, various technologies are emerging, giving fleets multiple pathways to reduced emissions. RoL-MM featured 14 trucks in regional-haul and line-haul duty cycles operating with a variety of powertrains, including diesel, biodiesel, compressed natural gas (CNG), renewable natural gas (RNG), battery-electric, and fuel cell electric.

Diesel, having powered this industry for decades, is the well-established giant and therefore the baseline against which the cost, operational effectiveness, emissions, and efficiency of newer technologies are measured. RoL-MM included two trucks operating with conventional petroleum diesel, one truck which was frequently fueled with renewable diesel and one configured to operate on B99 biodiesel. The diesel trucks showed that conventional diesel and renewable diesel provide the greatest operational flexibility, as demonstrated by their routes and duty cycles, while achieving a combined 11 mpg over an 18-day period.

This impressive fuel efficiency is notable for two reasons. It is more than 50% higher than the average for diesel in the US, indicating that there is significant opportunity for the average diesel truck to improve using in-production technology and the same fuels that fleets use today. It is also a 9% improvement on what very high-efficiency trucks (two trucks with the same drivers) achieved in Run on Less in 2017, when the average was 10 mpg, indicating that the diesel benchmark continues to improve.

> "Diesel trucks provide the greatest operational flexibility while achieving 11 mpg over an 18-day period—more than 50% higher than the average for diesel in the U.S."

# Renewable natural gas

Diesel and natural gas have been used as transportation fuels in the US for several decades. More recently, alternative variations, such as RNG, have received more attention. RNG is a form of natural gas that is fully interchangeable with conventional natural gas but is generated from various feedstock sources, such as livestock manure, landfills, or wastewater. While it has been in use as a transportation fuel in niche applications for years, it is not well known as a fuel in typical freight-moving applications.

Depending on the feedstock source, using RNG as a fuel can result in a net-negative carbon intensity (CI),² removing more GHG from the atmosphere than is emitted over the fuel's lifecycle.³,⁴

## RNG in heavy-duty applications

In 2023, Shell unveiled our third-generation Starship. On the surface, Shell Starship 3.0 looks similar to the first two generations. However, it is now powered by the new Cummins X15N™ engine and fueled with RNG. In this configuration, it achieved 9 mpg (diesel gallon equivalent) and a freight efficiency of more than 200 ton-miles per gallon on demonstration runs in California, both very high benchmarks for trucks powered by natural gas.⁵ In 2025, Shell Starship 3.0 went on to operate on the INDYCAR circuit, hauling the Pennzoil Tech Inspection trailer over 4,800 miles to nine events and proving that natural gas can deliver meaningful efficiency benefits in demanding, real-world applications.

The Cummins X15N engine represents a significant step forward for heavy-duty natural gas engines. Reliability issues seen in earlier generations have largely been addressed, with extended spark-plug intervals, quieter operation, and diesel-like power and torque—making performance nearly indistinguishable from diesel while improving serviceability.

CNG and RNG also featured in RoL-MM. Three of the 14 trucks were powered by the same Cummins X15N engine used in Shell Starship 3.0. The CNG trucks operated on routes that allowed them to return to base every day, making fueling predictable. Most importantly, these trucks (one of which frequently ran on RNG) demonstrated that CNG trucks can handle demanding duty cycles. All three trucks frequently hauled multiple trailers, some with a GCVW of 140,000 lb (63,000 kg) over hilly terrain.

> "Shell Starship 3.0, fueled with RNG, achieved 9 mpg (diesel gallon equivalent) and a freight efficiency of more than 200 ton-miles per gallon."

## Protecting hotter-running natural gas engines

The demands that natural gas engines—whether running on RNG, CNG, or LNG—place on lubricants are different from those of diesel engines. They need low-ash formulations that protect components without creating deposits that can foul spark plugs or exhaust valves. Because natural gas burns hotter than diesel, oils must offer higher thermal and oxidation stability, with a greater focus on managing oxidation and nitration, rather than soot formation. Choosing the right oil is critical to realizing the benefits of gas engine operation.

Shell Rotella NG Plus 10W-30 is designed specifically for these conditions. It is approved for Cummins LNG/CNG engines and provides a low-ash formulation to protect exhaust catalysts and particulate filters. The oil has an optimized detergent–dispersant balance to keep engines clean and running efficiently, and is suitable for diesel engines to help simplify lubrication inventories for fleets operating both natural gas and diesel engine vehicles.

# NACFE measurement and verification framework

Over the years, NACFE has worked with multiple fleets and organizations, such as Shell, to measure and verify fuel and freight efficiency. On the surface, measuring freight efficiency appears to be a straightforward process. The quantity of fuel, the distance traveled, and the payload carried are all that are needed to calculate fuel and freight efficiency. Depending on the purpose of the measurement, this seemingly straightforward measurement can become complicated. SAE publishes several standards for how these measurements are carried out, depending on the level of precision needed and where the test is carried out. These standards help to control the external variables that affect fuel-efficiency measurement, such as road surface, grade, temperature, and wind. If the desire is to have a precise measurement under controlled conditions, such as when trying to understand the effect of a specific component, this may be the approach that is needed.

NACFE's framework for measuring freight efficiency does not extend to the level of a standard. It is as the word suggests: a framework for measuring overall system (e.g., whole-vehicle) efficiency over a longer duration in a real-world setting. The focus is on taking frequent measurements over longer durations with a primary and secondary measurement for fuel and distance.

For Shell Starship, during the cross-country runs and while it was in use for the INDYCAR series, an engine control module (ECM) generated fuel consumption, while GPS coordinates were read on an ongoing basis using commercial telematics. NACFE also had access to the vehicle's odometer data (also read from the ECM and accessed through the telematics device), and Shell provided copies of the fuel receipts as a secondary fuel measurement. This secondary measurement provides confidence that there is not a systemic flaw in the data from the primary measurement. It is not intended to catch or prevent deception. NACFE works cooperatively with the interested parties to provide a reliable measurement.

# Conclusion

In some respects, this report covers two technologies that are at nearly the opposite ends of the spectrum when it comes to cost, implementation difficulty, and emissions impact. Diesel technology has improved at a rapid rate in terms of efficiency and emissions. Fleets that are not yet optimizing the efficiency and emissions of their diesel equipment can take many steps to improve their freight efficiency. Some are low risk and may require little or no up-front investment, such as using low-viscosity lubricants. They provide a modest improvement in efficiency and help a fleet on its journey to lower fuel costs and emissions.

RNG is a solution that can reduce emissions compared with diesel and compressed natural gas and help a fleet toward its sustainability goals. This may necessitate a considerable investment of resources and capital, depending on the starting point for a fleet.

Complete zero-emissions across the transportation network is still some years away. The path to that future appears messy with many tradeoffs, some of which will impact a fleet for years to come. Under the right conditions and for the right applications, it is possible to bypass some of this messiness by transitioning directly to technologies not covered in this report, such as battery and hydrogen electric vehicles.

> The key technologies and practices being implemented by high-efficiency fleets to achieve best-in-class freight efficiency and lower-carbon performance are:
> - maintenance practices and data use that prioritize fuel efficiency
> - adoption of low-viscosity and FA-4 technologies
> - increasing use of alternative fuels, such as renewable natural gas.

> **Figure 3**
> The figure shows that FFS fleets with the highest adoption rates have higher average fuel efficiency compared to other fleets. Between 2007 and 2023, fleets with less than 30% adoption (bottom, red line) saw their fuel efficiency rise from just over 6 to 6.8 miles per gallon (mpg). In the same period, those with 30–39% adoption (grey, dashed line) saw their economy decrease between 2007 and 2011 from 6.9 to 6.3 mpg before steadily increasing to 7.5 mpg by 2023. The 40–49% adopters (middle, yellow line) had a 7 mpg fuel economy in 2011 that rose to 7.5% by 2023. The 50% and above group (top, dotted blue line) briefly increased from 7.8 in 2014 to 7.9 mpg in 2015, which decreased to 7.5 mpg by 2018 to increase again to 8.8 mpg by 2023.

> **Figure 6**
> The flowchart outlines a strategic framework for fleet managers navigating the transition to sustainable powertrains. It uses a prioritized "waterfall" approach to help fleets move toward zero-emissions goals while managing available technologies. The process begins with battery electric vehicles (BEV), identified as the simplest zero-emissions path. If BEVs fail to meet operational needs—specifically regarding three core criteria of financial benefit, sustainability, or availability—the user moves to hydrogen fuel cell vehicles (H₂FC), which may better suit long-haul applications. If zero-emissions solutions are unfeasible, the framework suggests transitional options: natural gas, renewables/hybrids, or hydrogen internal combustion (H₂ ICE). Each is evaluated against the same three core criteria. As a final resort, fleets may remain with diesel, focusing on maximizing efficiency until a future transition to zero-emissions equipment becomes viable.
