# 1 INTRODUCTION
One of the ways the North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) encourages the deployment of technologies that improve freight efficiency is through [Run on Less](https://runonless.com), a series of freight efficiency demonstrations. The first Run was held in 2017 and focused on long-haul trucking. Run on Less Regional took place in 2019 and centered on trucks in regional haul applications. In September 2021, NACFE undertook Run on Less – Electric (RoL-E) which looked at electric vehicles in four market segments:
* Terminal Tractors
* Vans and Step Vans
* Heavy-duty Regional Haul Tractors
* Medium-duty Box Trucks

Following RoL-E, NACFE published an initial report, [*Electric Trucks Have Arrived: Documenting A Real-World Trucking Demonstration*](https://runonless.com). In addition, NACFE determined that, as we go forward with information and findings relative to the electrified vehicle markets, we will issue information when appropriate by market segment.

The focus of this report — the fourth and final of the market segment reports — will be to describe the North American market of medium-duty box trucks, review the fleets that operated a medium-duty box truck during RoL-E and share specific findings for this market segment from RoL-E.

For NACFE’s first three market segment reports in this series, it is possible to purchase the complete vehicle in one move. A fleet can buy a terminal tractor, van or step van, or regional haul tractor from one company and receive the complete vehicle in a ready-to-use format.

The box van is one of the simplest possible applications for battery electric vehicle (BEV) use since many of them require no auxiliary power for the body. A very common accessory for a van body is a lift gate. Lift gates allow a delivery to be made at street level without a loading dock, functioning as an external “elevator” between the ground and the floor of the van body. They add a relatively minor load to the batteries. A similar application on the work truck side would be a simple stake bed, which is merely a flat surface with slats on the sides to hold in vegetation, tools or other items required by the team using the vehicle.

The applications in the “limited body duty cycle column” of Figure 3 have a body that will require a higher level of power draw. For example, the dump truck, scissor lift and roll-off will all have a motor- or cylinder-driven mechanism to change the position of the truck body. Other applications may require the use of winches (roll-off, service body, etc.), elevated buckets or pumps to fill or empty a tanker.

The applications in the “significant body duty cycle” of Figure 3 require high levels of power to complete their tasks. Some will have continuous loads such as the pump on a fire truck, the temperature refrigeration unit on a frozen food truck or the barrel on a concrete mixer. Others, such as a garbage trucks, will have frequent need for large power draws to compact the trash into the body. Lion Electric and Oshkosh have made strides in electrifying several of these applications including garbage trucks and concrete mixers respectively.

The “Goldilocks” principle enters into freight logistics and makes the medium-duty box truck the best solution in many cases. A van or step van may be too small or too awkward to load a refrigerator freezer, a sectional sofa, or a pallet of heavy frozen or canned foods. A tractor-trailer even with the smallest trailer can be a difficult use for delivery into tight urban store fronts or suburban cul-de-sacs that have parked cars and children playing in the streets. The box truck, especially with a lift gate, becomes the most versatile “just right” tool for freight delivery. It can do what the tractor-trailer can do but it likely will require multiple trips depending on the size of the freight. It can transport anything that a van or step van can carry but is more difficult for a delivery person to get into and out of.

During the holiday season, you can spot medium-duty box trucks meeting delivery vans in neighborhoods to eliminate the vans having to return to the distribution center to reload. The box truck is loaded at the delivery center and meets the delivery van out on its route to save

<!-- page: 23 -->
the time it would take to get across town for a new load. This is a case of both vehicles performing the tasks they are best designed to complete.

Another benefit to operating many medium-duty box trucks is the driver is not required to have a commercial driver’s license (CDL) since the vehicles do not exceed the 26,000 lbs. weight limit. For fleets, this opens up driving positions to any licensed driver. While increased battery pack weight can result in transitioning to a higher vehicle class i.e., moving from Class 3 to Class 4, it does not result in the driver having or getting a CDL. This is a huge benefit today as the driver shortage continues for jobs requiring a CDL.

## 2.1 BATTERY INTEGRATION
For battery electric trucks, all power requirements must be served using batteries. But for trucks with many power requirements, specifically those for the truck and the body, there are two schools of thought on the number of batteries needed. In this early stage of BEV development there are two prevailing theories of battery use:
1. Integrate everything to draw from one large battery, or
2. Separate the truck’s traction motor battery from the truck’s body battery.

The benefit to an integrated system is that there is only one battery on the truck and therefore only one charger to connect for recharging the battery. The challenge of this integration is that the truck body can draw the battery down below the charge level required to drive the truck back to the fleet’s depot.

Using separate batteries between the truck and the body allows the truck manufacturer to know exactly what the battery draw of the truck will be since the body accessories are isolated from the main traction system. The drawbacks include the likelihood of having two charging systems to manage.

Both of these solutions may have benefits and challenges to battery life; however, it is difficult to predict what they might be at this very nascent stage in the development of electric trucks with body loads.

<!-- page: 24 -->
It is reasonable to expect continuous developments in system integration as battery electric trucks mature and advance. Gordon Wolverton, director, zero-emissions powertrains, Navistar, stated at the ACT Expo 2022 panel *ePTO Development Standards and Applications* that the company has an interface box developed to handle high voltage DC power with a J1939 high speed serial data link interface.

“The industry needs to grow in the ePTO space,” said Bruce Vasbinder, director, medium-duty segment marketing, Navistar. “That has to be a scalable solution in various kilowatt ratings to be able to grow into the vocational workspace.”

## 2.2 TRAINING
Given the addition of the truck body and its controls, the training efforts to support this segment are going to be considerably higher than the other RoL-E market segments. If the body is a simple one with no control features, the training may be limited to general safety precautions that would be similar to a dealership’s training effort. However, if the body features interface to the high-power batteries and complicated electronic controls, the challenge could easily become one of the highest levels of training seen in this portion of the industry to date.

Over the past two decades, body integrators have become trained in and familiar with multiplexed body controls to allow additional safety interlocks and increased functionality. Now designing power interfaces and properly manufacturing, testing and operating such features will require considerable training as well as enhanced communications with the vehicle OEM.

The OEMs will need to learn more from the body builders as well. Drawing power from a mechanical PTO didn’t require the body builder to provide operational information to the truck OEM. With battery electric trucks and ePTOs, OEMs will need to understand the power being drawn away from their main batteries if the body is an integrated one-battery system. In that case, operational data from a completed vehicle will need to be studied to fully understand the capabilities of the body and the range of the truck.

Training needs also will extend beyond the people directly involved with the battery electric trucks. Emergency responders already are receiving training handling accidents with battery electric cars and trucks, but ongoing training will be needed. During the Chicago Area Clean Cities Green Drives 2022 show, a class of emergency responders was allowed access to the array of battery electric trucks during the seminar portion of the day. Emergency responders

<!-- page: 25 -->
also will need to learn about the addition of a second rechargeable battery package on some units. They already may be trained on how to find a battery disconnect for the main drive batteries, but they also will need to know where the additional battery for the body is located and how to disconnect it as well.

## 2.3 CAB CONFIGURATION
One of the long-time basic choices for box trucks is the configuration of the cab. If the truck has the cab behind the hood, the truck is referred to as a “conventional” truck. If there is no hood and the windshield is above the front bumper, the truck is referred to either as a low front entry (LFE) or as a cab over. See Figure 5. Cab over engine (COE) is another common name for the latter, but with the battery electric truck no longer having an engine, this term has a limited lifetime in the industry. In the past, the choice of cab determined whether accessing basic fluids in the engine compartment required opening the hood on a conventional-style truck or tilting the cab forward on an LFE. Given that there won’t be as much fluid level checking with battery electric trucks, it will be interesting to observe how this trend changes in coming years.

## 2.6 THE PATH TO ELECTRIFICATION
The electrification of medium-duty box trucks can take several different paths. First is the traditional production line vehicle that is built from the ground up, like the Freightliner eM2 or the International eMV shown in Figure 15.

Second is upfitting, which allows a fleet to electrify a new vehicle that would otherwise be powered by a gasoline or diesel engine. The process begins once a traditional production line vehicle is purchased and sent to an upfitter for electrification. For example, a fleet will purchase a gasoline-powered Ford F-450 or F-650 and send it to an upfitter such as Lightning eMotors, Phoenix Motorcars, SEA Electric or ROUSH CleanTech. The upfit team will then convert the truck by removing the internal combustion engine, exhaust system, cooling system and other components no longer required and installing the battery electric drive system, inverters and battery packs. This process can be far more efficient when the upfitter can purchase a glider kit, which is a new truck without any powertrain.

The third option is remanufacturing (frequently shortened to "reman”) or repowering an existing vehicle that is already in operation. This follows the same process as upfitting, except the vehicle is not new. Whether fleets choose to electrify existing equipment or purchase equipment on the secondary market, remanufacturing allows them to upgrade their vehicles without the additional cost of purchasing a new vehicle.

Medium-duty box trucks make home deliveries, just as vans and step vans do, but usually for larger goods or for larger quantities of product. For example, durable goods such as appliances, furniture, and large sporting goods are delivered in box trucks. New home and apartment construction sites are very common locations for deliveries via medium-duty box trucks. Cabinets, countertops, flooring, plumbing and much more are delivered in box trucks. Organizations such as ACT Research and FTR commonly point to new housing starts as a measure of demand for medium-duty trucks. Since the end of the Great Recession in 2009, new housing starts have risen steadily as shown in Figure 16 with thousands of new housing starts in the United States per month. [22]

# 3 DUTY CYCLE CONSIDERATIONS
Operationally, medium-duty box trucks are used in return-to-base duty cycles, for urban and last mile deliveries. Their service territory is usually within a 50-mile radius of their home base or depot, and they travel less than 100 miles per day. [2] See Figure 17. While these vehicles operate in a low-mileage duty cycle, they do have the capability of running between 300 and 400 miles on a single tank of gasoline or diesel fuel over several days. Therefore, a single truck might travel less than 100 miles for most days of the week but be used for a longer run on any one day. This creates challenges for the range-constrained battery electric truck.

Medium-duty box trucks do not typically run multiple shifts, but they may return to base for reloading during normal daily operations. Many use cases in this market also can be described as having a diminishing load duty cycle, which is a route where the truck leaves the base fully loaded with goods and stops at various locations making deliveries along the way, and then returns to base empty. Good examples include the shipments of restaurant supplies or furniture delivery.

In some cases, however, these delivery vehicles are not going back to their home base empty. Instead, they stop at smaller hubs, stores or drop off locations, to pick up outgoing packages or other freight that will be sorted at a larger facility for shipment to their final destinations. Customer demand for fast and cheap shipping has had a bullwhip effect on distribution networks, the results of which are these micro supply chains. To facilitate the need for speed, companies are adding more regional distribution hubs and fulfillment centers, which has shrunk the average length of haul from 800 miles to 500 miles per trip. This in turn has spawned an increase of shorter pickup-and-delivery jobs within the transportation industry [11], especially for last mile deliveries. The growth of last mile deliveries for items such as appliances and furniture has had a profound impact on the electrification potential of medium-duty box trucks.

Collectively, the duty cycles and use cases of food and furniture delivery, and vocational pickups and deliveries align well to the sweet spot of the current battery technology. And while there are some load types that can add an additional layer of complexity, for example, refrigeration, the challenge is manageable in many cases.

# 4 CHARGING CONSIDERATIONS
Generally speaking, when it comes to charging speed, there are three types of Electric Vehicle Supply Equipment, commonly referred to as EVSEs: Level 1, Level 2, and DC Fast Charging. For a more complete discussion of charging see NACFE’s Guidance Report, [*Amping Up: Charging Infrastructure for Electric Trucks*](https://runonless.com).

With medium-duty box trucks, fast charging is not a requirement that is discussed frequently. The fleets in this market segment that participated in RoL-E utilized maximum charging rates of 19.2 kW up to 125 kW. Operationally these are slow charges compared to the megawatt options in development for larger Class 8 tractors.

One recently published example shown in Figure 18 is a 28’ box by Lightning eMotors on a GM 6500 LFE chassis. The GVWR is 25,950 lbs. with 13,650 lbs. of freight and passenger weight capability. [23]

Operationally, slow charging takes place over an 8-to-10-hour time span which easily satisfies the requirements needed for a single-shift operation. For example, a 70-kWh battery pack at 50% state of charge (SOC) requires only a 35-kWh recharge over that 10-hour shift, a minimal average rate of 3.5 kW per hour. This is equivalent to the load from several hair dryers running continuously. Therefore, introducing medium-duty box trucks to a new or existing facility does not automatically require infrastructure upgrades and/or cause concern about an impact on the grid. Still, fleets should engage their utility provider before, during and after their charging design and build-out.

Depending on the vehicle manufacturer, the charging port on the vehicle may be in the front, or either side of the truck, which would require the driver to park a specific way to be able to connect to the charger. Another consideration would be to add opportunity charging — sometimes called en route charging — to increase the range of these vehicles. An example of that would be to add charging to the loading docks or in specific dock doors, so a vehicle could charge during the loading and unloading process.

Thoughtful parking lot design for public charging also can reduce the total number of chargers needed, as well as eliminate the need to shuffle vehicles around to ensure they all have adequate access to the chargers.

Unlike vans and step vans, medium-duty box trucks are larger vehicles that do not fit into passenger car parking stalls, so they most likely will be unable to use public charging that is being built specifically for cars. Even if it was possible, the size of the truck likely prevents it

# 5 BENEFITS OF BATTERY ELECTRIC MEDIUM-DUTY BOX TRUCKS
After five reports on electric trucks coming out of RoL-E, it is not surprising that we have discussed all possible benefits for these return-to-base trucks, either in general or specific to use case.

Here are some key benefits of battery electric medium-duty box trucks over their diesel- and gasoline-powered counterparts. However, the ability of BEVs to operate emissions-free in an urban environment and their lower noise when operating could be the most compelling benefits.

## 5.1 FEWER MOVING PARTS EQUALS LOWER MAINTENANCE COSTS
As fleets begin to integrate BEVs into their operations, a common theme when discussing total cost of operation (TCO) benefits has been the estimated maintenance savings. While there still will be routine preventive maintenance needed on BEVs, like changing tires, checking fluids, filters, hoses etc., the fact remains that fewer moving parts should equate to lower maintenance costs. For example, the heavy stop-and-go drive cycles experienced by delivery vehicles, especially in urban areas, put tremendous wear and tear on transmissions and other mechanical components on the vehicle; these components are not found on BEVs.

When considering the maintenance benefits, it is important to acknowledge that fleets should expect some near-term delays as BEV manufacturers work to build their service and support networks and train technicians, which will have an impact on uptime or downtime.

## 5.2 HEALTH IMPACTS
The positive impact of BEVs on drivers still is considered a soft cost, when calculating TCO. However, from a quality-of-life perspective, the benefits are tangible and will undoubtedly have real dollars associated with them in the not-too-distant future. Drivers across the board report they are less fatigued when driving BEVs. Because there is no longer an engine, BEVs are much quieter and have less vibration, both of which reduce driver fatigue. Additionally, drivers no longer must deal with heat coming from the engine, which also contributes to driver fatigue. Medium- and heavy-duty electric vehicles even can be operated safely inside of warehouses with no fumes, allowing loading and unloading to occur in air-conditioned or heated spaces.

Day & Ross’ driver, Francis Lajoie, said, “I’ve been a truck driver at Day & Ross for 28 years. The biggest difference is sound — or actually the lack of it. The truck is comfortable, the design is outstanding, and the visibility is great.” He also commented on the reliability, the efficiency of

<!-- page: 39 -->
the acceleration, the handling, the braking, and the overall performance. “After only two days 
on the road, I felt like I’ve been driving it for 10 years. I am pleasantly surprised — it’s a 
beautiful machine.” Lajoie went further, stating, “It’s not often you can combine work with 
having fun, but when I’m driving this truck, I do.” [11] To learn more about Lajoie’s driving 
experience, watch this video. 

From an environmental standpoint, medium-duty box trucks have a positive impact on the 
communities they serve by reducing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions in urban areas and 
neighborhoods. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) reports the health effects of air 
toxins in urban areas is higher because of population density and a higher concentration of 
emission sources, which includes both mobile and stationary sources. [24] 

## 5.3 NOISE REDUCTION

Less noise is not only a benefit to the driver, but it is also a benefit to the community. It is not 
uncommon to have noise ordinances or restrictions for commercial deliveries during the 
overnight hours. These restrictions are typically put in place to protect neighborhoods that are 
adjacent to commercial zones. Electric vehicles in general create an opportunity to make 
deliveries during these restricted times because they won’t disturb the neighbors. In a 
residential neighborhood, electric medium-duty box trucks also may open the door to early 
morning package and parcel deliveries. 

It should be noted however, that while these vehicles are significantly quieter than their 
conventional counterparts, they are not completely silent. There is still noise associated with 
back-up alarms, rolling doors, etc.

### 6.3 TRANSITIONING DEPOTS

Warehouses and depots typically only have a finite amount of space to work with. As a result, 
transitioning a location to a BEV hub can be very difficult, with timelines encompassing design, 
permitting, construction and installation. Added expense can be incurred if additional 
transformers are needed to support the new power requirements or if conduit must be run to 
more optimal locations at the site. 

Furthermore, for those sites that are leased, all the design, upgrades and installation work also 
must be approved and likely paid for by the landlord, which may entail additional cost for the 
fleet. Lead time for these large projects can easily last more than a year or even two, especially 
if infrastructure upgrades are required by the utility.

While preplanning is a huge benefit for new construction sites, NACFE has found several key 
factors can impact upgrading existing facilities. Playing a significant role is the age of the 
building, as relatively low impact upgrades can result in cascading changes that need to be 
addressed across the entire site. Additionally, previous retrofits to lighting and HVAC systems 
also can increase the complexity as well as the quantity of new Level 2 chargers necessary for 
the initial implementation and future growth projections.

### 6.4 MULTIPLE BATTERY SYSTEMS AND CHARGING

If the truck has separate battery systems for the truck powertrain and the truck body system, it 
is possible that the two systems will need separate charging cables. This adds complexity to the 
layout of the facility, charging systems and even the driver’s daily duties. See Section 2.1 of this 
report for more information on battery integration.

Vehicle performance is always in context of the operating environment. For RoL-E, NACFE 
pursued regions with diverse weather conditions. The local weather data for each day and fleet 
location were obtained from OpenWeather. [4]

NACFE reported distance traveled on the RoL-E metrics dashboard. First, total miles for the day 
were tabulated and reported as a number, then distances were graphed versus time along with 
battery state of charge (SOC). 

NACFE reported speeds on the RoL-E metrics dashboard. First, speeds along with SOC were 
graphed versus time, and then speed was categorized as percent distance in speed bands. 

SOC is like the diesel fuel gauge. It is just as problematic and challenging. Battery SOC for RoL-E 
is a measurement made by the vehicle by continuously monitoring voltage, amperage, and amp 
hours remaining after fully charging — essentially counting the amp-hours expended over time 
and subtracting that from the full charge state. 

Opportunity charging, charging midway during a work shift in between driving, for example 
over lunch breaks, can extend the daily range of a battery electric truck. The only market 
segment in RoL-E that saw opportunity charging successfully used was terminal tractors, but it 
was not yet in significant use with medium-duty box trucks. DCFC charging and MCS charging at 
public sites is some time in the future once higher volumes of BEVs are operating in a region. 

Battery electric vehicles generally can recover energy by using the drive motors to slow the 
vehicles. This is called regenerative braking. Essentially the motors rather than using energy are 
acting as generators and putting energy back into the batteries. 

Deliveries, the act of stopping the vehicle and unloading (or loading), are metrics NACFE 
captured for RoL-E. For medium-duty box trucks, as well as vans and step vans and heavy-duty 
tractors, NACFE used one algorithm to estimate if a delivery was made in order not to mistake a 
stop at a traffic light as a delivery. 

For RoL-E, NACFE described the charging rate as how fast and at what power level. Charging 
rate was determined from vehicle data bus signals using SOC over the time when the vehicle 
was plugged into the charger. The vehicle data bus continuously reported voltage and 
amperage, and integrated that over time to provide SOC.

Consumption is the inverse of efficiency. Diesel vehicles report fuel efficiency as miles traveled 
per gallon of fuel expended. Consumption for them is then gallons of fuel expended per mile 
traveled. In battery electric vehicles, the metric often reported is kilowatt hours expended per 
mile or kWh/mi. This is not efficiency but rather consumption. 

For RoL-E, trucking activity was categorized as: inactive, idling, charging and driving. These 
terms can mean different things for BEVs than for diesel-powered vehicles.

that is going out and the amount of energy coming in. This occurs at the same time, for 
example, a BEV exiting from an Interstate engages regenerative braking by removing pressure 
on the accelerator pedal to slow the vehicle.

Today’s battery electric medium-duty box trucks in production and operational across the US 
and Canada are giving fleets more real-world data to rely on when making TCO decisions for 
their businesses. In fact, many key stakeholders in the BEV space have developed TCO 
calculators to help fleets assess if transitioning to BEVs makes financial sense. These calculators 
vary in complexity and scope, so care must be taken in understanding both the inputs and 
outputs. 

Each fleet’s own specific duty cycles, drivers, routes, vehicles, weather, traffic, etc. all 
contribute to unique experiences. There are no average trucks, so while these TCO calculators 
can help understand how various factors impact TCO, each fleet’s own experience is critical. 
Therefore it’s important for fleets to engage the technology first-hand. 

In the past, TCO was focused on fairly easy to track costs such as purchase price, maintenance 
costs, residual value, etc. The true TCO however is all inclusive and fleets are finding there are 
many factors to consider. Argonne National Laboratory issued an excellent guide on these TCO 
factors titled *Comprehensive Total Cost of Ownership Quantification for Vehicles with Different 
Size Classes and Powertrains*. [29] 

A new category of TCO cost offsets is low carbon fuel standard (LCFS) credits [3]. Fleets that 
control their own fueling are able to accrue annual credits for using low carbon energy in place 
of traditional diesel or gasoline. These credits can be sold to companies that need them to 
offset carbon producing operations such as oil refineries. This represents a positive cash flow 
stream for fleets operating in a growing number of regions like California, Oregon, Washington 
and British Columbia. A more detailed look at LCFS credits can be found in the NACFE report 
*Electric Trucks Have Arrived: The Use Case for Heavy-Duty Regional Haul Tractors*. [30]

## 10 FINDINGS
The study team has five key findings based on the three battery electric medium-duty box 
trucks that participated in RoL-E and based on other available resources.

* **Medium-duty box trucks are a great application for electric trucks given their short 
distances and return-to-base operations.** The vast majority of medium-duty box trucks 
are not driven long distances and are home very night. They are an ideal portion of the 
overall medium-duty truck market for electrification. Other medium-duty applications 

---
Electric Trucks Have Arrived: The Use Case for Medium-Duty Box Trucks
June 28, 2022 70

<!-- page: 71 -->
that do not require much battery power to operate aspects of the body also can be 
good candidates for electrification.

* **Medium-duty trucks almost always have a second manufacturer who adds body 
devices requiring engineering design and validation as well as manufacturing planning 
for electrification.** Truck equipment manufacturers (TEMs), also known as body 
builders, will be adding a wide variety of bodies to medium-duty chassis. While a simple 
van body doesn’t require a lot of additional engineering or validation, many other 
applications will be much more challenging in terms of installation, operations, 
safeguards and more. TEMs will need significant training and support to know what they 
can change, move and integrate with during installations. TEMs know how to modify 
current diesel trucks, for example, moving fuel tanks, but dealing with high voltage 
batteries will be a new, difficult challenge.

* **More complex Class 6 and 7 trucks such as snowplows, refuse trucks, and fire trucks 
will require significant efforts which will delay electrification timing.** Trucks that see 
continuous operations for a day or more, such as a snowplow or utility truck, will be 
obvious challenges for battery electric powertrains. This isn’t to say that some 
prototypes will be developed and shown, but they are less likely to be seen in significant 
volumes in the near future. The industry will be generating a great deal of new data that 
will help OEMs, fleets and battery producers develop and integrate more sophisticated 
interfaces and vehicles.

* **Application expansion into more complex medium-duty trucks will occur as 
knowledge is gained.** Tackling relatively simple and straightforward applications such as 
box trucks provides the trucking industry with a launching point to design, build, 
validate and refine battery electric trucks. The industry will learn things such as how 
much of the battery’s power is consumed by various cab air conditioning and heating 
demands. It also will learn about how lift gates and other peripheral devices tax power 
consumption. This knowledge can be carried over to more difficult applications, 
providing a “known step” as the industry works through the issues in other more 
complex applications.

* **Other aspects such as driver attraction and retention benefits, maintenance savings 
and infrastructure challenges exist as they have for other market segment use cases.** 
As described in the three previous RoL-E market segment reports, battery electric trucks 
will offer both benefits and challenges. Finding grants and incentives to make the 
vehicles more affordable is not easy and available funds disappear quickly when they 
become available. Charging infrastructure presents an entirely new set of challenges for 
fleets from parking lot planning, to building permits, inspection approvals and 
operations. What has been very positive is the response of the drivers that have

find attractive at a time when drivers are hard to both find and keep; these differences 
can be valuable.
