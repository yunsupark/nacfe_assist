SuperTruck 2: Empowering Future Trucks

1 INTRODUCTION

In 2016, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) inspired American truck manufacturers through funding the
SuperTruck 2 program to demonstrate a system of new technologies with the primary benefits of improving freight
energy use efficiency, reducing vehicle operating costs and improving environmental sustainability. The program
built off the SuperTruck 1 program successes. The opportunity to harness the creativity of OEMs and suppliers to
push the envelope of vehicle design was embraced by five teams lead by:

• Cummins Inc. & Peterbilt
• Daimler Truck North America (DTNA)
• Volvo Trucks
• Navistar
• PACCAR Inc.

Over the period 2016 through 2024, each of these five teams succeeded in innovating complete tractor-trailer
systems capable of exceeding freight efficiency performance improvements in excess of 100% versus each team's
Model Year 2009 baseline diesel tractors with standard 53' dry van box trailers of each team's choosing.

These accomplishments created a watershed of practical engineering and operational information to assist in
developing production tractors and trailers to meet increasingly demanding performance needs of the North
American freight industry.

These successes were in no small part due to the ingenuity and persistence of each of the team members as these
programs were accomplished amid the complications and ramifications of the COVID pandemic and related supply
chain challenges.

The fundamental basis of these SuperTruck 2 demonstrations is that a systems engineering perspective,
encompassing the entire tractor-trailer as a single performing unit, can yield significantly better performance
improvement than more piecemeal approaches looking at optimizing individual systems.

SuperTruck 2 was not a competition between teams, rather each team was competing against itself to
demonstrate significant performance gains versus their own production baselines and their own defined duty
cycles. As such, each team had their own specific goals, objectives and timelines.

Funding for each team included both federal monies and each team had substantial matching funds, what is often
termed "skin in the game" or more precisely, a public-private partnership.

This program was expertly managed at the DOE by the Vehicles Technology Office (VTO), with progress publicly
documented and peer reviewed through the DOE Annual Merit Reviews (AMR). Links to these are found in Section
15.

The North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) participated in all AMRs as official peer reviewers as
well as hosting multiple discussions on SuperTruck 2 technologies and writing thought leadership pieces in industry
trade magazines. The following report documents the significant technologies investigated and demonstrated by
the SuperTruck 2 teams, and the potential for these technologies to influence future production truck designs.

2 SCOPE

This report focuses on an overview of the technologies investigated and demonstrated in the DOE SuperTruck 2
program by the five teams, and the potential for those technologies to be applied to future production vehicles.

5 TECHNOLOGY DEMONSTRATIONS

What's so super about SuperTruck? That was the question asked in a NACFE thought leadership piece published in
the Commercial Carrier Journal in July 2023 [1].

Truck manufacturers and suppliers struggle to decide which technologies to place into production. There are a lot
of complex questions, interrelated factors and costs to consider. Neither are there "sure-things" as any new
technology can be considered a gamble. Time is also a consideration, with new models often requiring five or more
years of development and testing before market introduction. Optional technologies that can be inserted in mid-
life truck updates still require time and effort to certify they are ready for customers [2]. Development also can be
expensive, with new model programs costing upwards of $1 billion.

Very rarely do manufacturers get to put the combined talents of their own staffs and select suppliers into a
completely new vehicle, where the primary focus is to demonstrate what technology could do. OEMs generally
either produce trucks or they produce trailers. Until just recently, there were no truck OEMs with feet in both the
tractor and trailer pools. This has meant that technology demonstrators never optimized the tractor-trailer as a
complete single performing system.

Then amid the Great Recession in 2008, the White House and DOE embarked on funding just such a truck-trailer
technology demonstration program. The official funding opportunity had the lengthy title American Recovery and
Reinvestment Act (Recovery Act): Systems Level Technology Development, Integration and Demonstration for
Efficient Class 8 Trucks (SuperTruck) and Advanced Technology Powertrains for Light-Duty Vehicles – DE-FOA-
0000079 [8]. The program was simply called SuperTruck.

Funding and awardees were announced in 2009 with four teams being selected to pursue the project. There were
two key elements for ensuring the success of the projects. One was a requirement to share costs, where each team
would provide 50% of the funds and the government would provide the other 50%. The other key element was
that each team had responsibility for the performance of the entire tractor-trailer as a complete system,
unshackling the typical piece-work approach to technology adoption. This freedom let weight increases from
adding technology in one part of the truck or the trailer to be offset with weight savings attained by a different
part of the vehicle. It allowed optimization of the aerodynamics and rolling resistance of the vehicle recognizing
the interdependence of all of the vehicle on these factors.

Teams had to develop their own production baselines based on real-world fleet vehicles. They had to develop test
protocols that represented real-world operations. The metric of merit was determined to be freight ton efficiency

SuperTruck was such a success that the White House and the DOE initiated a SuperTruck 2 program in 2016
through its VTO under the Office of Energy Efficiency and Renewable Energy (EERE) with an initial funding of $80
million [7][9]. This funding opportunity carried the title Advanced Systems Level Technology Development,
Integration and Demonstration for Efficient Class 8 Trucks (SuperTruck 2) - DE-FOA-0001447 [9]. The program was
simply referred to as SuperTruck 2 and the original program then became SuperTruck 1.

The SuperTruck 2 program again required that OEMs' "cost share must be at least 50% of the total allowable costs
for demonstration projects [9]." The period of performance was to be 48 to 60 months, with extensions authorized
later due to challenges from the COVID pandemic.

Initially four OEM teams were awarded SuperTruck 2 contracts. The PACCAR team was added after the initial DOE
award announcements. The five teams selected are:

• Cummins Inc. & Peterbilt
• Daimler Truck North America
• Volvo Trucks
• Navistar
• PACCAR Inc.

Where SuperTruck 1's primary goal was to achieve in excess of a 50% freight ton mile per gallon, SuperTruck 2 had
more aggressive goals. As the DOE Notice of Intent states [10]:

Each team created their own targets for their vehicles against their own baselines. The teams were not competing
against each other but against themselves to push the technological envelope. This is why it is inappropriate to
make comparisons between the teams; it's apples and oranges and neither accurate nor relevant to make such
comparisons.

The intent of this report is to highlight the innovative technologies developed and demonstrated by these five
teams, and how they may benefit future production freight vehicles. Many of the concepts are universally
applicable to new vehicles, irrespective of future powertrain choices. Others are specific to internal combustion
engines.

6 ABOUT BASELINES

Sage advice in engineering is that you must measure something to be able to improve it. The starting point for
SuperTruck projects was identifying a baseline for comparison. That comparison point is locked in time, but the
real world is constantly evolving. Understanding both the baseline and the real-world moving target for
comparison is needed in judging progress of the SuperTruck technologies and their impact on real-world vehicles.

Trucks built in 2008 are generally referred to in the industry as Model Year (MY) 2009 vehicles due to the way
OEMs and the government designate model years. Figure 2 is a sampling of recent images of trucks from this era
from five manufacturers. These images are used trucks for sale as of December 2023, many with more than a
million miles on them, reflecting the longevity of these types of vehicles which may live more than 20 years in
commercial service in gradually lower mileage duty cycles as maintenance costs increase with age. Baselines
selected by the SuperTruck 2 teams were the same as those chosen for SuperTruck 1. These types of vehicles
reflected models sold in higher volumes by each OEM. Each OEM was then basing performance improvement
against themselves, rather than their competitors.

Market penetration for new technologies can take years to reach high percentages. The time span from idea to
production adoption has been expedited through the SuperTruck 1 and 2 demonstrations. While not all
technologies have made it into production, many have influenced new models introduced by OEMs and purchased
by fleets in large numbers. Many ask "when will we see a SuperTruck in production?" The reality is fleets release
them one feature at a time.

Commercial vehicle research and development is complex, often with individual ideas sparking a variety of
unexpected innovations, often called "cross pollination" of ideas. The SuperTruck 1 and 2 projects provided a
fertile environment for that cross pollination to occur by defining the vehicle as a tractor and trailer system whose
net performance potential is due to physical interactions of both the tractor and trailer moving as a unit down the
road.

7 ABOUT DRIVERS

Trucking technology does not exist in the absence of humans. The truck driver is just as much a part of the tractor-
trailer system as are the engine, tires, cab, trailer, etc. NACFE has reported on studies in its report Determining
Efficiency that indicate the driver can impact net fuel economy by more than 30% [21]. Clearly a truck can be
driven with a focus on fuel economy as demonstrated by the drivers in NACFE's Run on Less 2017 and Run on Less
Regional 2019 demonstrations. But it can also be driven poorly, without attention to fuel economy.

The importance of drivers was repeatedly demonstrated in SuperTruck testing programs as the programs had a
clear objective of driving the technologies well. JB Hunt released the 2015 study, 660 Minutes, How Improving
Driver Efficiency Increases Capacity, highlighting that the total freight delivery system must also include the
driver's actual time available rolling versus being parked [38]. That study showed that in the study's population,
driver's averaged only 6.5 hours rolling in their 14-hour workday as shown in Figure 8. Helping the driver improve
performance and automating aspects of safe and efficient driving are some of the technologies demonstrated in
SuperTruck programs.

8 WHERE DOES IMPROVEMENT COME FROM?

engine on an engine test stand, the second was to demonstrate a complete tractor-trailer vehicle system that
exceeded a 100% improvement in freight-ton-efficiency (FTE) versus their selected MY2009 baseline vehicle with a
gross vehicle weight of 65,000 lbs. in a representative on-highway long-haul duty cycle of their own choosing.
Some teams chose to integrate the 55% BTE engine into their tractor-trailer vehicle demonstrators and others
chose to view these two goals as separate objectives.

A consistent finding with all five SuperTruck 2 teams is that fuel economy performance improvement requires
optimizing both the tractor and trailer as a complete vehicle. From an engineering and physics perspective,
improvement is a symbiosis of all the tractor-trailer elements, making the whole greater than the sum of its parts.
For example, aerodynamics of the tractor influence the aerodynamic performance of the trailer, but also the trailer
influences the tractor performance. This is easily demonstrated by running a tractor with a flatbed trailer versus a
van trailer. Similarly adding technology generally means adding tare weight to the vehicle, requiring other
technologies to shed weight so as not to impact the permissible freight weight.

The multiple paths to the target demonstrate that there are many ways to improve the net performance of
tractor-trailers, but from a physics standpoint, there are some priority technologies that achieve greater gains,
irrespective of the chosen power systems. Those are:

• Aerodynamic improvement
• Rolling resistance improvement
• Weight reduction
• Parasitic loss reduction

These four common improvement factors are very pertinent to evolving technologies including battery electric
vehicles (BEVs), fuel cell electric vehicles (FCEVs), alternative fuel vehicles such as renewable natural gas (RNG),
compressed natural gas (CNG), hydrogen internal combustion engine (H2 ICE), propane, and all the hybrid
permutations of these zero- and near-zero emission technologies. SuperTruck 2 investigations essentially are
breeding grounds to propagate innovation well beyond just the subject vehicles. Using these technologies to
improve the efficiency of Zero Emission Vehicles (ZEVs) will be discussed more later in this report.

SuperTruck 2 engine technology improvements both on the test stand achieving 55% BTE and in the vehicle
demonstrators, help quantify diesel engine technology possibilities as the industry begins to transition toward
lower emission vehicles. SuperTruck 2 helped clarify technical and commercial realism in internal combustion
diesel improvement in the 2020 decade.

9 SUPERTRUCK 2 TECHNOLOGIES

A range of technologies were evaluated in SuperTruck 2. The nature of a "systems perspective" is that these
technologies are not operating independent of each other, but actually coexist and likely influence each other, i.e.
the whole is more than the sum of its parts. The following sections discuss these different technologies.

9.1 AERODYNAMICS

A significant portion of energy is expended by trucks to counteract aerodynamic drag at highway speeds. In the
1990s, more than 50% of the engine's power was consumed fighting aerodynamic drag. Over the last two decades,
OEMs have produced ever more improved aerodynamic tractors and trailers. Where the drag coefficient (Cd) of a
1990's truck might have been 0.7 and 0.8, current aerodynamic production trucks are in the 0.3 to 0.5 Cd. The
MY2009 baselines for SuperTruck 2 likely had Cds in the 0.6 range. The SuperTruck 2 trucks and trailers have Cds in
the 0.3 to 0.4 range. There is a practical lower limit on drag coefficients due to the volumetric demands of hauling
freight in a 53' dry van trailer, and mating with all the warehouse, factory and storage infrastructure relevant to
shipping docks.

SuperTruck 2 teams all used advanced aerodynamic tools such as wind tunnel and computation fluid
dynamics to model complete tractor-trailer vehicle systems. They identified optimum aerodynamic forms

9.1.1 Trailer Aerodynamics

Freight is still moved on simple wooden pallets and those pallets come in specific sizes and are moved by forklifts
designed for those loads. SuperTruck 2 teams focused on the tractor-trailer system moving on the road, rather
than recommending changes in freight infrastructure. The 53' dry van trailer is typically 8' wide and just over 13'
tall at its front. That geometry is ubiquitous with millions of existing trailers in North American freight operations
as detailed in NACFE's report Intentional Pairing.

The challenge for SuperTruck 2 teams was to maintain the volume and similar shape of the freight storage space of
the trailer, while improving the overall aerodynamics of the tractor-trailer as a single system. Baseline trailers
essentially had no efficiency improvements, no aerodynamics, high rolling resistance dual tires on steel wheels,
with a focus on long-term durability and low cost rather than aerodynamic or weight efficiency.

Another challenge for the SuperTruck 2 teams is that the manufacturing segment is bifurcated with tractor
manufacturers different than the trailer manufacturers. Until recently, no Class 8 tractor OEM was tied to a trailer
manufacturer. Hyundai is the recent exception as it is planning to introduce Class 8 tractors to the US market along
with their existing trailer products.

Adding aerodynamic devices to a trailer inherently adds weight. That added weight must be compensated for by
reducing weight in other areas of the vehicle. The SuperTruck 2 teams included trailer manufacturers. The projects
allowed developing lightweight trailers taking advantage of aluminum, carbon fiber, and other materials, reducing
trailer tare weights by 1,000 lbs. to perhaps as much as 3,000 lbs. before adding aerodynamic parts back on. An
11,000-lb. 53' dry van trailer is technically feasible with materials such as carbon fiber, however that comes at a
significant premium with current pricing of this material.

Confidence Report on trailer aerodynamics devices [31]. That report was updated in 2020 and re-released [32].

9.5 LIGHTWEIGHTING

Evaluating FTE improvement for a tractor-trailer as an entire system of interacting subsystems and parts was
critical to the success of the SuperTruck 2 projects. Adding technology invariable means adding weight which must
be compensated for by removing weight elsewhere on the vehicle in perhaps on seemingly unrelated systems.
"Lightweighting" is a verb coined to capture these efforts.

The weight of a vehicle is due to its surface area. Just as the human skin encompasses the body, tractors and
trailers have exteriors. That surface area represents combinations of sheet metals and composites that have
weight. That weight must be supported, just as the human body has a skeleton. That skeleton consists of structure
that must survive the harsh dynamics of operational use.

The starting point for design in the past was to use steel parts for both the skin and the structure. Over time,
lighter weight, but comparably strong materials, have replaced steel in many roles. Truck hoods, cab roofs and
fairings are now made of light weight composites. Trailer skins are engineered materials consisting of layers that

<!-- page: 34 -->
SuperTruck 2: Empowering Future Trucks

March 2, 2024 34

may include aluminum and composites. Frames have migrated to aluminum extrusions and castings. The castings
often incorporate multiple functions to reduce part count and reduce the need for fasteners.

Baseline high-roof sleeper tractors may have tare weights between 16,000 lbs. to more than 20,000 lbs. depending
on option content. Trailers may weigh more than 14,000 lbs.

Lightweighting is a continual focus for OEMs as each year fleets and regulators demand new options and features,
while expecting the addition of those not to decrease the amount of paid freight the vehicle can carry.

SuperTruck 2 teams investigated a wide range of materials and concepts for lightweighting. All teams had a
requirement to design for a gross vehicle weight of 65,000 lbs. Additionally, reducing the tare weight of the vehicle
had the bonus of increasing the amount of freight it could carry. Weight reduction then not only helped make
room for adding technologies, it also could directly improve FTE.

9.5.1 Tractor Chassis Frames

The skeleton of the tractor is the chassis. Baseline chassis are generally steel channels connected by cross-
members made of steel or aluminum. These skeletons must be robust as the frame must handle all of the
operational conditions and survive the life of the vehicle. SuperTruck 2 teams all realized that hundreds of pounds
of potential weight savings could be found through frame innovations while still meeting performance objectives.
Several of these SuperTruck 2 concepts are illustrated in Figure 27.

Innovations included replacing older material designs with light-weight magnesium, aluminum, composites, and
high-strength steels. These innovations generally add cost and complexity to vehicles —lighter materials generally
cost more which is why they are used more judiciously in production vehicle designs. However, SuperTruck 2
allowed teams to pursue and evaluate complete real-vehicle designs to identify the potential benefits to the entire
vehicle system rather than merely evaluating them as one-part-replacing-another. The systems' perspective
permits trade-offs to be made on potentially complete disparate systems so that the entire vehicle system can
achieve its goals. Also, these frame lightweighting concepts might be required in future trucks such as zero-
emission vehicles where tare weight may be an even greater concern for designers and fleets.

9.6 REDUCING FRICTION

Friction wastes energy by converting it to heat that is simply expelled to the environment. Friction exists anywhere
that two parts are in relative motion, so pistons inside engine cylinders, rotating parts like drive shafts, meshing
gears in transmissions, planetary gears in axles, bearings in wheels, and more. Those parasitic losses also induce
wear and vibration that eventually force parts to fail leading to vehicle downtime for repairs.

Reducing friction is a continual effort by all vehicle and component developers. The SuperTruck 2 program
empowered a system-wide evaluation of all the contributors to parasitic losses, Pareto prioritization of which were
the most significant, and quantifying where the greatest opportunities were for reduction. Friction reduction has
had decades of industry technology development, so the potential improvements now are often measured in mere
fractions of a percent. One SuperTruck 2 team estimated gains on the engine BTE of 0.9% through friction

<!-- page: 37 -->

reduction [22]. While small in perspective of the entire vehicle, continuous improvement is necessary in all
systems.

SuperTruck 2 teams developed improved machining processes to attain better fits between parts. The teams also
worked with experts to refine lubricants to further reduce friction. Further extensive SuperTruck 2 research
included advanced coatings for combustion chambers. Much of this technology is highly proprietary so very little
detail is available to the public. However, the lessons learned increased the industry knowledge base and likely will
benefit production efforts on a range of future products whether ICE, alternative fuel or zero emission.

9.7 48V HYBRIDIZATION OF ACCESSORIES

Traditional truck diesel engines not only provide the motive force to move the truck down the road, but the engine
also powers a range of additional systems such as power steering, alternator, pumps, cooling fans, and a range of
accessories collectively categorized as front engine accessory drive (FEAD). The FEAD takes power from the engine
through a series of belts and pulleys. Those mechanical connections add power-stealing friction or what is
collectively termed parasitic loss. Friction also induces vibration and heat which can increase maintenance
demands.

SuperTruck 2 teams investigated reducing parasitic losses by replacing the belt-driven systems with electric ones.
Electric accessories generally have less frictional losses, less vibration and create less heat.

Truck electrical systems have operated for decades on 12 volt (12V) electrical systems. This became a convenient
standard common with automobiles and boats. However, higher voltages allow more efficient use of electricity
such as in electric motors. This is termed increased power density and lower resistance losses. The automotive
industry has been investigating adopting more efficient 48V systems since the 1990s. The SuperTruck 2 program
permitted the OEMs to pursue 48V systems to replace the FEAD. The industry term adopted for this is "mild
hybridization."

Removing the FEAD has additional vehicle benefits by providing for much more flexibility in packaging components
under the hood, which allows designers much more flexibility in reshaping the critical exterior form of the hood. A
graphic in Figure 13 from How To Design A Truck illustrates how the FEAD and diesel engine constrains the overall
packaging of a truck [42].

Some SuperTruck 2 teams found additional space by also reducing the engine size from a 15L typical of the
MY2009 baselines to smaller 11 to 13L size optimized around the 65,000-lb. GVW target for SuperTruck 2 vehicles.

The configuration shown in Figure 29 is representative of a traditional cab-behind-engine (CBE) tractor design
typical for the 2008 North American market. Additional design flexibility may be obtained by adopting a cab-over-
engine (COE) configuration more prevalent in European markets. COEs were quite common in the US until the
deregulation of the freight industry in the 1980s, after which CBE configurations completely dominated the North
American market. There are trade-offs with CBEs vs. COEs, such as maintenance accessibility, perception of ride
quality, entry and egress, etc. The PACCAR and Cummins-Peterbilt SuperTruck 2 teams developed more of a COE
type vehicle challenging the CBE cab design paradigm.

## 9.9 WASTE HEAT RECOVERY

WHR systems attempt to capture and reuse that lost heat to help propel the vehicle. A variety of concepts for WHR have been investigated over decades, many funded through the DOE.

## 9.11 SOFTWARE CONTROLS

Software was non-existent in freight trucks in the 1960s. Everything was analog. In the 1990s advancements in engine controls saw adoption of ECMs, engine control modules based on integrated circuitry (IC) chips and software. The growth in control modules was not limited to engines, as they were adopted for automatic brake systems, communication and tracking systems, lighting systems, dash information systems, heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) systems and infotainment systems. All these systems require software code, lines of instructions. Many share and communicate signals across common lines, called data buses. Software is ubiquitous, but not something seen by truck drivers.

Software and ECMs permitted automatically controlling the speed of vehicles in what became known as cruise control. This has evolved from just setting a fixed speed to a complex range of variations that can include maintaining a set distance or time between vehicles, to predicting the rise and fall of the highway or establishing the most fuel efficient or time efficient routes. These systems now have access to geospatial data from global positioning systems (GPS), real-time regional weather conditions, real-time traffic conditions and more.

The SuperTruck 2 teams demonstrated the potential of integrating these vast digital data sources into intelligent decision-making systems governed by software to assist the drivers in attaining the best fuel economy for their specific vehicle on their specific routes in real time.

The Navistar SuperTruck 2 team developed a connected cruise control that evaluated the terrain and traffic ahead and modulated vehicle speed to maximize fuel efficiency. Their concept is shown in Figure 51.

## 11 FUTURE PRODUCTION TRUCKS

It’s very easy to predict the future, once you are there. Documentary series such as BBC’s James Burkes’ Connections and the History Channel’s Modern Marvels can trace with precise clarity that modern inventions originate from a broad series of seemingly unrelated historical innovations [48][49]. Even fictional stories have fueled the creation of modern necessities such as satellite networks, mobile phones, computers, space travel, solar farms and more. How many inventions came out of children watching Star Trek and Batman episodes in the 1960s [50][51]? Or even the Jetsons [58]? Those children became adult innovators and investors that took those ideas and turned them into real-world products.

In 2009, the first DOE SuperTruck program empowered engineers and designers to significantly push the bounds of production trucks with a goal of a 50% improvement in freight ton efficiency. The four program awardees greatly exceeded this goal, delivering to the public production-worthy prototypes in the period 2014 to 2016. Much of the ideas fleshed out during what became known as SuperTruck 1 inspired actual production models in the 2012 to 2020 timeframe as production trucks from multiple OEMs started to achieve greater than 10 MPG efficiencies in long-haul duty cycles.

In 2016, work began on DOE’s SuperTruck 2 program with five OEM teams representing the entirety of diesel Class 8 production in North America. That program goal was to exceed 100% improvement in FTE for long-haul tractors versus a 2009 Model Year representative baseline. All five programs exceeded expectations with prototypes entering the public in 2023 to 2024 timeframe.

What will the insights from SuperTruck 2 inspire in future production trucks? Could you have asked a similar question in 1968 when the first Star Trek television series ended production? In 10-years’ time will truck manufacturers admit that ideas from SuperTruck 1 and 2 inspired some aspect of their latest production innovations? Will innovators even recognize that their ideas incubated in the SuperTrucks program? Or will we have to wait for the latest iteration of Connections or Modern Marvels to connect the historical dots?

Capital investment loves innovators. Innovators chasing venture capital seem never short on ego. Creating the “next new thing” rarely acknowledges how they got there until long after that “thing” is successful. One of the benefits of NACFE is that it is unbiased and is not selling a product or chasing venture capital. As an impartial observer, NACFE concludes that SuperTruck technologies have influenced the industry over the last decade.

SuperTrucks have influenced manufacturers, fleets and regulators. They have inspired improvements, replacing “what if” with “why not” on a range of technologies.

Those technologies have contributed to the “body of knowledge” on freight improvement, impacting ideas on the basic elements of all future vehicles, not just diesel trucks. Those elements are:
* Truck/trailer/driver/payload as a complete system
* Aerodynamics
* Rolling resistance
* Driver ergonomics
* Lightweighting
* Speed, configuration and route management for efficiency
* Mild hybridization and electrification of accessories
* Advanced materials
* Emissions reduction
* Cost reduction

NACFE participated in SuperTruck 1 and 2 in different ways, including providing Volvo with a report on the potential of Intentional Pairing of specific tractors with specific trailers to optimize freight movement [52]. The growth in real-time geospatial vehicle information combined with the Internet of Things such as digital load boards will make possible significant future gains in efficiency by putting the right truck with the right trailer driven by the right driver and taking the most efficient route based on ever-changing environmental and traffic conditions. Those technologies have been investigated in SuperTruck programs.

Efficient energy use in hauling freight is a key performance indicator irrespective of whether the power turning the wheels originates from fossil fuels, green fuels or electricity. Less energy used means lower operating cost and fewer emissions. It often also means greater profitability.

Miles-per-gallon or miles-per-kWh or any of a number of efficiency metrics related to alternative fuels can be expressed in the common measure of miles-per-gallon-diesel-equivalence (MPGde), or diesel gallon equivalence (DGE). Conversion factors are readily available at the DOE Alternative Fuels Data Center (AFDC) [53]. NACFE has discussed these in detail in published reports such as Guidance Report: Electric Trucks – Where They Make Sense [47].

This efficiency metric has evolved into additional factors such as miles-per-fill-up or miles-per-charge. One of the findings in SuperTruck 1 was that as efficiency improves, drivers need less fuel on-board to go the same distance. The significant improvements seen in SuperTruck 1 and 2 have permitted downsizing fuel tanks and their associated heavy diesel fuel load, often decreasing tanks from 200 gallons to 100 gallons or even 50 gallons to go the same distance. Conversely, keeping more fuel on-board may mean fewer fuel stops for a truck during a week, month or year of operation, reducing operational costs for out-of-route miles. Fuel per trip savings have a domino effect on the entire infrastructure supplying the fuel to the pump. Less fuel required to be pumped into a truck has ramifications to the supply chain of fuel all the way back to the well. It can impact locating fueling stations, refinery capacity, the cost of transporting fuels to the pump, storage capacity at intermediate sites, pipelines, etc.

As future trucks are evolving into zero-emission solutions such as battery electric, the concept of miles per charging event replaces miles per fueling. Many of the core technologies developed for the diesel-based SuperTrucks are helping extend the range of zero-emission vehicles, getting more miles out of each charging event or each kg of hydrogen. For example, a 30% improvement in MPG would make a 300-mile BEV capable of going 400 miles.

Changes in diesel fuel demands will also impact things such as low-carbon-fuel-standard (LCFS) credits as less fuel demanded likely translates to less fuel produced requiring credits, impacting the market value of these credits. SuperTruck technology can have a broad impact on the freight system beyond just the individual truck and trailer.

One of the most insightful aspects of SuperTruck is the electrification of accessories through mild hybridization. Direct fuel efficiency gains from removing load from the engine and related design opportunities they enable such as more aerodynamic hood shapes, are key to future truck designs. Full hybridization pairing a diesel engine to high-voltage power storage and recovery systems was shown to offer significant potential efficiency gains versus straight diesel, but also adds significant system complexity and cost. Hybridization of diesel powertrains has been investigated for some years, but SuperTruck demonstrated the opportunities and challenges. Production Class 8 trucks with diesel hybrid powertrains may evolve from this demonstration, or the technology may be viewed as a bridge too far in light of other alternatives. The insights gained with SuperTruck 2 have greatly advanced the understanding of hybridization.

Lightweighting always has been fundamental to trucking. Less weight has been pursued as a way to increase payload and always has a goal of reducing cost as less material is supposed to mean less cost. However, lighter materials such as advanced steels, aluminum, magnesium, carbon fiber and other composites generally come with an increased raw materials and/or processing cost. Cost/benefit tradeoff studies are needed with lightweighting concepts. Fleets and manufacturers often evaluate this as dollars per pound saved (\$/lb. saved), where shipping rates for different commodities may have differing values. A pound saved to one fleet may be worth more or less than that same pound to another fleet.

SuperTruck has expanded the body of knowledge on cost/benefit comparisons of many new materials, again moving the evaluation from “what if” paper studies to “why not” measurable examples. These evaluations have identified goals for material technologists to aim for in reducing the cost of raw and manufactured materials for different trucking applications. Where a particular material may not be cost effective today for today’s vehicle, it may well be cost effective in five-years for the vehicles designed then. The “system” perspective of the complete tractor and trailer also has identified where savings in one element may benefit increases in completely unrelated parts of the vehicle, enabling adoption of a new technology that otherwise might not appear feasible.

A prime example of cost/benefit vs. time is in adoption of aluminum wheels in place of steel ones. Today there are clear duty cycles where aluminum wheels are being purchased for both tractors and trailers, and others where they may make sense only for the tractors, and still others where they do not have a positive return on investment. Another is the adoption of trailer skirts and aerodynamic tractors in what NACFE has termed aerodynamification of trucking over the last 25 years. This is an area where the SuperTrucks have pushed convention and impacted production vehicle designs [54].

Weight savings has a significant role to play in zero-emission truck development where the addition of heavy high-voltage battery packs or very high-pressure carbon-fiber tanks adds weight to trucks on the order of thousands of pounds. Those additions need to be offset to minimize the potential impact to allowed payload weights. The starting point for all trucks and trailers is their frame system. SuperTruck 2 evaluated a number of innovative approaches to removing weight from frames. Many of the concepts may not make financial sense to the return on investment for a diesel truck, but they should have much greater value for a zero-emission one.

Ergonomic design and cab styling boundaries were certainly pushed by all five SuperTruck teams. Radically new cab interior configurations, optimizing on 4x2 vs 6x4 configurations, highly optimized aerodynamic trailers, all projected where the future may be for truck-trailer designs. The most intriguing concepts from NACFE’s perspective are replacing mirrors with camera systems, variations on cab-over-engine designs and the potential for center seating location for the driver. These technologies are already being seen on some production Class 8 trucks.

## 13 CONCLUSIONS

Given our review of the SuperTruck 2 program, NACFE concludes that the teams delivered extraordinary performance against deliverables, were inspired to innovate by the unique SuperTruck program and delivered efficiency technologies to make zero-emission solutions more successful.

1. **Fuel Economy Improvement:** SuperTruck 1 vehicles demonstrated MPGs in the range of 11 to 13 MPG and FTEs greatly exceeding their target 50% improvement versus their MY2009 baselines. The SuperTruck 2 technologies had a target of exceeding 100% versus those same baselines and MPGs as much as 16 MPG by further optimizing diesel technologies, and incorporating 48V mild hybridization, and in one case, full high-voltage hybridization. NACFE estimates the equivalent MPG for the emerging battery electric vehicles is starting at 18.5 MPGe [47]. The performance of the SuperTruck 2 demonstrations shows there are significant efficiency gains remaining for diesel powered vehicles, but it also highlights the complexity required to achieve those gains. SuperTruck 3 and the associated hydrogen programs are innovation solutions for battery electric and fuel cell medium- and heavy-duty trucks in development over the next five years, hopefully with prototype vehicles seen in public as early as 2026.
2. **Innovation Diffusion:** Trucks that exist as ideas, what NACFE terms “paper trucks,” are part of the research and development process, but they are easily debated as they largely lack real-world testing. The SuperTrucks are real-world trucks evaluated strenuously by expert teams that have skin in the game with their own money, resources, and reputations along with funding from the government. Teams often include participants not typically allowed behind the wall of proprietary development at OEMs including national laboratories and university researchers. Potentially excellent ideas that normally sit on shelves from lack of management engagement or interest can make it into full system development of vehicles. Development perspectives that may be hamstrung with component-level development are set free to bloom by participating in a complete vehicle system perspective where, for example, performance gains in one area are achieved by weight reductions or simplification in completely unrelated areas of the vehicle. SuperTruck projects empower designers to literally think outside the mental boxes they typically must work within under normal organizational product development.
3. **Universal Ideas:** Many tractor-trailer system efficiency gains are universally applicable to all powertrain choices. Reducing the energy required to move freight over a distance means less operational cost. Another way to view that is that less energy required per mile can translate into greater net range for the same amount of energy, and or greater payload. Improving the tractor-trailer as a system is a key perspective to help emerging zero emission vehicles to achieve more miles per fill-up. Future trucks as suggested in Figure 56 will build from technologies demonstrated in SuperTruck 2 to achieve increased range and freight capacity. For example, a 100% scale improvement in FTE easily demonstrated by SuperTruck 2 teams through a range of vehicle technology improvements could make a 300-mile BEV capable of going 600 miles.

The Department of Energy Vehicle Technologies Office (VTO) holds Annual Merit Reviews (AMRs) for major DOE funded programs under their supervision. Those reviews require the participating contract awardees to present on the status of their program for a peer review. Each presenter has a strictly controlled time limit to get through presentations and live peer reviewer and public questions. Peer reviewers submit written evaluations which are summarized in an annual DOE report.
