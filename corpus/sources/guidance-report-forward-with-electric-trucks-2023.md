# 1 INTRODUCTION

This document updates and goes beyond a 2019 North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) report titled *Amping Up: Charging Infrastructure for Electric Trucks*.

NACFE’s work has shown that there are many use cases where truck electrification makes both economic and logistical sense [1]. This movement toward electrification is accelerating, with the economics and vehicle capabilities continuing to improve.

Successful incorporation of electric vehicles into your fleet requires that they can be easily, reliably, and efficiently fueled — that means fleet managers and drivers know they will be able to complete their routes without having to worry about running out of power.

This report provides information and advice for fleets on how to charge their vehicles reliably and cost-effectively. This includes selecting the appropriate level of charging and the type of charging connection, managing total electrical load for fleet charging, and — very importantly — working closely with your utility to assure sufficient power is available when needed.

The focus of this report is charging infrastructure considerations for North American commercial battery electric vehicles (BEVs). In its previous electric vehicle Guidance Reports, NACFE found that while the benefits of electric vehicles can be significant, specifying, installing, and operating appropriate charging infrastructure is one of the largest unknowns and sources of anxiety for fleets considering near-term adoption of this technology [2]. 

NACFE created this Guidance Report to provide unbiased information detailing the multiple factors to consider in infrastructure planning for charging BEVs. There is no “one-size-fits-all” solution to charging. However, there are common steps and considerations that any fleet considering deployment of electric trucks should take to ensure they have an effective, reliable, and cost-efficient charging infrastructure in place.

The goals of this guidance report are to: 

a) Provide an overview of the integrated nature of battery electric trucks, their charging infrastructure, and the utility services that support them; 

b) Provide information on designing and procuring a charging system that will keep an electric fleet operating and delivering the goods required; and 

c) Provide common steps and considerations to ensure a cost-effective charging strategy. 

# 2 SCOPE OF REPORT 

This report covers charging considerations for commercial battery electric vehicles (BEVs) currently in production for freight delivery. Charging infrastructure includes not only the chargers themselves, but the interrelated system of vehicles, duty cycles, chargers, and electric utilities. Because most BEVs are currently being deployed in the goods movement sector in the medium-duty urban delivery and heavy-duty drayage sectors, many of the best practices and lessons learned come from these applications. And while we touch on considerations for long-haul BEVs, much of this information is speculative; although battery electric truck deployment for long-haul usage is rapidly expanding, it is still in its pilot phase. 

# 3 OVERVIEW OF ELECTRICITY AND UTILITIES 

Electricity service is something that truck fleets often notice only when something bad happens, like a power outage or when they receive a surprisingly high utility bill. Fleet managers that are considering electric vehicles need a basic working understanding of electricity and the way utilities work in order to estimate charging costs, install chargers easily, and realize the fuel savings that can come with using electricity instead of diesel to power trucks.

## 3.1 Understanding Electric Bills

To estimate charging costs, it is critical to understand how electricity costs are calculated. 

In general, the following components make up a commercial electricity bill. However, with more than 3,000 electric utilities in the United States, you must contact your local utility to understand the structure and options available to you.

* **Service charges** are charges for connecting power and setting up and maintaining the account and meter. Customers pay this base charge even if they use no power.
* **Demand charges** are for the utility’s investment in infrastructure to deliver the power. This is generally based on the maximum power usage over the past 12 months. It takes more infrastructure to deliver higher power levels, even if just for a short period of time, and the utility recovers infrastructure costs through demand charges.
* **Usage charges** are for the amount of electricity used, measured in kilowatt-hours (kWh). The price of a kWh of electricity is set by a tariff that may vary by time of day. Customers of some utilities can select a company other than the utility to provide electricity.

The bill is based on two different characteristics of the electricity service provided. One aspect is the usage charge — the charge for the kWh of electricity used. The other is the demand charge, based on how fast electricity is consumed, measured in kilowatts (kW) or megawatts (MW). One MW equals 1,000 kWs. 

It costs a utility more to provide the same amount of electricity at a higher power level. That is, in a shorter amount of time. The wires and transformers need to be larger, and this cost is reflected in the demand charge.

Some utilities vary the consumption charge — the cost per kWh — based on the time of day, with energy used outside the peak usage hours — generally at night — being less expensive. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) provides an overview on several time-of-use rates that are available in various locations [15,16,17]. 

The two most important strategies for reducing overall electricity costs within this structure are:
* Minimizing the demand charge by spreading charging over as many hours as possible, and
* Charging when energy prices are lower, generally overnight.

In some areas demand prices vary by season or time of day. In those cases, it may make sense to have higher demand during off-peak overnight hours to avoid any demand during afternoons. In the east coast, summer afternoons are often priced highest. Minimizing total demand is a good first approximation of a charging strategy but may be able to be improved upon.

A box truck using 50 kWh of electricity a day, five days a week, would use about 1,000 kWh per month. At $0.10 per kWh, that would result in a usage charge of $100. But the demand charge associated with that charging would vary depending on what time of day and how quickly the truck was charged. If the truck was charged overnight at a depot that already uses a lot of power during the day for other purposes, vehicle charging would not create new demand and the only cost would be the $100 for energy. But if the truck was charged using a fast charger during a high electricity demand period for the depot, it could incur 50 kW to 100 kW of demand or more and create a new demand peak. The greater the amount of power required to charge (that is, the speed of charging), the more expensive the infrastructure to support it. 

## 3.2 Utility Connection Processes 

If you need additional power at your site, you will need to work with your utility to upgrade your connection. Utility connection processes and timelines vary by utility. Your architectural and engineering (A&E) firm will help you navigate the process. However, most utilities share these common processes and allocation of responsibility.

### 3.2.1 Owner Responsibilities

If additional service is required to power the chargers, the property owner must submit a request to the utility for additional electric service. This application can be submitted by you or someone representing you, and generally will require the following information:

* Charging hardware quantity and specifications for each type of BEV charger and associated electrical equipment,
* Charging hardware location(s),
* Proposed location for electrical support equipment, electrical switchgear and utility transformer, and
* Construction drawings (generally as CAD files). 

### 3.2.2 Utility Responsibilities

The utility is ultimately responsible for bringing the electricity to a location on the property. For some utilities this is a “property line box” right at the edge of the property. The utility will note this connection point in its drawing and project plan. It’s in a customer’s best interest to have the property line box built as quickly as possible, as some utilities need that in place before they start their construction work. 

Some interim steps before construction begins include the following.

* **Preliminary Map:** A preliminary map is an elementary sketch showing the nearest power source along with the route to bring electric power onto your site and to the new utility transformer. At this point the utility is providing you with their best educated guess. Timelines range from 1 week to 10 weeks.
* **Final Map:** This is the utility design which includes power source, conduit routing, size and quantity, specific utility standards for safety clearances and protection, transformer pads and vaults. The utility also will provide a CAD file for the A&E team to incorporate into the 100% Construction Document (CD-100) set. Many Authorities Having Jurisdiction (AHJs) require that the utility final map be included in the construction documents submitted for permitting. Timelines to the utility final map vary dramatically based on project size and type of utility. Based on research and interviews, NACFE has identified three sizes of infrastructure projects.
  * For a small project (e.g., two Level 2 chargers and two fast chargers), expect 2 to 10 weeks.
  * For a medium-sized project (e.g., six Level 2 and four demand charge controllers), expect 4 to 12 weeks.
  * For a large project (e.g., 20+ fast chargers), expect 2 to 24 months.

### 3.2.3 Easements

An easement is a defined right to cross or otherwise use a portion of someone else's land for a specified purpose. For instance, an easement is required for a utility to install their equipment (e.g., transformer or electrical primary line) on or across your property. An easement prohibits the property owner from building on top of that easement. Site development must take place in ways that do not interfere with easements. 

### 3.2.4 Public Right of Way

Public right of way is defined as any passageway such as streets and/or other property (such as sidewalks) that are owned by a city, state or federal authority and used by the public. When proposed construction impacts the public use of these right of ways, municipalities require permits. 

## 3.3 Types of Utilities

There are three types of electric utilities in the United States: 
* Investor-owned utilities, 
* Government- or municipal-owned utilities, and
* Electric cooperatives that are owned by the customers.

# 4 THE BASICS OF CHARGING VERSUS FILLING UP 

This section provides electric truck charging basics, describes charging speeds, and covers the fundamentals of electricity generation and delivery. It also provides an overview of key factors for determining your budget and timeframe when installing chargers. Electric trucks may have simpler drivetrains than diesel ones, but their fueling requires a lot more planning.

## 4.1 Charging Infrastructure

It takes energy to move a truck. For internal combustion engine (ICE) trucks, that energy is in the form of diesel or gasoline carried in a tank on the vehicle and replenished by pumping fuel into that tank to replace what has been used. In a BEV, the energy is carried in an onboard battery, and is replenished by connecting the battery to an off-vehicle source of electricity to recharge the battery. 

The primary differences between diesel/gasoline refueling and electricity recharging are:
* Liquid fuels are easily and inexpensively stored in quantity on site and transferred to a vehicle quickly. Fuel prices vary daily and seasonally, but not based on when or how quickly you fill the vehicle’s tank. 
* Unlike liquid fuels, electricity prices are set by rates that — depending on the provider — can vary widely by time of day. Electricity providers often charge more per unit of electricity during peak periods — that is, when electricity demand is high — than in off-peak periods. This is because electricity is costly to store and thus must be used as it is generated, whether from the grid or via onsite generation like solar panels, wind turbines, or generators. In addition, the more electricity used at any one time, the more electrical infrastructure is required to deliver it, and the cost of that infrastructure needs to be recovered through demand chargers.

Fueling BEVs economically requires charging in a way that works with the grid economics of minimizing demand (the maximum rate which you are charging) and maximizing off-peak charging (the time of day you are charging). Thus, charging infrastructure is a combination of: 
* Hardware, including the number of charger plugs, their power levels, and the equipment to deliver that electricity, and
* Rigorous management, generally via software, of overall electricity demand and cost to provide the necessary amount of charge so the trucks can do their jobs at the lowest cost.

## 4.2 Charging Speeds

The industry uses a simple typology to classify the charging power of electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE).
* **Level 2 Alternating Current (AC)** provides 208/240V AC at up to 80 amps to provide up to 19.2 kW of power. This is the same voltage typically provided to a home electric stove or dryer. This level of charging is sufficient for many commercial truck fleets, particularly those that use Class 3 through 6 vehicles, return to base, and are parked for a long period each day (e.g., overnight). Level 2 charging provides AC current to the vehicle’s on-board charging system, which converts that electricity to the direct current (DC) electricity that is required to charge a battery.
* **Level 3 Direct Current (DC) charging**, more commonly referred to as DC Fast Charging (DCFC), is a broad category of charging that delivers DC, rather than AC, to the vehicle, eliminating the need for on-vehicle conversion from AC to DC. DCFC uses three-phase 480V AC industrial power as the input to the off-vehicle equipment that converts AC to DC. DCFC delivers power at rates that range from 22 kW to 480 kW. # 5 PLANNING FOR CHARGING 

Charging electric trucks is a complex process that requires considering a variety of factors. Fleets need to allow sufficient time and resources for the careful planning required.

Determining your charging needs is somewhat of a chicken-and-egg iterative process. It is significantly more complex than planning for an on-site diesel fueling operation. You need to know how much energy you will need to keep your fleet operating, add a margin for uncertainty, and then design a charging system that can reliably deliver that power to your vehicles in the least expensive way. 

That energy can be provided in many ways and can be optimized for the fleet’s property and operational preferences. A MWh of charge to a battery can be provided in many ways. Figure 2 shows three ways that a 1 MWh charge can be delivered: 100 kW over 10 hours, 250 kW over 4 hours, or 1 MW over 1 hour. In general, charging at a lower power is less expensive both for the infrastructure and the electricity.

## 5.1 Vehicles

While vehicle selection is not the focus of this report, the characteristics and use patterns of BEVs determine the characteristics of the charging infrastructure required to deploy them effectively, cost

<!-- page: 23 -->
efficiently, and with the least disruption to your fleet’s operation. The selection of vehicles requires a thorough understanding of your operations. Key information you need to have on hand before you specify your charging infrastructure includes the following.

* An in-depth understanding of the current use of your vehicles, including:
  * Typical and maximum daily miles per vehicle,
  * Load typically carried by each vehicle throughout its day,
  * Time spent parked at domicile (both duration and time of day), and
  * Non-traction load of each vehicle, including heating and cooling.
* For vehicles that do not return to base, where are they domiciled?
* What mix of electric vehicles will you be able to substitute for your diesel or gasoline vehicles to provide the same services for your fleet?
* What is your strategy for pilot testing EVs and integrating them into your fleet?
* Do you have a timeline from your vehicle OEM or dealer on when vehicles will arrive on site after placing an order?
* Will you want one charging plug per vehicle, or will you be able to have staff move vehicles between chargers and parking spaces (or move plugs between vehicles)? 

Unfortunately, particularly for DCFC, there is not yet reliable interoperability between all BEVs and all chargers. Therefore, fleets are encouraged to seek advice from their truck OEM(s) on the best chargers for their BEV fleet. 

## 5.2 Key Charging System Components

The charging system is made up of many components. Typical charging system components are described below.

### 5.2.1 Hardware

Hardware comprises the physical charging stations. Hardware needs to be carefully specified to meet the needs of the fleet.

Note that a single charger may have multiple plugs, allowing it to charge multiple vehicles in parallel or in series. As noted below, a charging management system can make effective use of this capability.

### 5.2.2 Infrastructure

This includes transformers, electrical switchgear, asphalt, concrete, trenching, conduit, wiring, striping, and landscaping. Installing this infrastructure requires construction work that needs permits.

### 5.2.3 Charging Management Systems

Charging Management Systems (CMS) allow a fleet to control and optimize the distribution of charging across all the electric vehicles being charged. For example, this allows the fleet to be charged for energy when it is least expensive or to temporarily reduce vehicle charging when building energy load increases. 

A good CMS will assure that every vehicle has a sufficient charge to carry out its duty and will assure that charge is provided by maximizing the use of the available on-site energy. A well-designed CMS will allow a fleet to avoid purchasing either more or higher-powered (and thus more expensive) chargers than they need. CMSs have the capability to integrate distributed energy resources (solar, wind, battery storage) and manage grid services [28].

<!-- page: 24 -->
### 5.2.4 Networking

Chargers can use software, access cards, or both to restrict and record usage. Networked chargers can offer cloud-based services that benefit the site host and customer, such as utilization monitoring, diverse payment options, app or web-based station locators, real-time status availability reports, and station usage reporting. 

### 5.2.5 Maintenance

A recent study of public electric vehicle charging in the San Francisco Bay area found 23% of public charging stations were not useable [4]. Similar issues have occurred for private chargers as well. Maintenance and repair of charging equipment is essential for ensuring vehicle uptime. Fleets should ensure they have a service package in place to monitor and repair equipment with a guaranteed minimum downtime.

### 5.2.6 Public Charging

Fleet operators may want to allow public usage of their chargers, or give their drivers access to public chargers. Public charging networks have similarities to cell phone networks. Like phones, hardware can be locked to one charger network or be “open” and allow different networking software. Some networks also have roaming agreements with other networks. Almost all networks are pay-for-use.

Several companies, including WattEV and TeraWatt, are planning charging hubs for electric trucks. These are planned to begin coming online over the next few years [11, 39].

### 5.3.1 Level 1 Charging Stations

Level 1 AC charging provides 120V of AC at 12 to 20 Amps to provide 1.3 to 2.4 kW of power. This is the same as typical home current in North America. This type of charger is often simply plugged into a standard home wall outlet. While this level of power is adequate for many to use for home or workplace charging for light-duty vehicles, it does not provide sufficient power for commercial truck fleets.

### 5.3.2 Level 2 Charging Stations

Level 2 AC provides 208/240V AC at up to 80 amps to provide up to 19.2 kW of power. This is the same

<!-- page: 25 -->
voltage typically provided to a home electric stove or dryer. This level of charging is sufficient for many commercial truck fleets, particularly those that use Class 3 through 6 vehicles, return to base, and are parked for a long period each day (e.g., overnight).

Level 2 AC charging stations range from 7.2 kW to 19.2 kW. They require a dedicated 208/240V AC electrical circuit. These charging stations are offered in single phase with more powerful stations sometimes allowing three-phase wiring. Three-phase power has electricity being supplied from three live wires (rather than one live and a neutral or two neutrals). It is more powerful. For any given voltage and amperage, it provides √3 (~1.73) times as many kilowatts.

Level 2 EVSE are technically not charging stations, but rather devices that communicate with and safely deliver AC power to the vehicle’s onboard charger. The vehicle onboard charging system accepts this AC voltage and converts it to DC voltage. The DC power is used to charge the battery pack. 

When selecting a Level 2 EVSE it is important to know the rated kWh of the vehicle’s onboard charger. Most vans, step vans and medium-duty BEVs vary between 11 kWh to 19.2 kWh. Therefore, it is important to match the Level 2 charging hardware to the vehicle’s onboard charger. 

This example illustrates the difference. Assume a BEV with a 100-kWh battery pack charging from a 20% state of charge (SOC) to an 80% SOC (adding 60 kWh):
* A 19.2 kW Level 2 charging station requires 3.13 hours (60 kWh/19.2 kW = 3.13 hours).
* An 11 kW Level 2 charging station requires 5.45 hours (60 kWh/11 kW = 5.45 hours). 

In addition to various kWh outputs, Level 2 EVSE manufacturers offer both networked and non-networked charging stations. Networked chargers (also called smart chargers) communicate key charging parameters (charging on/off, charger errors, disconnection, current charge level and charging complete) to external systems so it can be monitored via a dashboard. This information can be used either to passively monitor the charging status of the vehicles in your fleet or can be connected to a CMS to actively manage charging to optimize a range of parameters.

Non-networked chargers simply start charging and continue to do so until the vehicle is fully charged. There is no communication system to alert you remotely of status changes. Note that electric vehicles generally offer control for programming charging start/stop times and duration of charge and can communicate data on SOC to fleet managers. 

Networked 19.2 kW Level 2 charging stations costs start at about $3,500. Non-networked 19.2 kW Level 2 charging stations costs start at just over $2,000. 

The good news for fleet operators is that almost all BEV OEMs recognize the need to offer Level 2 charging stations matched to work with their vehicle. Many also offer a suite of AC and DC charging solutions and charging management systems along with design and installation services arranged through third party providers. [5,6]

### 5.3.3 DC Fast Chargers

Level 3 DC charging, more commonly referred to as DC Fast Charging (DCFC), is a broad category of charging that delivers DC, rather than AC, electricity to the vehicle, eliminating the need for on-vehicle conversion from AC to DC. DC charging is not new. Electric forklifts along with golf carts using lead acid batteries have been around for decades using DC charging system. These systems typically limit DC charging voltage to 24V, 36V and 48V. 

Current DCFC for lithium-ion batteries uses three-phase 480V AC industrial power as the input to the

<!-- page: 26 -->
off-vehicle equipment that converts AC to DC. DCFC delivers power at rates that range from 22 kW to 480 kW. The Megawatt Charging Standard (MCS) is expected to be deployed by 2024; equipment meeting this standard is designed to deliver DC power up to 3.75 MW (3750 kW). 

Air-cooled cables are not tenable for high power DCFC (150 kWh and above) due to the cable size and weight that would be required to deliver power safely. For these systems, liquid cooling for both the cable and connector is used. Liquid cooled cables reduce the heat load by circulating a cool liquid in the same housing as the conductors. This removes the heat, allowing for high levels of power to be transferred via a cable that is safe and with a bulk and weight easily managed by BEV drivers.

Note that as of 2023, charging for heavy-duty commercial BEVs generally is carried out by a direct plug connection while the vehicle is parked.

### 5.4.1 Matching Chargers with Vehicle Charging Rates

All chargers have an upper limit to the rate at which they can deliver electricity (the maximum charge rate). In addition, each vehicle has an upper limit to the rate it can accept electricity (the maximum charge acceptance rate) [32].

<!-- page: 27 -->
The rate at which a vehicle will be charged is limited by the lower of these two rates. This is controlled by the communications protocol between the charger and the vehicle. For example, if a truck that can handle a charge of 250 kW is plugged into a charger that has a maximum charge rate of 50 kW, it will be charged at no more than 50 kW. Conversely, if a vehicle with a maximum charge acceptance rate of 50 kW is plugged into a charger with a maximum charge rate of 250 kW, the communications protocol will keep the level of power delivered from the charger to no more than 50 kW to avoid damage to the vehicle’s charging system.

### 5.4.3 DC Fast Charger Hardware

DCFCs are manufactured by an assortment of global companies. The variety of product offerings is staggering compared to just four years ago, when NACFE’s *Amped Up* report was released. Following are brief overviews of several varieties. 

#### 5.4.3.1 Small Wall-Mounted DC Chargers — 20kW+ Units

Small wall-mounted DC chargers can be used in place of Level 2 EVSE. Benefits include bypassing the vehicle’s onboard charger by connecting directly to the battery pack. Another benefit is these chargers are available in three-phase power ranging from 220V up to 480V. Three-phase power requires smaller circuitry thus saving infrastructure costs. Most commercial facilities have three-phase power either in 208/220/240V or 277/480V. 

If your facility has 480V power, take advantage of this when selecting charging hardware. Using higher voltage allows smaller gauge wire to deliver the same amount of power, which saves infrastructure dollars.

#### 5.4.3.2 Stand-Alone Systems

Just as the name implies, these DC chargers are self-contained. They include the power electronics that rectify AC to DC, power modules, HDMI screens, payment systems, and RFID all wrapped into a single charging station ready to uncrate and install. These standalone systems range from 20kW to 480kW. Some charge a single vehicle while others can simultaneously charge up to four vehicles. 

#### 5.4.3.3 Modular Station Charging Systems

Modular station charging systems consist of power cabinets that house rectification power modules. The resultant DC power is distributed to pedestals called dispensers. The power cabinets are typically grouped and located near the dispensers, away from parking areas when space permits. Dispensers are typically much smaller than standalone units and intended to be placed in locations convenient for drivers to use.

#### 5.4.3.4 Integrated Station Systems

Integrated station systems are designed for large fleets [7]. They are characterized by a centralized system for converting AC to DC that can be configured to charge tens of vehicles. For large fleets, this is more easily managed and less expensive than multiple stand-alone or modular systems. These large systems are able to connect either to industrial power at 480V or, in some cases, connect directly to the medium voltage distribution grid (at, say, 13,000V). A system connected to the distribution grid can convert that electricity onsite to 480V eliminating the need for a utility transformer. Purchasing power from the utility directly from the grid should be less expensive. In addition, an integrated system uses significantly less space than multiple smaller systems and is overall more efficient in converting AC to DC [33].

Such a solution can readily integrate battery storage and solar and/or wind renewable energy. Such energy is output as DC rather than AC power. An integrated station solution eliminates the hardware used in other systems to convert from DC to AC and back to DC, as well as eliminating the associated power/efficiency losses.

<!-- page: 30 -->
#### 5.4.3.5 Systems with Integrated Battery Storage

DC charger systems with integrated battery storage allow high-speed charging while limiting the size and impact on the grid. These systems take power from the grid at a lower level than a charger supplied to the vehicle, reducing the power required from the grid, thus lowering both the demand charge and providing charging power higher than the grid can directly. That power is stored in batteries that are drawn on when a vehicle is charging. Power system infrastructure costs are lower for these systems than for a standalone system. Another advantage of using internal battery storage is a vehicle can charge at speeds matching other systems by drawing from the stored energy to produce the charge. The battery storage can then be charged when electricity rates are lowest. 

The chief downside is that the system cannot provide full power when the batteries are discharged, limiting the number of vehicles that can be charged in a given period. Despite this limitation, these types of systems may make sense when higher power grid capacity is unavailable or expensive to provide. 

### 5.4.4 Wireless Charging

While most EVs charge by plugging the vehicle into a charger, it is also possible to charge a vehicle by transferring power wirelessly, using electrical induction. This is similar to the way an electric toothbrush charges. 

* Wireless charging requires three components. 
* A primary coil referred to as the ground pad, 
* A secondary coil called the power pad, mounted underneath the vehicle, and
* Power electronics components and user interface contained in a wall box.

# 7 OTHER CHARGING CONSIDERATIONS 

In the vehicle analysis you have carried out, you will have established how much electricity you need to provide to your vehicles to ensure they can carry out their daily tasks with a buffer for unexpected conditions. 

The next step is to select a set of chargers to provide that electricity in a way that minimizes overall total cost of operation (TCO), including capital cost for the infrastructure (with installation), cost for electricity (including any increase in electrical service, demand charges, and coordinating usage with the rate structure), any associated equipment (such as a CMS), and maintenance and repair costs. Also, be sure to confirm warranty details, guarantees of uptime, and guarantees of response time for repairs and replacement with your equipment providers.

Unfortunately, not all chargers communicate reliably with all electric vehicles. While the industry is working actively to improve interoperability and reliability, it is critically important that fleets consult the manufacturers of intended vehicles as part of charger selection. All OEMs have performed capability and acceptance testing of their vehicles with a variety of charger manufacturers and have specific models that they have tested with specific vehicles that they will recommend. 

## 7.1 Charger Communication

Vehicle batteries differ in how much energy they can accept during the charging process. In addition, that amount of electricity changes during the charging process, as the battery’s SOC changes. Proper battery charging requires an EVSE protocol, which, on a basic level, is a two-way communication between the charger and the vehicle. The charger detects the battery’s SOC and adjusts the charging current accordingly, limited by the maximum current the charger can provide as well as the maximum current the vehicle can receive. There also are safety features that will prevent current from flowing when the charger is not connected to the vehicle, when proper grounding has not occurred, or when the EVSE detects hardware faults. These features prevent battery damage, electrical shorts, or fire.

The EVSE’s ability to detect battery SOC also creates opportunities for smart charging systems to optimize charging from the fleet’s perspective rather than by individual truck. For example, a truck with batteries that are at a 20% SOC (i.e., 80% depleted) will need more power and therefore more charging time than a truck with batteries that are at a 70% SOC. Smartly managing these trade-offs and interactions requires a charging management system. 

## 7.2 Sequential and Simultaneous Charging

As discussed, standalone Level 2 chargers are available in networked and non-networked configurations. Networked smart chargers have an advantage when used with a charging management system. A CMS oversees multiple chargers and controls not only charging on and off events but can assign different kW levels to each charging plug based on the number of vehicles being charged, various start times, dwell time, SOC, and required charging completion times. The CMS can direct simultaneous and/or sequential charging as well as set charging to occur when electricity costs are lowest. The fleet operator sets these parameters, and the CMS manages the charging. 

In sequential charging, vehicles are brought to the desired state of charge one at a time, with the charge being moved to the next vehicle in the queue when charging is completed rather than each vehicle

<!-- page: 33 -->
being plugged into its own charger and being charged simultaneously.

Using an example of a 100-kWh battery pack charging from 20% to 80% SOC at 19.2 kW, this medium-duty truck charges in 3.13 hours. Now let’s assume a fleet dwell time of 13 hours with three BEVs to charge. For a fleet of three such vehicles, with three 19.2 kW chargers, all vehicles could be charged simultaneously in 3.13 hours. However, if they were charged sequentially on a single 19.2 kW charger, the total charging time would be 9.39 hours, well below the 13-hour dwell time. Note that a CMS can be used to direct the charge sequentially, with each vehicle being connected to the charger when parked. There is no need to manually move the plugs.

Sequential charging systems reduce infrastructure costs. In our example, purchasing, installing, and operating three 19.2 kW chargers simultaneously would require three separate 100 amp circuits adding 300 amps of load to your electrical system. Using a single 19.2 kW charger sequentially requires only a single dedicated 100 amp, 208/240V circuit. In essence, sequential charging allows you to maximize use of your charging equipment and spread out your electrical load to minimize demand charges. See Figure 9.

## 7.4 Charging Locations

Charging will roll out in stages, first at a fleet’s primary depot. Later, fleets may wish to install charging at its smaller depots. Over time, the charging world may evolve to the point when fleets share charging, where a truck goes from its primary depot and charges at its destination depot, also equipped with chargers, and perhaps belonging to a charging vendor or another company. Eventually, public charging is expected to emerge on high-density freight corridors where distances demand a mid-trip boost or overnight recharge. 

Due to the unpredictable “hub-and-spoke” nature of many commercial trucking operations, most fleets currently adopting electric truck technology will want to place chargers at a central home base such as a warehouse, distribution center, headquarters or depot where trucks start from and return to each day. This type of “return-to-base charging” also makes sense because fleets have full control over site access, charger type, placement, and timing. This may require modifying the site, as the vehicles must be co-located with the chargers long enough to allow charging.

As NACFE’s *Run on Less – Electric* reports concluded, there are many use cases where electric trucks can easily perform all day on a single overnight charge [9]. 

However, charging vehicles at the fleet’s base during dwell times between shifts may not be sufficient for vehicles with larger battery packs and/or longer routes. One potential solution, at least for dedicated regional routes, might be to install charging stations not only at the fleet depot, but also at the customer’s site(s). This could allow vehicles with relatively long A-B-A routes to charge at point B while unloading, giving them enough of a charge to make it back to their home base for further charging between shifts. 

In addition to depot charging, fleets also may consider “opportunity charging” on the road. For example, vehicles may take advantage of the quickly developing public charging network if necessary for range extension or in emergencies. However, because of the costs of using public chargers and the uncertainty of availability, vehicles will likely only want to rely on public charging in case of emergency. But knowing that this option exists should relieve some of the “range anxiety” that fleet managers and drivers may feel about potentially running out of power while away from their home base. Regardless of where charging takes place, fleets that invest in charging infrastructure will want to ensure that station utilization is maximized in order to justify the significant expense.

## 7.5 Powering Your Chargers

Charging BEVs (as well as efforts to electrify other categories of energy use) will increase the demand for electricity. The implications of this increased demand for overall generation, transmission, and distribution will be addressed by utility planners and regulators. However, upgrading the grid is likely to take many years, as it requires significant capital investment, and changes in the regulatory environment by each state’s Public Utility Commission. 

Until the grid is fully updated, you may face challenges and delays in having your local utility provide the electricity needed at your site to charge your vehicles. Because of these challenges, it is exceptionally important to:
* Contact the utility serving your site as soon as you begin thinking about electrifying any trucks;
* Manage the degree and timing of the electricity demand of your charging infrastructure to

<!-- page: 35 -->
flatten the load as much as possible and to avoid times of day when electricity rates are highest;
* Design the phasing in of BEVs to match the power availability schedule your utility provides.

Utilities have numerous strategies to help balance timing concerns with electricity supply and demand. These include demand management and battery storage solutions. Similarly, tariff structures may be used to encourage charging when electricity supply is available, clean, and economical.

Given current grid constraints, utilities would prefer that electric vehicles not charge during “peak” times when electricity demand is highest, typically in the late afternoon or early evening when people return home from work and begin doing energy-intensive chores. Rather, utilities are interested in encouraging charging (and other energy-intensive tasks) during “off-peak” hours when the grid has more excess capacity [36]. Fleets whose operations allow them to charge in these off-peak times can achieve significant savings on their electricity bill.

Because many utilities earn a profit based on a “cost-of-service” business model that guarantees a “rate of return” on the company’s assets or “rate base,” utilities have an economic incentive to build the necessary infrastructure to support transportation electrification. With this information in mind, fleets should not be shy about demanding reasonable support and accommodations from utilities to facilitate vehicle charging.

## 8.4 Procuring Charging Infrastructure

There are two main business models for procuring charging stations and associated infrastructure. The most common is by buying the stations outright, often through a request for proposal process. In this scenario, fleets may hire a consultant to help with the process and set up the infrastructure (and potentially also help manage the relationship with the utility), but in the end, the fleet owns and

<!-- page: 38 -->
manages the chargers, which are then considered a capital expense.

The other way is through leasing in which the supplier owns the stations, and the fleet simply pays a fee for using them. This model allows the fleet to pay for the stations out of their operational expense budget. In both the lease and own options, fleets often pay charging infrastructure suppliers not only for the physical stations but also for access to their fleet management networks, which again, are a recurring operational expense.

Other business arrangements may be possible, including using third parties to create turnkey systems and take on the management of part or all of the charging system. Such third parties may specialize not only in infrastructure procurement and installation, but also in optimizing charging, which can help keep the cost low.

For fleets with little experience or interest in optimizing charging, this charging as a service (CaaS) model can be a good option since these third-party companies may be better able to maximize efficiency and avoid load spikes and demand charges than would a fleet manager. CaaS is discussed in more detail in Section 9.2.

## 8.5 Procuring Electricity

Just as there are various ways to procure charging infrastructure, there are also various ways to procure electricity. Most fleets procure electricity the traditional way — through their local utility using a commercial or industrial tariff. Depending on whether a fleet is located in a regulated or deregulated electricity market, it may be possible to select which company provides the electricity their utility delivers. A fleet also may be able to select tariffs that provide lower pricing at different times, allowing them to manage their charging system to take advantage of those times. Electricity pricing can be complex, containing charges for maximum demand that can greatly impact cost. It is essential that fleet managers fully understand the details of the rate structure, and its implications for their operations.

Fleets also can source some or all their electricity from on-site “behind the meter” solutions such as battery storage, microgrids, and renewables such as solar photovoltaic. However, integrating systems like these into electric fleet charging systems can be complex, as it requires additional infrastructure. Some fleets may wish to defer such integration until later stages of fleet electrification.

## 9.2 CHARGING AS A SERVICE

Charging as a Service (CaaS) is new to the industry but gaining traction in the market [37, 38]. A CaaS provider invests in all the assets needed to assure client vehicles are ready to carry out their duties when needed. A CaaS company will select, procure, permit, install and provide power for all the hardware needed to charge your fleet. As the customer, you will pay a negotiated fee. CaaS may be provided at the fleet’s site or may be provided at a nearby off-site facility that might be used by other BEV customers as well.

CaaS removes the challenges of ownership of charging infrastructure, deferring all of the charging capital costs and logistics to a third party. Fleets that want to pilot a small number of vehicles may make use of a nearby off-site CaaS provider. While CaaS may have a higher total dollar cost, it may be worth considering for some fleets, particularly for smaller fleets or fleets located near an off-site CaaS provider.

AssetWorks, a CaaS provider, provides the following questions for fleet leaders to ask themselves to decide if CaaS is a good fit for their EV fleet [37].

* “Do I have enough capital for charging equipment and electrical upgrades? Would CaaS minimize my upfront costs enough to allow the purchase of additional vehicles or other equipment?”
* “Am I comfortable assuming responsibility for things like charger maintenance and operations after the warranty period?”
* “Does my organization want to handle LCFS credit reporting and selling on its own or with a broker?”
* “Does my organization have the capacity to maintain high charger uptime or is it better for the vendor to be responsible?”

## 10.2 CHARGING OPTIONS

For fleet owners there are three options when it comes to managing charging sessions.

### 10.2.1 Simple Unmanaged Charging

Simple unmanaged charging begins immediately after the vehicle is plugged into the charger. This method requires the simplest hardware and no network. However, it provides no information to the user nor allows any control charging beyond choosing when to insert and remove the plug.

### 10.2.2 Smart Charging

Smart charging allows fleet operators to turn vehicle charging sessions on or off remotely, or to adjust the amount of power provided to each vehicle without having to physically be at the charger. Some smart charging systems also offer some level of sequential charging and/or error alerts. However, smart charging falls far short of the real-world needs of larger electric vehicle fleets that want to focus on running their businesses, instead of manually controlling their charging schedules. This is where managed charging strategies go well beyond the limitations of smart charging.

### 10.2.3 Managed Charging

Managed charging is smart charging augmented with more sophisticated management or artificial intelligence. A sophisticated CMS can suggest or even dynamically adjust charging for each vehicle in a fleet based on facility loads, real-time grid costs, and the planned deployment of each vehicle.

For almost any circumstance with more than a single vehicle, smart or managed charging, as opposed to simple unmanaged charging, will allow a fleet to save operational and electrical infrastructure costs. Smart and managed charging can reduce charging costs by using lower-priced off-peak power and minimizing peak demand. A CMS also can alert the fleet operator to charging failures, like an uninserted or non-working plug, that could lead to a vehicle being under-charged.

CMS comes built-in to some chargers. Sophisticated CMS systems also can be purchased from third-party vendors to complement the chargers’ built-in software. In addition to real-time charging optimization, software also is capable of collecting data and providing analytics to help fleet managers make informed charging and vehicle deployment decisions. For instance, you can learn the state of charge at which vehicles are returning to learn which ones might be able to take longer routes.

# 11 WORKING WITH YOUR UTILITY

Your local utility is an essential partner as your fleet adds electric vehicles to its operations. Remember that your utility has a (regulated) monopoly. No one else can deliver your power. It is essential to speak with your utility as soon as you have even the slightest notion of moving to electric trucks for your fleet. There are more than 3,000 individual utilities in the United States alone, and no two are exactly alike.

## 11.1 ELECTRICITY 101

Electricity is delivered to your site by your utility via a complex interconnected electricity grid that is designed to keep supply and demand in precise balance, as electricity is very challenging to store. Grid managers attempt to keep grid voltage and frequency within a narrow range by adding and removing generation and load as needed, with a goal of delivering electricity from the least expensive mix of generation sources available at any given moment.

Electricity delivered by the grid is generated by spinning turbines or solar power. Turbines can be spun in a wide variety of ways, including by wind or by steam produced by nuclear energy or by burning fossil fuels. Fossil fuels like natural gas, oil, and coal create greenhouse gases and toxic pollutants during production, transportation, and combustion. However, because of the energy efficiency of electric motors, even electricity from the most polluting energy generation is cleaner from an overall life-cycle perspective than burning diesel fuel or gasoline in an internal combustion engine.

The cost of electricity generation varies by location and by time of year in response to fuel supply, available sunshine, wind patterns, and electricity demand. It also varies regionally. For instance, in the northeastern US, electricity demand is generally lowest in the spring and fall, when air conditioning and heating loads are lowest, and at night when lights are off and electrical equipment is less likely to be operating. In parts of California, extensive availability of electricity from solar photovoltaic panels may result in low electricity prices on sunny days.

## 11.2 GETTING UTILITY BUY-IN EARLY

Because freight depots — particularly those without refrigeration or air conditioning — are typically not large users of electricity, adding electric vehicles to a site typically requires increasing the electrical service at the site.

The time it takes to get new power to a site is highly variable. If there’s insufficient nearby capacity, it can take two to five years, or even longer, as the utility will need to design, procure, and construct new substations and grid infrastructure (poles and wires). The cost for interconnection can be significant, and how that cost is allocated between the utility and the customer depends on rules of each utility and state and is sometimes even negotiable between the utility and a large customer. It is thus critically important to learn at the outset of your planning what timeline and cost your utility can offer for various levels of service. If a utility has spare capacity from a nearby substation, the cost will be lower, and the interconnection time will be faster than if the utility needs to build a new substation.

### 12.1.4 Assess Your Electrical Service

Charging BEVs takes a lot of electricity. Even a small fleet of electric trucks will use more electricity than the lighting, HVAC systems, refrigeration and/or shore power for refrigerated trucks, and other equipment used by a typical diesel trucking depot. Thus, step three is to **Assess Your Electrical Service**. You need to understand how much electricity your BEVs will need, and how much spare capacity you have available to charge trucks. (This is also discussed in Section 7.5.)

Your existing electric service provides you with a specified amount of power. This is the maximum power you can use at any one time. Like most customers, your electricity use varies by day and season, depending on what lights are on, how much electricity the HVAC system is using, and what other equipment is being used. Most customers use significantly less than their maximum power most of the time.

Spare capacity — often occurring at night — can be used to charge vehicles. Using existing capacity is far easier than adding new capacity. Careful planning may allow smaller fleets to use spare capacity for all their charging and can allow many fleets to get started while they work with their utility to bring additional power to the site.

Finding out how much spare capacity your facility has is a simple, quick, and straightforward process. Engage with a local electrical contractor (not an electrician but an actual electrical contractor). Electrical contractors have an electrical engineer on staff (or retained) who understands the calculations needed to sum up the existing circuitry and determine your actual capacity. An electrical contractor also has the experience to ensure that the use of any spare capacity is permitted.

To confirm capacity, the electrical contractor will need to know the size of your existing electrical service and your peak kW usage for the prior year. Peak kW usage is often listed right on your electric bill. Having the past 12 months of bills available for the electrical contractor to review will allow them to determine spare capacity and what can be used for BEV charging.

Small fleets planning to install five or fewer Level 2 charging stations for use in a return-to-base operation is a much simpler task than medium fleets contemplating installing a combination of 15 or more Level 2 and DC fast chargers. Large fleets with a wide variety of use cases face the challenges of incorporating combinations of Level 2 chargers and DC fast chargers perhaps using a phased construction process.

Fleets must treat each location they are electrifying as a separate project — no two locations are the same. Fleets should do their best to quantify their infrastructure needs based on today, year two, three, four, and five. Be sure to work with your utility to understand the rate structures available to you.

# 13 PLANNING FOR POWER OUTAGES

Utility disruptions — blackouts, brownouts, weather-related damage, or willful acts of damage to utility facilities — can disrupt operations of any business. But electric truck fleets are particularly vulnerable. Utilities do not like disruptions in service any more than their customers and work hard to keep the grid providing power. However, as the grid expands to accommodate growing numbers of electric vehicles and other electric technologies, such as heat pumps, there may be strains on electric utilities. Fleets that have made the decision to electrify need to have a plan for power outages.

This plan will depend on several factors. Generally, a utility will be able to provide its customers an expected time for the power to be back on. If this time is short enough, and the outage occurs when the trucks are on the road, there may be no need to act immediately. If the outage is expected to be of longer duration, it may be possible to meet your business’s core function with a mix of your electric trucks (if they are sufficiently charged) and non-electric trucks. You may wish to make a contingent arrangement with a nearby truck rental company.

It is important for an operator of an electric vehicle fleet to know what public charging facilities are nearby, to know their capabilities, and perhaps have an agreement to be able to use them in the event an outage is highly localized. You also may want to make arrangements with nearby private facilities.

Note that in the event of a broad outage, such as from a natural disaster like a hurricane, wildfire, or earthquake, there may be widespread disruption to the economy and society, including closed roads and closed customers. In those cases, your own operational needs are likely to change.

## 13.1 GENERATORS

Perhaps paradoxically, fossil-fuel powered generators (also known as gensets) are an important consideration for BEV charging infrastructure, as they can reliably provide electricity in a power outage. Generators are comprised of a power source (diesel, gasoline, natural gas, biodiesel, propane or some other fuel). Coupled to this engine is an alternator that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy. Generators are sized according to kW output along with anticipated run time. As such, generators come in many different sizes. Some are permanently pad-mounted while others are mounted on a trailer and ready to relocate at a moment’s notice.

Generators can be bought new or used, leased, or rented. Fuel, service, and preventive maintenance all can be planned to ensure your generator automatically operates when needed. If you want to be able to operate through an extended electric outage, you may wish to ensure access to generators, either by owning a generator or by having a contractual relationship with a generator supplier who can bring in a generator or generators and fuel in the event of an outage.

## 13.2 BATTERY ENERGY STORAGE SYSTEMS

Unlike generators that produce energy for use when operating, a Battery Energy Storage System (BESS) captures and stores energy for use at some future point in time. Such a system has several uses that can in some cases make economic sense. First, a BESS can help manage and reduce utility costs. Utility billing structure is discussed in Section 3. If your vehicles will need to charge when electricity prices are high, you can store electricity in a BESS while your trucks are on the road and move it to your vehicles’ batteries when they return. A BESS also can be used to smooth peak electricity demand.

A second use for a BESS is to store energy from on-site intermittent sources, such as solar photovoltaic panels or wind generation. Storing electricity from these intermittent generation sources helps assure power will be available when you need it. A BESS is a significant capital investment and should be carefully evaluated before moving forward.

## 13.3 MICROGRIDS

If you operate in an environment where power outages are frequent, in an isolated area where grid capacity is expensive or hard to come by, and you want to have the highest probability of operating during long outages, you might consider building an onsite solar photovoltaic generating system with an attached BESS to store excess energy produced for use when needed.

While this is an expensive option, if it is designed to operate as a microgrid, it not only will allow you to have a highly resilient system, but it also could allow you to operate independent of the utility part or all the time and sell excess power to the utility to generate revenue. See Figure 20. Designing and operating a microgrid is outside the scope of this report but may merit consideration in some instances.

# 15 EMERGING CHARGING TECHNOLOGIES

Technology is changing rapidly in both battery electric vehicles and in charging infrastructure with some developments in the charging arena on the horizon.

## 15.1 MEGAWATT CHARGING SYSTEM

The Megawatt Charging System (MCS) is an emerging standard for high-powered charging at a range significantly higher than currently provided by DCFC. The development of the standard is being led by the Charging Interface Initiative (CharIN) Task Force for Heavy Duty Charging for Commercial Vehicles [34]. The standard is designed for a maximum current of 3,000 amps at 1,250V resulting in maximum charging power up to 3,750 kW (3.75 MW). The MCS standard is not yet final, nor are there any production vehicles available that can use the standard. However, charging at these high levels has taken place on an experimental and pilot basis.

The final MCS standard is expected to be published in 2024. When implemented both in chargers and vehicles, these high levels of charging may allow Class 8 battery electric trucks to charge very quickly, making the longest diesel runs feasible with BEVs. However, that day is not yet here.

## 15.2 IN-ROAD DYNAMIC WIRELESS CHARGING

Another type of wireless charging (see Section 5.4.4) is dynamic in-road wireless charging. This nascent technology uses a series of near continuous charging pads installed beneath the roadway surface, charging properly equipped vehicles as they drive on the road.

# 16 CONCLUSIONS

There are many factors that go into successfully installing an electric vehicle charging infrastructure. The study team offers the following recommendations on how to implement a successful charging infrastructure.

1. **Electric trucks and chargers must work together.** Charging infrastructure is just one part of a system integrating your vehicle needs, electricity rate structure, and the timing and cost of bringing additional electricity to your site. Choose individual components only after designing the entire system, not only for initial implementation, but for potential future needs. Choose equipment with a track record for reliability and make plans for its maintenance. Not all chargers work well with all vehicles. Work closely with your vehicle manufacturer(s) as you select charging equipment. Expect the process to be iterative.
2. **Your utility is a key partner.** Electric trucks use a lot of power, probably more than you currently have available. Your power usage will grow as your fleet’s use of BEVs grows. Determine how much power you need in both the short- and long-term to fully implement your plans. Learn how much power you already have on site to charge trucks, and how much more you will need to get beyond the pilot stage. It is essential that you meet in person with your utility as soon as you begin thinking about electric trucks, as they will be your partner in providing the power you need. Increasing power delivery to your facility can take time. Coordinate closely with your utility and modify your implementation schedule to assure you have power when you need it.
3. **Use and design greatly affect charging cost.** Both AC and DC chargers are available in wide variety of power outputs and technologies appropriate for any vehicle. In general, spreading charging over the longest time and using lower charging power makes both the charging equipment and electricity costs lower and maximizes battery life. Design your charging strategy to make the best use of vehicles’ scheduled downtime. Charging management software can pay for itself by ensuring your vehicles are ready to roll when needed at the lowest cost.
4. **The transition requires staff and attention.** The transition to BEVs and associated charging infrastructure requires attention and expertise. You need to have a single point of contact with internal and external authority and to lead the project. If your utility has a BEV team, reach out to them. Talk to other fleets and resources like your local Clean Cities Coalition. You may benefit from hiring an expert consultant at the start of your move to owning BEVS.
5. **Consider other charging business models.** You may wish to explore options other than owning and operating your own charging infrastructure as a stopgap or long-term model. These include

6. **Other key considerations.** Grants, incentives, and subsidies, both at the state and federal levels are at an historic high offering a window of opportunity for fleet electrification. If you do not own your facility, talk with your landlord early.
