# 1 INTRODUCTION

The messy middle was coined by the North American Council for Freight Efficiency (NACFE) in 2019 and 
was used to describe the period between the present diesel-dominated truck freight market and the 
zero-emission future dominated by electrified powertrains, both battery electric and hydrogen fuel cell, 
where the energy is generated from green zero-emission sources [1]. The messy middle describes the 
interim period in the trucking industry highlighted in Figure 1 where there will be many powertrain 
offerings designed to decarbonize emissions. We are in this phase now because there are many 
unknowns and challenges for low- and zero-emission technologies, and currently none alone are capable 
of getting the industry to zero emissions by 2050.

While there is no one solution for all regions and applications, the currently predominant diesel engine 
cannot be the solution in many instances due to evolving state regulations and corporate shipper and 
fleet sustainability goals. California and other states have created laws which mandate zero-emission 
solutions at both the OEM and fleet levels. Many Fortune 500 companies have established sustainability 
plans to move their supply chain to zero emissions. More stringent greenhouse gas (GHG), nitrogen 
oxide (NOx) and particulate pollutants, increased warranty periods and useful life regulations also will 
continue the need for further improvements in the internal combustion engine.

Given all of these factors, we have written this report to help fleets think through the various options. 
The scope of this report is directed toward the Class 8 long-haul segment — the most demanding freight 
segment based on performance requirements. Long-haul Class 8 trucking is one of the largest

<!-- page: 11 -->
contributors to transportation GHG emissions due to the disproportionate high mileage these vehicles 
see in daily operations versus other vehicles. 

The messy middle might also be a term applicable to medium- and light-duty commercial vehicles, which 
also are transitioning from gasoline and diesel-based emitters to zero- and near-zero alternatives. 
However, NACFE’s Run on Less – Electric reports highlighted that many of those medium- and light-duty 
segments can be adequately served today by emerging battery electric vehicles [2][3][4][5][6]. The 
messy middle term also is applicable to Class 7/8 regional haul tractors, which NACFE also found in Run 
on Less – Electric could be served well by emerging battery electric trucks [7]. 

NACFE’s goal is to remain unbiased, to provide useful guidelines, and to help fleets move toward 
effective GHG reduction while enhancing their ability to meet their financial and operational goals.

This paper discusses each major powertrain option, its advantages and limitations. We identify the 
major factors that a fleet must consider when determining the appropriate powertrain and provide 
fleets with a decision-making process with guiding principles. 

## 2 WHAT IS THE MESSY MIDDLE AND HOW HAS IT CHANGED

In the fall of 2017, the NACFE board of directors approved the group expanding its work to include 
guiding future change — later changed to emerging change. This began efforts to help guide the 
industry through many challenges that would arise as we move to a much greener future movement of 
goods. NACFE started producing Guidance Reports, analyses and research that help guide the industry 
through these transformational changes. Already having created a successful process to scale available 
technologies using Confidence Reports, Guidance Reports were launched, with a focus on electric trucks. 
By early 2019, NACFE had produced three such reports.

* *Electric Trucks: Where They Make Sense*, May 2018 [10]
* *Medium-Duty Electric Trucks – Cost of Ownership*, October 2018 [11]
* *Amping Up – Charging Infrastructure for Electric Trucks*, March 2019 [12]

Having studied the overall situation for commercial battery electric trucks, the early adopting medium-duty marketplace, and how the industry would create the charging infrastructure, it was now time for 
NACFE to complete work in the more challenging segment of heavy-duty electric tractors and a team led 
by Rick Mihelic, NACFE’s director of emerging technologies, was created. 

As the team finalized the work leading up to the analysis and report, in late 2019, a key new finding 
emerged. Unlike other segments like vans and step vans, medium-duty trucks and heavy-duty terminal 
tractors, electrifying heavy-duty tractors would take longer and be more challenging. Given this and the 
growing expectations around sustainability, NACFE concluded that the transformation would not only 
take longer to get to zero emissions, but that the change would cover several decades where other 
alternatives would emerge and even sunset on the path to fully electric vehicles powered by 100% clean 
energy. The report, *Viable Class 7/8 Electric, Hybrid and Alternative Fuel Tractors*, was published in 
December 2019 [1]. 

The graphic in Figure 2 was the key one published with the report and quickly became known as 
NACFE’s “messy middle,” becoming a common discussion topic from 2020 until today. The presentation 
version shown in Figure 3 was used extensively in conferences, panel discussions and seminars.

## 2.1 CHANGES SINCE THE INCEPTION OF THE MESSY MIDDLE

The messy middle of decarbonizing heavy-duty tractors has grown in importance with the increasing 
emphasis on decarbonizing the trucking industry. Many of the changes and much of the progress have 
focused on commercializing zero-emission vehicles, both battery electric and hydrogen fuel cells (H2FC). 
For example, Volvo and Daimler are building and delivering Class 8 battery electric vehicles (BEVs) and 
Tesla just announced that its Class 8 Semi traveled 500 miles fully loaded [34][35][36]. 

Also, we now see some applications such as terminal tractors and short regional haul having favorable 
total cost of ownership (TCO) relative to diesel as detailed in NACFE's two reports, *The Use Case for 
Terminal Tractors* and *The Use Case for Heavy-Duty Regional Haul Tractors* [4][7].

On the H2FC side, we have seen a lot of activity. Daimler and Volvo have invested $1B in a joint venture 
to design and produce fuel cells; all major OEMs have collaborated with fuel cell companies and have 
built demonstration vehicles [37]. New OEMs have entered the North American market with H2FC 
tractors, including Nikola and Hyundai. 

The Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) was passed in August 2022 by the US government and has placed 
emphasis on green hydrogen and provides billions of dollars in funding and tax incentives to produce 
green hydrogen [14][15]. The Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (IIJA) passed in November 2021 
provided similar emphasis and funding on the electrification of transportation [16][17]. Both bills 
promote investment in zero-emission vehicle technologies and the critically necessary infrastructure to 
generate, transmit and deliver energy to those vehicles.

While there has been tremendous progress in all these areas, there still are significant challenges that 
likely will exist over the next decade. Because of these challenges, there is a lot of activity toward

<!-- page: 16 -->
advancing technology to reduce the carbon and emissions impact of trucking now and over the next 
several decades. 

There is one point of note on the description of a messy middle for decarbonizing trucking that we want 
to make clear. In calling it the messy middle, NACFE has not and does not mean for these times to be 
problematic or too much for the trucking industry to handle. We do believe the messy middle is a time 
of opportunity. Fleets along with their suppliers that make good, data-based decisions on which 
technologies to deploy and when to deploy them will be successful. Mistakes will be costly. This period 
will be a bit chaotic, confusing, and challenging, but also incredibly rewarding when done right.

Another way to describe the messy middle is as a period where fleets will face an overwhelming number 
of technology choices. Buying and operating commercial trucks in an economically viable way always has 
meant fleets faced many choices. This is reflected in the thousands of option content decisions needed 
to order trucks from OEMs. However, until recently, the primary fuel choice was simply diesel, with 
some recent growth in natural gas use. 

The messy middle is now presenting several new energy types, each adding complexity to the fleet 
buyer’s decision-making process. Not only is the fleet buyer having to decide on truck technology, but in 
many cases, the fleets also must decide on infrastructure technology and services. The interim period 
we refer to as the messy middle will have multiple good choices, each better with respect to GHG 
emissions than the diesels they are replacing. In the interim period of the messy middle, there will be no 
“one right choice,” no clear single national market-leading technology or OEM to simplify the fleet’s 
decision-making process.

A key success factor mentioned by a few of the leaders we interviewed was the need for manufacturers 
to really help fleets make the right choices. Fleets will be very demanding of their supplier partners for 
not only trucks and components but also for credible data upon which to make decisions. With a 
plethora of available solutions, it will be very important for suppliers to be transparent and detailed 
concerning the benefits and challenges of their solution(s). It is even more important to have correct 
and reliable data to make decisions from suppliers that are dedicated to their customers’ success. 
Forcing technology onto the incorrect customer or duty cycle will be unacceptable in these challenging 
times.

## 3 ANALYSIS AND REPORT METHODOLOGY

Findings in this report were determined given a thoughtful effort by NACFE during 2022. The study team 
used the work already completed including Confidence and Guidance Reports, as well as the three Run 
on Less events and other work completed by NACFE and others. Interviews specifically asking a series of 
questions concerning these technologies and the messy middle period of decarbonizing trucking were 
conducted with specific leaders at fleets, truck builders, component manufacturers, industry 
organizations and others. The NACFE and RMI freight teams were very helpful in bringing centuries of 
combined experiences to this work. Finally, the team used informal discussions at truck conferences to 
explore the topics with an eye on completing this report.

## 4 LATEST DYNAMICS DRIVING THESE CHANGES

This report is intended to assist fleets, manufacturers and others in navigating these exciting, but 
challenging times. The following is a summary of where new dynamics have evolved over the past few 
years, bringing these now inevitable changes.

<!-- page: 17 -->
## 4.1 CUSTOMER AND STAKEHOLDER EXPECTATIONS

There has been an increased emphasis on the environment and sustainability goals. Shippers are asking 
fleets to provide sustainability information and take active steps to reduce emissions. This has changed 
the game from “deliver at the lowest cost and on time” to “deliver at the lowest cost, on time and in an 
environmentally favorable way.” One reflection of this is the signatories to the Climate Pledge, an 
initiative to encourage commitment to setting carbon-reduction goals and enacting credible plans to 
achieve them. As of December 2022, Climate Pledge reports 378 signatories representing $15 trillion 
total market capitalization [23].

## 4.2 INTRODUCTION OF NEW ENGINES

Over the past few years, there have been several advancements in engine technology. Examples are the 
natural gas, hydrogen and the 15-liter fuel-agnostic engine. They can run on hydrogen or renewable 
fuels such as renewable natural gas or diesel, biodiesel, etc. These engines are worth noting because 
they are addressing one of the most difficult areas in trucking — Class 8 long haul. These trucks produce 
significantly less NOx than their diesel counterparts and thus will be able to meet the new ultra-low NOx 
regulations [22]. They also provide significant CO2 reductions. Providers also are developing new or 
reviving older engine architectures such as hybridization and opposed-piston engines offerings.

## 4.3 REGULATIONS AND INCENTIVES

Regulation and policy also have significantly changed over the last several years. These include ultra-low 
NOx requirements which will reduce NOx to 80% below current standards, extending required warranty 
periods to 450,000 miles, increasing useful life limits to 650,000 miles, and zero-emission mandates on 
the truck manufacturers and fleets [26]. 

The IRA provides incentives to reduce the cost of hydrogen production, reduce the cost of 
manufacturing, and it provides a $40,000 credit to purchase a zero-emission vehicle (ZEV). There also 
are numerous incentives and tax credits at the state and municipality level. The IIJA provides $7.5 billion 
in incentives to deploy and operate a nationwide network of charging stations, and $65 billion to rebuild 
the electric power grid for future demand. The IIJA also has $50 billion in funding to improve the 
resilience of infrastructure to protect against droughts, floods and wildfires, in addition to a major 
investment in weatherization [14][15][16][17].

Diesel emissions reductions have been the focus of ever-increasing federal regulatory requirements 
since the mid-1990s. The EPA Phase 1 heavy-duty GHG rules were enacted in 2011 effective with model 
year 2014 and beyond [20]. EPA Phase 2 heavy-duty GHG rules were finalized in 2016 and applied to 
model year 2018 and beyond [21]. The EPA Clean Truck Plan governing heavy-duty emissions that 
includes NOx requirements was announced in December 2022 for model years 2027 and beyond [22]. 
The goal of the latest rule clearly is to enable “swift adoption of zero-emission vehicle technologies” 
while recognizing that a transition period from fossil diesel is a necessary reality.

The regulations and incentives are designed to accelerate the transition in the messy middle, eventually 
leading to the goal of a zero-emission transportation system. The reality is that improvements in diesel 
engine technology also will be part of the messy middle and diesel commercial vehicles likely will 
continue to be built and operated for several decades, but in decreasing numbers over time. Regional 
differences in energy availability, truck factory production capacities for different technologies, 
availability of regional grants and incentives to purchase, operate and/or scrap vehicles and many other

<!-- page: 18 -->
factors will influence the speed at which diesels begin to exit the marketplace. Class 8 long-haul trucks 
invariably operate across regions. For example, a compressed natural gas (CNG) truck running a diesel 
internal combustion engine may fuel with renewable natural gas (RNG) in California and then fuel with 
CNG in Arizona. Regional rules also may necessitate changes in operations, with more trailers being 
handed off at strategically located hubs to address regulatory differences. 

## 4.4 OPERATIONAL CHANGES

While the OEMs and Tier I and II suppliers are working to introduce ZEVs or new internal combustion 
engine (ICE) concepts, there also are significant, and often highly effective ways to reduce carbon 
through fleet operational changes. Some of these are not new since the coining of the phrase “the 
messy middle,” but they are being discussed more. For example, converting long haul to intermodal can 
yield a 60% to 80% reduction in CO2 emissions, and many carriers are planning to significantly increase 
shipments via intermodal. The ability of North American rail systems to adapt to new capacity demands 
will be crucial for any increase in intermodal shipments. Rail power units also are starting to transition to 
lower emission alternative powertrains, with demonstrations of both battery electric and hydrogen fuel 
cell-based engines to replace traditional diesel-electric ones [18][19].

Other examples for commercial truck operational opportunities to reduce emissions are to use more 
double trailers, utilize data analytics to optimize routes and loads and implement the emerging use of 
autonomous vehicles to deal with the perennial driver shortage and driver hours-of-service limitations. 
A range of efficiency enablers such as low rolling resistance tires, lightweighting, aerodynamic tractor 
and trailer devices and many others continue to be relevant to all the existing and new powertrain 
alternatives. The technologies are universally applicable. 

Finally, on the ZEV side, fleets can optimize their routes and operations to find ways to use quick 
enroute charging and regenerative braking energy recovery throughout the day mitigating some of the 
range issues associated with BEVs.

## 4.5 OTHER CHANGES WORTH NOTING

Other changes since the inception of the messy middle that are worth noting are:
* **Many new concepts** — There are numerous other new ideas and concepts. Hyliion has 
introduced a hybrid natural gas solution [38]. A concrete company in Canada is capturing the 
CO2 on the vehicle in motion and then using it in the cement making process [39]. Various 
companies are developing swappable battery concepts to resolve range issues with a quick swap 
battery exchange [40]. Demonstrations of overhead or under pavement catenary power systems 
that allow powering vehicles in motion continue to be funded [41][42].
* **Fuel price instability** — Things have been turbulent with fuel prices and instability has 
increased, most recently with significant increases in 2022. Given current world policies and the 
unpredictability of global events, diesel fuel prices likely will remain at higher levels for some 
time. This instability in fuel pricing can accelerate solutions such as BEV and H2FC, allowing each 
to reach TCO parity with diesel sooner.
* **Second life uncertainty** — Since there is no one solution that fits all needs and the life of these 
different technology solutions is unknown, there is tremendous uncertainty as to the value of 
the vehicle after its initial use. Vehicle first owner life expectancy also may be changing as the 
newer powertrain technologies may have significantly lower maintenance issues allowing longer

<!-- page: 19 -->
use for the first owner of the vehicle. Technology obsolescence regarding batteries and 
computers also may be a significant factor with the new powertrain technologies.

# 5 THE DIFFERENT POWERTRAIN SOLUTIONS

Given these dynamics a plethora of different powertrain solutions have emerged. Some offer zero 
emissions from the vehicle movement of goods while others are more quickly integrated offering 
improvements in sustainability over today’s diesel. The following describes those with the most 
potential.

## 5.1 BASE DIESEL AND ADVANCEMENTS OF FUTURE REGULATION

The diesel engine has been the powertrain solution in transportation for more than 100 years. It has 
provided the lowest cost freight transportation solution when not factoring in environmental 
ramifications and social equity considerations. The diesel engine has undergone significant changes in 
recent years as EPA regulations have mandated lower emissions. According to the Diesel Technology 
Forum, more than half of all diesel commercial vehicles on the road in the US are advanced diesel 
technology models [45].

In its current state, diesel provides the best range, lowest cost, widest available fueling and best 
packaging in the vehicles. Current vehicles are significantly lower emitters than older models while also 
being capable of achieving much higher fuel efficiency as shown for particulate matter (PM) and NOx 
emissions in Figure 6 and for GHG emissions in Figure 7. Alternative fuels, such as renewable forms of 
diesel and natural gas, also are helping reduce emissions.

## 5.2 RENEWABLE FUELS

There is significant interest and development in renewable fuels and engines. Renewable fuels can range 
from renewable diesel to renewable natural gas to renewable propane. Renewable natural gas for 
example can provide a negative carbon intensity (CI) factor. 

With Cummins’ introduction of the 15-liter fuel-agnostic engine, this could be a good solution for Class 8 
long-haul applications. Renewable fuels also can provide a financial benefit to the fleet from the 
standpoint of lower cost fuel as well as tax credits.

Availability of the renewable fuel is not universal in the North American market. The DOE maps 
alternative fuel stations, including biodiesel, CNG, electric, ethanol, hydrogen, liquified natural gas (LNG) 
and propane [24]. Several of these maps are included in the Appendix.

### 5.2.1 Renewable Diesel

Renewable diesel is a biomass-derived transportation fuel suitable for use in diesel engines. Renewable 
diesel, previously known as green diesel, is a hydrocarbon produced most often by hydrotreating and 
also via gasification, pyrolysis, and other biochemical and thermochemical technologies. According to 
the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), renewable diesel can be made from nearly any 
biomass feedstock. Put more simply, renewable diesel is made by hydroprocessing fats, vegetable oils 
and waste cooking oils. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) says most of the renewable diesel 
produced in the US is used in California [46][47][48]. 

Renewable diesel has reduced GHG emissions and can be used in existing diesel engines. It can be 
transported via existing pipelines and sold at conventional truck stops and fueling stations.

Renewable diesel typically costs more than traditional diesel. There also are some concerns about the 
feedstock being used to make renewable diesel and its impact on deforestation and the destruction of 
natural habitats. Another concern is about the availability of feedstock needed to make renewable 
diesel [49][50]. 

### 5.2.2 Renewable Natural Gas

According to Cummins, “Renewable natural gas (RNG) is obtained from biogas, a methane-rich gas 
resulting from the fermentation of organic waste such as cow manure, sewage sludge or landfill 
organics.” [50] According to Sightline Institute, “These places are rich sources of RNG because when

<!-- page: 22 -->
animal waste and trash decay, the microbes that break them down produce gases that contain methane, 
which can, in turn, be captured, cleaned up, and pumped into a pipeline [51]. 

Renewable natural gas can be used in any natural gas engines and has become part of climate goals in 
some states. Most renewable natural gas has a lower carbon intensity than fossil natural gas [52]. 
However, currently there is not enough RNG available to be a wholesale substitute for diesel although 
the number of production facilities has grown in recent years [51][52]. 

RNG also is more expensive than diesel and other fuel sources and since natural gas is made of methane, 
it does emit carbon dioxide when used as a fuel [52]. The additional cost can be offset with Low Carbon 
Fuel Standards credits and other incentives.

### 5.2.3 Biodiesel

Biodiesel is made from waste vegetable oils, animal fats, or recycled restaurant grease and is renewable 
and biodegradable. According to an article on the Conserve Energy Future website, “It can be used in 
pure form (B100) or can be blended with petroleum-diesel in the form of B2 (2% biodiesel, 98% 
petroleum diesel), B5 (5% biodiesel, 95% petroleum diesel), B20 (20% biodiesel, 80% petroleum diesel) 
and B100 (pure biodiesel) [53].” 

According to the *Transport Biofuels* report from IEA, replacing fossil fuels with biofuels is one of the 
primary ways to decarbonize the transport sector. In the report’s Net Zero Emissions by 2050 Scenario, 
biofuel consumption triples between 2019 and 2030, equivalent to 12% of global transport fuel demand 
in 2030 [60]. 

Biodiesel has some advantages including the fact that it can be used in a diesel engine with little to no 
modification. Biodiesel also is said to reduce GHG emissions; for example, B20 reduces CO2 by 15%. It 
also produces less PM emissions. “Vehicles that run on biodiesel achieve a 30% fuel economy than 
petroleum-based diesel engines,” according to the Conserve Energy Future article. With a lower 
flashpoint it is safer to store and transport [53]. 

Biodiesel improves lubricity, which reduces friction in the moving parts, and raises cetane levels, which 
makes the engine easier to start and reduces ignition delay [61]. It is said to provide better resistance to 
cold weather as well [62]. 

On the negative side, since biodiesel is made from a variety of crops there can be variation in the quality 
of the finished product. Biodiesel also is prone to gelling in low temperatures which makes it unsuited 
for use in certain climates. It also is more expensive than diesel and is not as widely available as diesel 
[53]. 

## 5.3 NATURAL GAS

Even if the natural gas is not from a renewable source, it can provide a net benefit over diesel. These 
engines also provide significantly lower NOx output. Natural gas engines already have been certified to 
the California ultra-low NOx standard of 0.02 g/hp-hr (grams per horsepower-hour). 

The concerns of natural gas engines are methane leakage, regional fuel availability, lower fuel efficiency 
and vehicle fit. Natural gas is high in methane and there is known leakage which occurs throughout the

<!-- page: 23 -->
supply chain. For example, if more than about 1% leakage occurs, the net benefit of CNG is eliminated, 
and more leakage results in more harmful GHG effects.

### 5.3.1 CNG

Compressed natural gas technology for heavy-duty trucks for moving freight has been in use for a 
decade, having been through several iterations of engine development since 2009 [1]. CNG is made from 
methane and “is extracted from the source and compressed to a high pressure,” according to California 
Air Resource Board [54]. 

There are several benefits to using natural gas, according to the Energy Education website, including the 
fact that the gas for CNG can be sourced locally, reducing US dependence on foreign oil. It burns cleaner 
than diesel and therefore has less harmful emissions [55]. It also is less expensive than diesel [58]. 

CNG fueling can be a problem as there is not a widespread fueling network [57]. See Figure 8. Because 
of its low energy density, the driving range of a CNG powered truck is generally less than that of a 
comparable diesel, according to DOE [59]. Extra storage tanks can be added to increase range, but that 
also will increase the weight of the vehicle, which can be an issue for weight-sensitive fleets. The upfront 
costs of a CNG vehicle are approximately 1.5 to 2 times higher than that of the diesel. The cost of the 
fuel is approximately 50% that of diesel, so it can have a favorable TCO.

### 5.3.2 LNG

LNG, similar to CNG, has had significant real-world fleet use over years and miles. While the overall 
market share versus diesel is small, the technology for using this alternative fuel has matured well past 
prototype levels. Liquefied natural gas stores the natural gas under low enough temperature 
to turn it into a liquid [1]. 

Even though LNG is a fossil fuel, it can produce between 10% to 20% less CO₂ emissions compared 
to diesel, according to Volvo. It is widely available and generally is cheaper than diesel. “Currently LNG-powered trucks can travel up to 1,000 km (approximately 620 miles) before refilling, making it a viable 
fuel for long-haul applications,” according to Volvo [56]. 

One disadvantage, of course, is the fact that LNG is a fossil fuel. In addition, the filling network is limited. 
Fleets also will need to add additional tanks for the LNG which will add weight to the vehicle and reduce 
payload [56]. 

LNG provides much better volumetric density than that of CNG by a factor of approximately 2.5. It can 
improve vehicle fit issues. However, LNG must stay below -260°F to remain in the liquid state. The 
“holding time” is the time that the tanks keep it cold and is estimated at five days. If this is exceeded, 
the LNG is vented into the atmosphere, and releases methane, resulting in more harmful GHG effects.

## 5.4 DEVELOPMENT OF THE HYDROGEN ENGINE

A significant development since the inception of the messy middle is that of the hydrogen internal 
combustion engine. Cummins has announced its agnostic engine will run on hydrogen, as well as other 
fuels. Vehicles would be configured for specific fuels due to differences in storage systems. 

Burning hydrogen in an ICE-powered vehicle can provide zero carbon emissions, assuming the hydrogen 
is produced through green processes using energy from renewable sources. It also produces very low 
NOx. This could be a viable solution for Class 8 long-haul applications. The NOx characteristics are like 
that of natural gas engines which have been certified to 0.02 g/hp-hr. Press releases also indicate that 
the thermal efficiency is approaching that of a diesel, although hydrogen engines still are much less 
energy efficient than BEVs. Producing and delivering hydrogen is very energy intensive, leading to high 
fuel costs unless heavily subsidized [25]. 

The major concern with this approach is the availability of green hydrogen. The IRA has created major 
incentives to provide green hydrogen, but for now there are only a handful of hydrogen fueling stations 
for long-haul trucks. As of December 2022, the DOE shows only 60 public stations in North America for 
all vehicle types, the majority (53) of which are in California. See Figure 9. Of these, only four are heavy-duty truck hydrogen fueling sites.

## 5.5 HYBRIDS

Hybrids provide another potential solution in this phase of the messy middle. They can be powered by 
diesel or renewable fuels and can provide adequate range. They provide regenerative braking and thus 
recover energy while going downhill and in stop-and-go traffic. They are an excellent solution for many 
medium-duty and shorter regional haul applications below 200 miles per day. However, they provide 
limited benefit in long-haul applications due to the weight of the batteries needed for those distances.

Hyliion is a new company offering a new solution since the inception of the messy middle. They have 
adequate battery storage on the vehicle so the vehicle can travel for 75 miles on the battery and then 
use the onboard engine for much longer range. This is referred to as a “near-zero” vehicle and has 
emissions benefits [38].

## 5.6 BATTERY ELECTRIC VEHICLES 

NACFE has written many Guidance Reports regarding the applicability of BEVs in trucking. In summary, 
they provide a great solution for Class 3 to 6 vehicles that run in urban stop-and-go, regional haul with 
return-to-base operations, and travel less than 200 miles per day. Some of these applications today have 
positive TCO. Examples include terminal tractors, vans and step vans, medium-duty box trucks and short 
or medium regional haul.

Vehicle prices are expected to decrease over time as production volumes increase, and TCO is expected 
to continue to improve. Federal, state, and local incentives currently are required to bring the out-the-door vehicle price paid by fleets to parity with the diesels they are replacing.

<!-- page: 26 -->
H2FCs and BEVs are the only products which will meet evolving ZEV rules. The BEV provides a zero-emission vehicle solution with the simplest vehicle configuration of all these options. They also are 
expected to have considerably lower maintenance costs since there are many fewer moving parts, no 
emissions systems, easier reporting requirements, etc. 

The BEV also provides a viable solution in that it is the best in terms of overall energy efficiency. It has an 
overall efficiency of about 65% to 85% from the charger to the wheels. This compares to approximately 
40% to 50% for ICE and 30% to 50% for H2FC applications.

The three major challenges for the BEV are infrastructure/charging, range and cost. Today, many fleets 
are finding that the BEV arrives from the OEM, but the charging infrastructure is not available. In some 
cases, this is just a planning problem between the OEM, fleet, charging company and the utility. 
However, other problems are that utility companies are at capacity and additional capacity is years 
away. NACFE will be conducting Run on Less – Electric DEPOT in 2023 to help fleets with this problem. 
The IRA and IIJA are providing significant funding and tax incentives to help enable growth in energy 
generation and delivery capacities [14][15][16][17]. 

Range and weight issues prevent the BEV from becoming a solution for distances longer than 200 miles. 
However, recently Tesla and other companies are trying to address this. Tesla just reported its BEV is 
capable of 500 miles, fully loaded [36]. However, the actual payload capacity still has not been publicly 
stated for this range. BEVs are given an additional 2,000 lbs. federal payload gross vehicle weight rating 
(GVWR) exemption thus can be up to 82,000 lbs., however the actual payload of BEV trucks will be less 
than diesels which will limit their use in duty cycles operating near maximum weight.

## 5.7 HYDROGEN FUEL CELLS

The hydrogen fuel cell converts hydrogen into electricity. There are different technologies but the type 
being considered for transportation is the polymer electrolyte membrane (PEM). NACFE has written an 
extensive Guidance Report on this, *Making Sense of Heavy-Duty Hydrogen Fuel Cell Tractors* [25]. 
Hydrogen is a challenging subject due to the complexity, manner and cost of production, storage and 
distribution. 

The H2FC has the potential to solve the Class 8 long-haul zero-emission challenge. It qualifies as a ZEV 
and can provide adequate range. Daimler has announced a truck that will travel 600 km (approximately 
375 miles) on liquid hydrogen [43]. It is expected that refueling can be accomplished in times which are 
very similar to those of diesel.

The primary issues with the H2FC are maturity of technology, costs, availability, and infrastructure. The 
H2FC is behind the BEV in terms of maturity by probably a decade. To date, there have mainly been 
demonstration units. Nikola states it will start limited production in late 2023. Hyundai has introduced 
units in Europe and has them slated for the US.

The cost of the fuel cell system is estimated to be three to four times higher than diesel. On-board fuel 
storage for hydrogen is expensive, requiring Type IV tanks capable of 10,000 psi (700 bar) gaseous 
hydrogen. These tanks are designed and built with carbon fiber and taken through very stringent testing 
to ensure they can survive impact. Hydrogen’s volumetric density also is very low relative to diesel; 
therefore, a lot of space is needed on the vehicle for fuel storage. Liquid hydrogen would limit this

<!-- page: 27 -->
challenge but introduces other system complexities. (Note: More detailed information on these 
complexities will be published in the Hydrogen Guidance Report later in 2023.)

The IRA has significant financial incentives and tax subsidies to reduce the cost of hydrogen [14][15]. 
Most industry analysis indicates that the cost of hydrogen must reach about $3/kg ($1.36/lb.) to reach 
TCO parity with diesel. Initial purchase prices of the vehicles also must come down.

# 7 OTHER CONSIDERATIONS IN MAKING POWERTRAIN CHOICES

The trucking industry is very complex with many different operational duty cycles, challenges of 
geographic differences, economic cycles and on and on. Some challenges have been clear for decades, 
while others have emerged very recently. Given the research and analysis from this project, the 
following sections describe the decision considerations for powertrain choices that fleets, and those of 
us supporting them, must consider.

## 7.1 SUSTAINABILITY: DEFINING TERMINOLOGY

It is important to consider all factors that influence the environment. Two terms worth expanding on are 
“well-to-wheels,” and “tank-to-wheels.” Well-to-wheels is used to assess the life cycle assessment of 
fuels, including all phases of its life cycle, from the extraction of raw materials to their use. Tank-to-wheels refers to a subrange in the energy chain of a vehicle that extends from the point at which energy 
is absorbed (charging point; fuel pump) to discharge (being on the move).

A ZEV has zero emissions from a tank-to-wheels perspective. Depending on how the energy (molecular 
fuel or electricity) was produced, it might or might not be zero emissions from a well-to-wheels 
perspective. This is an important consideration. If the electricity was produced by coal or natural gas, or 
if the fuel cell vehicle consumed “grey hydrogen” produced through steam methane reforming (SMR) 
with no carbon capture or sequestration (CSS) there will be adverse effects on the environment which 
are not necessarily any better than fossil fuel-derived diesel.

From a GHG standpoint, some toxic emissions proven harmful to the environment and a person’s health 
include CO2, NOx and PM. Since 1974, the EPA has been limiting NOx and PM [29]. 

When comparing the various powertrains, only BEV and H2FC provide zero emissions from a tank-to-wheels perspective and are thus referred to as ZEV. BEVs emit zero CO2, NOx, and PM. In the case of 
renewable fuels such as renewable natural gas (RNG), there is a reduction in CO2 and in some cases, 
there actually is a negative carbon intensity factor as the fuel offsets expected emissions from other 
sources such as dairy farms. As for NOx and PM, solutions such as natural gas and renewables provide 
improvement over standard diesel.

## 7.2 TECHNICAL MATURITY 

The maturity of a product is a critical factor for a fleet. Over the past several decades, the industry has 
experienced new products and technologies such as exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), diesel particulate 
filters (DPF), and selective catalytic reduction (SCR). Several of these introductions were problematic 
which resulted in high downtime, excessive maintenance, and high warranty costs for the suppliers. The 
costs of downtime for a fleet significantly hurts its operational efficiency and thus finances. All new 
technologies face some sort of a learning curve when first introduced into real-world operations. 

Failures also can occur much later as these new technologies see high mileage or long life cyl issues. This 
often is referred to as the reliability bathtub curve as shown in Figure 11. Note also that failures always 
are possible, which is one of the reasons warranties exist.

The base case diesel has the highest level of maturity. While there have been incremental changes over

Moving along the scale of maturity in Figure 11, alternate fuel ICE products leverage many parts and 
components of the base diesel and thus have relatively good maturity compared to a fuel cell. While 
there were early life reliability problems with natural gas engines introduced in the 2010 to 2014 period, 
reliability of the technology is in the flat mid-life period today. 

The BEV and H2FC are the least mature. While the BEV still is relatively new, and there will be future 
changes with battery technology, it has shown to be a fairly reliable power source demonstrated in 
more than a decade of automotive use with millions of vehicles and miles. Production heavy-duty 
battery electric trucks are in their infancy at this time but have been through OEM development 
processes outlined in NACFE’s report *Defining Production* [30]. Maintenance costs of BEVs are estimated 
to be 50% to 60% of diesel due to the significant reduction in parts subject to friction and vibration. 

The H2FC engine is the least mature in heavy-duty trucks, although it has been in use in transit buses for 
more than a decade. To date there only have been prototype and pre-production heavy-duty truck 
demonstration units. Production units are expected to appear in 2023 from Nikola and Hyundai in the 
North American market, and in subsequent years from other manufacturers. The fuel cell stack and 
other components will need to be designed to reliably last 20,000 to 30,000 hours — comparable to 
diesel lifetimes — before refurbishment. Using long-life fuel cells in the challenging heavy-duty truck 
environment is technically demanding of materials. Simultaneously, there will be efforts to lower costs

<!-- page: 30 -->
such as reducing use of expensive materials like platinum. This too will drive change and adversely affect 
the technical maturity. 

## 7.3 RANGE AND ROUTE FLEXIBILITY 

In December 2022, Tesla announced that a Class 8 Tesla BEV drove 500 miles in mountainous terrain 
fully loaded at 82,000 lbs. [36]. The actual payload weight carried was not detailed in the public 
announcements. The 500-mile range demonstrated in this early Tesla Semi has the potential to expand 
the utilization of heavy-duty battery electric trucks, opening up the ability to consider BEVs to replace 
diesels in more duty cycles. The Nikola Tre BEV was introduced into production in early 2022 and offers 
a stated range of up to 330 miles. Other production offerings from Daimler, Volvo, Kenworth, Peterbilt 
and other new OEMs offer single-charge ranges of 150 to 250 miles as highlighted in NACFE’s report *The 
Use Case for Heavy-Duty Regional Haul Tractors* [7]. The heavy-duty BEV is not the replacement for all 
diesels. Figure 12 illustrates where the various technologies are best optimized for range and payload

## 7.4 INFRASTRUCTURE

Infrastructure, availability of the energy source, and the “greenness” of the energy are all important 
points to consider on this journey to a zero-emission freight transportation future. 

The North American trucking market has consumed diesel for 100+ years, therefore refueling stations 
are currently abundant. According to the National Association of Convenience Stores, the national 
association for fuel retailing, there are an estimated 145,000 diesel fueling stations throughout the US. 
The number of pumps vary by station, but diesel is considered readily available. 

On the other end of the spectrum, there are just 56 hydrogen fueling stations in California, of which only 
four are capable of fast fueling heavy-duty trucks today. California does have a plan to build 1,000 
stations by 2030, however, that will primarily be for passenger cars. The IRA will help this build out. 

Battery electric charging stations for automobiles exist in many regions in the US and more are planned. 
Heavy-duty truck charging standards are just now emerging. Battery electric trucks require significantly 
higher charging rates than automobiles. Heavy-duty BEV infrastructure lead times to procure, install and 
become operational can be lengthy due to a myriad of reasons detailed in NACFE’s *Amping Up* report 
[12]. There are some examples today where the grid capability and the charging station lag by two 
years. Costs for heavy-duty truck charging systems and the related grid infrastructure can be high. The 
nature of electric utility operations also means excess capacity is regionally rare and in some parts of the 
country like Texas and California the grids are often at maximum capacity. 

## 7.5 VEHICLE INTEGRATION

Diesel fuel has the best volumetric density compared to the other options. The volumetric density of 
diesel is approximately 39 Megajoules per liter (MJ/Liter), compared to natural gas at 9 MJ/Liter, 
hydrogen (700 bar) at 6 MJ/Liter, and a battery which is less than 2 MJ/Liter. This makes vehicle 
integration easiest for a diesel. It should be noted that over time it has become more difficult to 
integrate an ICE into a vehicle as emission control devices have been introduced. The addition of EGR, 
DPF and SCR have all claimed vehicle space and have increased vehicle weight and wheelbase length. As 
we look into the future, the additional components required to meet ultra-low NOx regulations will 
stretch this further. 

Another key aspect of vehicle integration is to ensure adequate cooling. Again, using diesel as the 
baseline, the task of integrating the engine into the vehicle is well known and real estate on the vehicle 
is well established.

<!-- page: 32 -->
Fuel cells and ICE are inefficient; devices that are inefficient release heat. This heat drives temperatures 
well above 105°C (221°F) if not cooled. The operating temperature of the fuel cell is approximately 85°C 
(185°F), compared to an ICE at around 105°C (221°F), requiring larger surface areas for fuel cell cooling 
modules. If the operating temperature needs to be 85°C (185°F), it is more difficult to cool than one that 
operates at 105°C (221°F). Basically, you require a lot more cooling to get down to 85°C (185°F) than 
105°C (221°F). 

Next, approximately one-third of the heat on an ICE is dissipated through the exhaust. Since there is no 
exhaust or combustion in a fuel cell, additional cooling is needed. Finally, not only does the fuel cell 
need to be cooled but other major components also need cooling — batteries, power electronics and 
electric motors. In summary, the total cooling requirements for a fuel cell will likely be more than twice 
that of the ICE powertrain. 

Another item to consider is gross vehicle weight and the weight on each axle. Given the large number of 
components claiming space within a BEV and fuel cell vehicle, this becomes a challenge, leading to 
higher front axle weight ratings and heavier structural demands. 

The vehicle integration challenges of a natural gas or hydrogen engine are primarily that of the fuel 
tanks. The tank space claim for these powertrain solutions typically requires the addition of fuel racks 
chassis mounted behind the cab, adding 24” or more to frame length. This challenge explains why we 
mainly see natural gas vehicles with day cab configurations. It is difficult to find adequate space for the 
natural gas tanks on a sleeper cab, although newer vehicles are emerging with saddle-mounted natural 
gas fuel tanks, eliminating the back of cab racks. 

BEV and H2FC trucks are typically heavier than their diesel and CNG counterparts. The additional weight 
translates into payload limitations. BEVs can introduce a weight penalty of 7,000 lbs. to 14,000 lbs. 
versus a comparable diesel day cab. Fuel cells can introduce a 6,000 lbs. to 10,000 lbs. penalty vs. a 
comparable diesel day cab. Note, for ZEVs maximum weight allowance has been federally approved to 
82,000 lbs. 

Freight weight actually carried varies considerably by truck, by route and by fleet. Care must be taken 
not to lump all vehicles into maximum weight as the national average and actual distribution of weights 
varies considerably as documented in NACFE’s reports *More Regional Haul – An Opportunity for 
Trucking*, and the Run on Less – Electric market segment report *The Use Case for Heavy-Duty Regional 
Haul Tractors* [32][7].

## 7.6 REGULATIONS

Regulations definitely will play a major role in the selection of the powertrain during the messy middle 
period. In some cases, ICE vehicles will not be allowed. In other cases, regulation will increase the cost 
and complexity of ICE trucks, making electrified options the better choice. 

When we speak of regulation, we should discuss the various types. They are:
* More stringent regulations on internal combustion engines, 
* Zero-emission vehicle mandates,
* Upcoming fleet and warehouse regulations. 

### 7.6.1 Stringent ICE Regulations

<!-- page: 33 -->
In 2010 the trucking industry felt that additional NOx regulation would not happen, and future efforts 
would be directed toward improving detection (e.g., on-board diagnostics, OBD). While there certainly 
has been an emphasis on OBD, there also have been new regulations on CO2, more stringent NOx rules, 
additional duty cycles aimed at light load conditions, extension of warranty, and useful life 
requirements.

The EPA threshold for NOx is 0.2 g/hp-hr. However, California adopted the ultra-low NOx rule in August 
2020 which will require NOx not to exceed 0.05 g/hp-hr by 2024 and 0.02g NOx by 2027 [33]. In addition 
to this, a new drive cycle has been created. Useful life warranty expectations are increasing to 800,000 
miles. In December 2022, the EPA adopted warranty life requirements of 450,000 miles and useful life 
increased to 650,000 miles. EPA’s Clean Truck plan requires NOx emissions to be 0.035 g/hp-hr during 
normal operation, 0.050 g/hp-hr at low load, and 10.0 g/hr at idle for model years 2027 and beyond 
[63]. 

The ultra-low NOx requirements are estimated to increase the cost of a diesel vehicle, with estimates 
hydrogen ICE can more easily meet the new NOx requirements than fossil fuel diesels.

in 2035. Several other states are adopting similar rules and timelines including Oregon, Washington, 
New Jersey, New York and Massachusetts. In total, 17 other states and the District of Columbia have 
signed a Memorandum of Understanding and are in the process of adopting or already have adopted 
similar rules [44][7]. Figure 13 illustrates the timing of California’s ACT rule.

### 7.6.3 Incentives

There are numerous government incentives and they are being used as ways for fleets, OEMs and Tier I 
suppliers to decarbonize. These incentives have been around for a very long time and all of them cannot 
be summarized in this paper. There are incentives from the US government, municipalities, states, etc. 
that provide funding and grants for ZEV and alternate fueled vehicles. For more information on 
incentives, read the Run on Less – Electric Report *Electric Trucks Have Arrived: Documenting a Real-World Electric Trucking Demonstration*. 

The IRA is one major incentive that is providing significant funding to increase the development and 
adoption of ZEVs [14][15]. It also has a major focus on building hydrogen capability in the US, both for 
transportation, as well as for other areas such as steel making.

The IRA allocates $368B toward decarbonization. It provides a production tax credit to produce green 
hydrogen. If the hydrogen is green, the tax credit is $3/kg ($1.36/lb.). There also are incentives for 
manufacturing and an Investment Tax Credit (ITC) under which 30% of capital costs can be deducted for 
projects. Finally, the IRA provides a $40K tax credit for the purchase of ZEVs.

# 8 PROJECT CONCLUSIONS 

After significant review of existing research, project interviews with experts, and discussions, the NACFE 
team offers the following conclusions. 

* **This is a time for action.** Yogi Berra was known for his many quotes, and one is very appropriate 
for these times. He said, “If you see a fork in the road, take it.” The messy middle and the 
movement to a 100% carbon-free future is exactly that fork in the road. Fleets will need to 
change their survival strategy from a “lowest cost and timely delivery” mindset to using a more 
complex decision-making criteria when selecting the specifications of their equipment and 
operations. This criterion certainly has cost and timeliness issues, but other factors such as 
infrastructure, sustainability, social justice and regulations have been added to the equation. For 
many fleets and on many routes, using diesel will not be an option. In this report we have 
provided the background and analysis and offered guidance as to how to navigate through this 
new world. We highly encourage fleets to get involved and to begin evaluating different options 
using the entire industry as a resource. Fleets cannot sit back and wait.
* **All stakeholders should support fleets in making the right adoption decisions.** There is a broad 
group of stakeholders that operate, build, test, regulate and incentivize the various solutions 
available for the industry to achieve its sustainability goals more aggressively. There will be 
multiple correct technology choices for each fleet or for multiple parts of a fleet’s business at 
the time they make procurement decisions. A great deal is riding on these choices, from the

<!-- page: 35 -->
fleet’s ability to deliver on sustainability promises, cost, speed of freight delivery, etc. From 
regulators passing rules for the supply and use of these technologies to the manufacturers who 
are describing and selling their products, fact-based, transparent sharing of information is 
critical. 
* **Decisions should include a realistic understanding of your pipeline capabilities.** The trucking 
industry has a long history of delivering through challenges from economic highs and lows to 
deregulation, building of the highway system, regulations around pollutant emissions and many 
other issues. Collaboration of all involved parties has been crucial to successfully navigating 
these challenges. Given the vast number of solutions emerging and the urgency to continue a 
pathway toward a zero-emission future, there is a lot for all industry players to do. For instance, 
a truck OEM will likely not have the resources to offer all these solutions to their customers and 
will need to make some difficult decisions concerning offerings and market segments. 
* **An even higher level of collaboration is essential.** Gone are the days when fleets could make 
investment decisions in isolation. Collaboration and a systems’ engineering perspective are 
necessary to chart a course through the messy middle into the zero-emission future. Fleets need 
to engage with utilities, city, county and state planners, fire marshals, charging electric vehicle 
supply equipment (EVSE) suppliers, charging service providers, OEMs, competitors, and others 
to plan their facilities and operations.

# 9 THE DECISION-MAKING PROCESS

This report is intended to not only provide education about the elements that are shaping the 
decarbonization phase of trucking, but also to provide useful guidelines on how fleets can make 
decisions. The world is changing very rapidly, and multiple options are available with hundreds of factors 
going into the decision-making process. Therefore, we encourage fleets to follow a disciplined process 
and employ guiding principles in order to make sound decisions.

Our goal is to remain unbiased and work toward solutions which have an effective impact on reducing 
greenhouse gas emissions.

In this project, the interview team asked many questions concerning the factors fleets should consider 
when making these important decisions. Figure 14 outlines many of these key factors.

## 9.1 WELL-TO-WHEEL IMPACT FACTORS 

Consider the source of the fuel/energy. If you are considering electrification, work with the utility to 
understand the mix of energy being used to produce the electricity. Is it all coal or natural gas? What 
percentage is renewable or nuclear? The average mix of sources throughout the US is approximately 
40% renewable or nuclear. If the energy production source is at or above this threshold, electrification is 
good for the environment. If it is not at this level, work with your utility to understand their transition to 
decarbonization.

If electrification cannot be implemented, are renewable fuel options available? Renewable natural gas 
has a negative carbon intensity, due to the use of methane as the feedstock, which is very harmful to 
the environment. Natural gas that is not renewable still emits 10% less CO2 than diesel. 

How green — meaning produced from renewable energy — is the hydrogen as you start operating your 
hydrogen vehicles and how so into the future? 

## 9.2 REGULATORY FACTORS

What are the upcoming regulatory factors facing your fleet over the next 5 to 10 years? Are you in an 
area where a ZEV is or will be required? Consider the upcoming EPA ultra-low NOx requirements. 
Consider the additional costs of an engine that meets regulations, where estimates range from $10,000 
to $40,000 over the life of each vehicle. Consider possible reliability problems that could create more 
downtime. When considering all these items, are solutions other than diesel more reliable or cost effective?

<!-- page: 37 -->
## 9.3 DUTY CYCLES

What type of duty cycle does your fleet run? Is it long haul at 65 MPH for 500 miles? Is it a regional haul 
with a lot of stop-and-go runs, lightly loaded? Does the vehicle return to base each day? Is your vehicle 
in operation for 8 to 10 hours per day or running two to three shifts? Do you run in flat regions or 
mountainous ones? Are temperatures moderate, or are you running in extreme desert conditions or 
cold areas?

If the duty cycle is more regional with stop-and-go runs, lighter loads, etc., then electrification could be a 
good option. If you run long haul, then H2FC (once the technology matures) or RNG might be the correct 
solution.

## 9.4 ENERGY/FUEL PRICING

Consider the cost of fuel, both from a capital and operational standpoint. For electrification, work with 
the utilities to understand their pricing and the capital investment needed. Diesel and other fuels 
fluctuate with many market conditions or world events. For RNG, understand the costs of infrastructure 
and the costs of RNG in a Diesel Gallon Equivalent (DGE) format. 

Also, understand the efficiency of these different fuel types and how much fuel will be consumed per 
mile or ton-mile of work. In general, RNG can be 10% to 20% less efficient and yield worse consumption 
numbers on a DGE basis.

## 9.5 ENERGY / INFRASTRUCTURE

Work with your utilities to determine if electricity is available. Make sure you have an estimate of the 
energy demand of your fleet. Also work with the charging company to determine what type of chargers 
are best for your operation and the schedule required to install the chargers.

As for renewable fuels, determine where the fueling stations are and whether they are in the routes 
that your fleet travels.

## 9.6 CUSTOMER/SHIPPER GOALS

As noted earlier in this report, a focus on sustainability is clearly one of the largest changes in the 
trucking industry. In the past, all that mattered was cost and whether the goods arrived on time. Today 
shippers are asking fleets what they are doing to reduce their carbon footprint.

## 9.7 OTHER CONSIDERATIONS

There are numerous items to consider in this category. This has to do with capital considerations, buying 
cycles, life of vehicle, etc. When possible, work to monetize as many of the considerations outlined in 
Figure 14 as possible when considering alternative fuels.

Today customers and shippers have Environmental, Social & Governance (ESG) and sustainability goals. 
They will place some weight on parameters such as well-to-wheel carbon footprint, ZEV usage, etc. The 
fleet must understand the different powertrain solutions and how they affect CO2, NOx, and PM.

# 10 A SUGGESTED FRAMEWORK FOR POWERTRAIN DECISION MAKING

NACFE suggests that a best practice for this decision-making includes many considerations with the 
primary ones being suitability for the duty cycle, financial implications and the sustainability of the 
energy used to power the trucks. Figure 15 provides that framework and offers a sequence for 
evaluating the various solutions.

* Start with battery electric trucks. If they meet your needs and the cost and timing is right, this 
can be the simplest path forward and a single change for your fleet.
* If BEVs don’t work, the next consideration might be hydrogen fuel cell vehicles, given another 
zero-emissions pathway with probably a better solution for long haul. (This assumes that the 
technology matures, and green hydrogen becomes more readily available.)
* Natural gas, hybrids, renewable fuels and hydrogen internal combustion engines are options in 
the messy middle that fleets might deploy if their strategies to decarbonize cannot be supported 
in a timely manner by zero-emissions solutions.
* In some cases, it may make sense to stay with a diesel strategy working to make the equipment 
and operations as efficient as possible, ahead of a later decision to move to zero-emission 
vehicles.

hydrogen engine solutions before finishing with H2FC tractors. There likely will be a large number of 
combinations of alternative powertrain journeys.

Given all the options and no clear one right choice, it may be tempting to just sit on the sidelines during 
the messy middle until things become clearer. However, the reality is there is no avoiding the messy 
middle. Fleets need to find the option(s) that work best for them today and keep working with them into 
the future. For the short-term that could include taking strides to improve the efficiency of their diesel-powered trucks while continuing to monitor developments in other powertrain options. The messy middle 
is a time for action and doing everything possible to become more efficient.
