REPORT

**ABSTRACT** This report contains information and findings on the three battery electric terminal tractors that took part in NACFE’s Run on Less – Electric (RoL-E) commercial battery electric vehicle production vehicle demonstration. Collectively, the duty cycles for these terminal tractors were highly representative of the overall market segment.

The event included a total of 13 fleets and 13 vehicle models from 13 manufacturers located in eight states or provinces in the US and Canada. However, this report is only focusing on the battery electric terminal truck segment of the market — a market NACFE believes is 100% electrifiable.

If 100% of the terminal tractors in the US and Canada were electrified, it would require approximately 726 gWh of electricity for charging and result in the avoidance of 929,687 MT $\text{CO}_2\text{e}$ annually. (e equals carbon dioxide equivalent.)

The Run took place from September 2 through September 19, 2021, with continuous tracking of vehicle parameters on two of the vehicles via a Geotab device and one via the manufacturer’s own system.

**Terminal Tractor Basics**

The North American Council for Freight Efficiency’s (NACFE) Run on Less – Electric (RoL-E) freight efficiency demonstration included three battery electric terminal tractors. A terminal tractor is defined as a kind of semi-tractor that is intended to move trailers within a cargo yard, warehouse facility, nearby warehouses, an intermodal facility, a large manufacturing facility or similar location. Terminal tractors have some unique features including a single-person cab, full height rear sliding door, short wheelbase, and more. See Figure ES 1. Terminal tractors in most applications travel very few miles in a day and their average speeds are very low. Mileages for the terminal tractors in RoL-E ranged from 14 to 29 miles. Frequently the maximum speed is limited to 15 to 25 mph. Some terminal tractors have a very high usage rate while others have substantially lower usage rates.

**Benefits of Electric Terminal Tractors**

Battery electric terminal tractors offer many advantages in the marketplace. First, the electrical power for a battery electric vehicle (BEV) should be much less expensive than diesel fuel on an equivalent energy basis. Second, the maintenance cost for a BEV should be much less than that of the most recent terminal tractors. There are some obvious benefits to using battery electric terminal tractors as well as some less obvious ones.

Obvious benefits include quiet operation, performance advantages, stay near home base to facilitate charging, use less energy during idling, have a smaller emissions footprint, and have no odors when operating.

Less obvious benefits of electric terminal tractors include possible avoidance of licensing fees, and potential for in-warehouse operation.

**Challenges of Electric Terminal Tractors**

Offsetting the benefits of battery electric terminal tractors, of course, is their purchase price. The cost of a battery electric terminal tractor is significantly higher and charging infrastructure to supply electricity for the vehicle is required. Incentives can be difficult to apply for and the process of procuring infrastructure can be long. There also is a need for training of drivers, technicians, first responders, and other fleet personnel.

**Terminal Tractor Report Findings**

The study team has six key findings based on the three battery electric terminal tractors that participated in RoL-E.

1. **NACFE considers terminal tractors one of the best, if not THE best, paths for a heavy-duty tractor fleet to learn about and implement a BEV in a fleet operation.**
   * Choosing to purchase a battery electric terminal tractor will help mobilize the fleet to understand the many aspects of what will be required in future larger purchases of BEVs. This would include evaluating the areas of purchasing, TCO considerations, facilities, vehicle parking, operations changes, selecting and installing electric charging infrastructure, driver training and reactions, maintenance, etc.
   * The battery electric terminal tractor is limited to a small area around the facility, so it is always close to home. This allows for opportunity charging.
   * Electric units are available to be a direct replacement of virtually all diesel-powered terminal tractor use cases with very few operational adjustments.
   * Most terminal tractor duty cycles can get by with low power level (and thus less expensive) charging stations.
2. **The drivers in Run on Less – Electric all rave about their quieter operation and improved performance.** All the drivers mentioned that they liked the responsiveness of the battery electric terminal tractor, and they also liked the regenerative braking feature once they got used to it. In fact, Ryder driver Ray Hancock, said, “For me the difference between an electric and a diesel tractor is response. When you drive an electric truck, when you press the accelerator, you get an immediate response.” Drivers all mentioned the quiet operation, and some mentioned that this made hearing dispatch orders much easier. Also, one driver mentioned that it was great not having to deal with diesel fuel odors and spills.
3. **The maintenance cost of a battery electric terminal tractor compared to the most recent diesel-powered terminal tractors (2017 and later with diesel particulate filters and selective catalytic reduction aftertreatment systems) is dramatically reduced (approximately 60% to 75%).** Multiple fleet leaders mention that newer diesel terminal tractors have created significant maintenance headaches and costs. Even with very little early experience with the battery electric terminal tractors, fleets are clear that these are a big improvement.
4. **Battery electric terminal tractors have a positive environmental impact and will contribute to overall business sustainability goals.**
5. **Payback times for a battery electric terminal tractor compared to diesel-powered units are currently long for most applications (eight or more years) without incentives and other factors to help the TCO calculation.** Initial cost of a battery electric terminal tractor is currently $275,000 to $350,000 compared to $100,000 to $125,000 for a diesel-powered unit.
   * In most cases, electricity will be much less expensive than the diesel fuel that would be consumed by a conventional terminal tractor. In addition, there are significant maintenance cost benefits to the battery electric terminal tractor.
   * In many areas of the country there are incentives available to offset some of the purchase price of the electric equipment as well as incentives to help substantially with the costs of installing charging equipment. In addition, in some areas, Low Carbon Fuel Standard credits are available to offset some of the ongoing electricity costs of charging the vehicles.
   * Many additional less obvious benefits should be monetized specific to each fleet’s circumstances.

6. **Tracking the vehicle to verify the cost benefits can be very difficult without significant prior planning.** Traditionally, tracking the expenses associated with terminal tractors is not a high priority for fleet operations. In the case of the battery electric terminal tractors, tracking operational costs will be a higher priority for most fleets due to the increased capital investment and ensuring that the TCO for the vehicles is managed. In order to adequately track the costs, data from the charging equipment, the electrical meter and the vehicle itself will be important to sorting out the return on investment. ## 2 What is a Terminal Tractor?

A terminal tractor generally is defined as a kind of semi-tractor that is intended to move trailers within a cargo yard, warehouse facility, nearby warehouses, an intermodal facility, a large manufacturing facility or similar location. Terminal tractors can be classified as either off-highway or on-highway depending on if they are licensed to travel on public highways. Manufacturers have estimated that about 90% of all new terminal tractor sales are configured to off-highway specifications.

Terminal tractors are referred to by a variety of other names including yard spotter, yard tractor, yard dog, yard hostler, mule, etc. NACFE will be using terminal tractors throughout this report as the most common term for these vehicles.

### 2.1 Features and Purpose of Terminal Tractors

Terminal tractors generally have some or all of these unique features:
● The tractor has a single-person cab offset to one side and usually has an automatic transmission for easy operation.
● Terminal tractors have a full height rear sliding door to facilitate easy accessibility to the trailer air and electrical connectors.
● The tractor has a very short wheelbase and sharp turning radius to facilitate working in very tight spaces.
● Terminal tractors can be either a 4x2, 6x4 or 6x2 configuration depending on the load requirements.

### 2.2 Terminal Tractors And Emissions

Starting around 2017 (exact timing varied by manufacturer), off-highway terminal tractors were required to meet what is known as Tier 4 final off-highway emissions regulations. [36] This forced the diesel engine systems to be equipped with both diesel particulate filters (DPF) and selective catalytic reduction (SCR) in order to meet these tougher emission requirements. The typical duty cycle for a terminal tractor is quite challenging for these aftertreatment components because the exhaust system rarely gets to the temperatures necessary for proper DPF regeneration to occur.

Customers have reported that they have experienced very high rates of DPF replacement on these types of applications — a very expensive repair. A DPF repair can run $2,500 to $8,000, depending on whether a remanufactured unit or a new one is installed and other factors like additional parts that must be replaced. [37] As a result, maintenance costs (and the consequent downtimes) for these vehicles have gone up substantially in the last few years. It is likely that the DPF/SCR aftertreatment system will be required on all diesel-powered terminal tractors going forward.

One fleet operating these new emission-compliant terminal tractors informed NACFE that their maintenance cost on these aftertreatment systems is more than $5,000 per year. It might be expected that this penalty will decrease with improvements to these production systems but will likely remain significant. Another fleet stated that they expect the life of diesel terminal tractors to be reduced from about 12 years to under 10.

● Does it make sense on a total cost of ownership (TCO) basis?
● What is the duty cycle of the diesel-powered units?
● Can an electrified unit do the same job as the diesel with adequate time for recharging?
● Does the electrical service to the property support the charging needs of the battery electric terminal tractor?

● What incentives are available to offset either the purchase of the vehicle or the purchase price and installation cost of the electrical infrastructure?
● Does the purchase or lease of a battery electric terminal tractor support the sustainability goals of the fleet?
● What are the minimum requirements for upgrading maintenance facilities and support for staff to ensure that the implementation is successful?

One factor to study when considering purchasing a battery electric terminal tractor is making sure that fleets understand the current vehicle duty cycle to ensure that an electrified vehicle is able to accomplish those same tasks at least as efficiently as its diesel-powered counterpart.

There are some key data points to keep in mind:
● **Fuel consumption:** Typically, this can be miles/gallon or gallons/hour. If, for example, a current terminal tractor gets about 4 miles/gallon and runs 60 miles a day, it would use about 15 gallons of fuel per day. An electric version of that same tractor might use 2.5 kWh/mile for that same 60 miles. Therefore, it would use 150 kWh/day. The battery pack size for the electric version of the tractor would need to be 225 kWh or larger to do the same job as the diesel-powered version.
*Note: A better unit of measure for terminal tractors is to relate critical data to hours of operation rather than miles due to the typical usage profile for terminal tractors. However, Run On Less – Electric featured 13 trucks with three terminal tractors and NACFE chose to measure and report data in miles. It is very difficult to recalculate given the way the data was collected. NACFE will take this into consideration as we move forward with other terminal tractor efforts.*
● **Idle time:** Terminal tractors often have very high idle percentages because of several factors. First, the engine generally is left running during pickup and drop maneuvers. In many cases, even when the tractor is not moving a trailer, the engine is left running to maintain the cabin environment for the driver. Idle fuel consumption on a typical diesel engine is usually one-half gallon of fuel per hour. Electrified terminal tractors are different as they don’t require energy to sit in one place, although they may require some energy to maintain the cabin environment.
● **Key off time:** Understanding this is critical since battery charging can theoretically be done any time the key is off as long as the vehicle is near a charger so it can be plugged in. Plugging in a battery electric terminal tractor is a very quick process if the tractor is within range of the cable.
● **Number of moves per day or moves per shift:** Understanding this will help the battery electric terminal tractor OEMs understand the most appropriate specifications for the battery and powertrain system. This also will help to understand the number of fifth wheel cycles and the energy required for those operations.
● **The need to be used in overtime situations:** Understanding the frequency of events where overtime is necessary for the terminal tractors combined with key off times during the shifts will help in figuring out the proper mix of charging power to ensure the tractor does not run out of power on a high use day.
● **Maintenance cost per hour or mile:** As mentioned before, the terminal tractor application can be quite difficult for today’s modern aftertreatment systems. This can frequently result in high

## 4 Charging Considerations

Electrified vehicles must be charged in order to maintain operation. With diesel-powered equipment, it often is possible to store several days of diesel fuel in the vehicle tank with a relatively small weight penalty. However, since batteries are very heavy per stored energy unit compared to diesel fuel, it is not economical to carry more energy than necessary on a battery electric vehicle. Therefore, the battery pack is generally sized to comfortably complete one day’s operation between charges. There should be enough extra battery capacity so there is virtually no risk of running out of power if the vehicle cannot get back to its charging location. This also must include taking into account the upper limits of the duty cycle as well as factoring in capacity for cold weather operation and possible stoppages due to a variety of factors (traffic, etc.).

**Note:** The battery pack size could be less than one day’s charge if the duty cycle allows for opportunity charging of sufficient length to maintain adequate capacity.

Battery electric terminal tractors take a significant time to charge. Depending on the capability of the charging system as well as the limits of the on-vehicle charging equipment, it can take several hours to recharge the batteries of the terminal tractor. This is fundamentally different than a diesel-powered terminal tractor where it only takes a few minutes to fill the fuel tank at a fueling station. This difference may mean that a fleet considering implementing battery electric terminal tractors might have to consider some operational and yard layout changes in order to accommodate this difference.

For example, if the terminal tractor uses 100 kWh during its shift and the charging system’s capacity is 25 kW, then it will take 4 to 5 hours to recharge the batteries to their full capacity. ## 5 Benefits of Electric Terminal Tractors

Battery electric terminal tractors offer many advantages in the marketplace. First, the electrical power for a battery electric vehicle (BEV) should be much less expensive than diesel fuel on an equivalent energy basis. Second, the maintenance cost for a BEV should be much less than that of the most recent terminal tractors.

### 5.2 Less Obvious Advantages

Battery electric terminal tractors also offer some other less obvious advantages that should be considered as part of the decision-making process.
● As mentioned in Section 2, terminal tractors are normally classified as on-highway or off-highway depending on whether they must be licensed to travel on commercial highways or stay confined to a warehouse or yard area. If the only reason that an on-highway vehicle must be purchased and licensed is so that it can get to a fueling station that is near the facility in which it works, then a battery electric terminal tractor might be able to avoid the licensing fees if the electricity to charge the vehicle can be obtained on site. One fleet indicated this was the primary reason they had to specify on-highway tractors.
● Similarly, the maintenance of a diesel-powered terminal tractor requires that fluids and filters be serviced periodically. The demand for this kind of maintenance on a battery electric terminal tractor are much reduced. Depending on the location of the service facility, it may be possible to avoid licensing and other considerations with the battery electric terminal tractor. Brake lining maintenance for battery electric terminal tractors should be lower compared to their diesel counterparts due to the regenerative braking function in the BEV that takes some of the braking function away from the traditional service brakes. It might be possible that most, if not all, of the maintenance could be completed onsite. Jason Sidders, senior manager, advanced applications engineering group, Dana Commercial Vehicles, said, “As you take advantage of that regen braking, you are getting less wear and tear on your traditional service brake system which makes those components last longer and reduces the interval times you have on those service brakes.”
● From the drivers’ perspective, the fueling process for a battery electric terminal tractor is actually easier than for a diesel-powered tractor. Filling diesel tanks can take anywhere from five to 15 minutes and the driver must be present throughout the process. However, fueling battery electric terminal tractors simply requires pulling up to the charging station and connecting the power cable. This process can be accomplished in much less time but may need to be done more frequently than the diesel fueling operation.

## 6 Challenges of Electric Terminal Tractors

Offsetting benefits of battery electric terminal tractors, of course, is their purchase price. Battery electric terminal tractors take a significant time to charge. Depending on the capability of the charging system as well as the limits of the on-vehicle charging equipment, it can take several hours to recharge the batteries of the terminal tractor.

The process of procuring both infrastructure and the EV itself is long and requires fleet personnel to focus and interact with more functional areas both within the fleet as well as with utilities and government entities. This can be challenging.

Speed is not a significant factor with the terminal tractors in the Run. Frequently the maximum speed is limited to 15 to 25 mph.

SOC is like the diesel fuel gauge. It is just as problematic and challenging. Battery SOC for RoL-E is a measurement made by the vehicle by continuously monitoring voltage, amperage, and amp hours remaining after fully charging — essentially counting the amp-hours expended over time and subtracting it from the full charge state.

Opportunity charging, charging midway during a work shift in between driving, for example over lunch breaks, can extend the daily range of a battery electric truck. RoL-E saw opportunity charging successfully used by terminal tractors.

Battery electric vehicles generally can recover energy by using the drive motors to slow the vehicles. This is called regenerative braking; essentially the motors rather than using energy are acting as generators and putting energy back into the batteries.

Deliveries, the act of stopping the vehicle and unloading (or loading), are metrics NACFE captured for RoL-E. Deliveries also have different parameters when looking at a terminal tractor versus a box truck due to the differences in what constitutes a delivery. For terminal tractors, NACFE looked for quick (within 90 seconds) back and forth movements that indicated a trailer drop or hook followed by another similar back and forth at least 60 seconds after the previous one. Two of those movements were counted as a delivery (i.e., one hook and one drop).

For RoL-E, NACFE described the charging rate as how fast and at what power level. Charging rate was determined from vehicle data bus signals using SOC over time when the vehicle was plugged into the charger. The vehicle data bus continuously reported voltage and amperage, and integrated that over time to provide SOC.

## 9.2 Tracking Costs

It is important to track the costs and benefits of battery electric terminal tractors versus the baseline diesel unit to determine ROI.

In many fleets, tracking either the exact costs or the benefits of terminal tractors can be difficult. Generally, the big costs in an ROI analysis for terminal tractors are the difference in purchase price or lease price, the ongoing costs of fuel (either diesel or electricity) and ongoing maintenance costs.

> **Lesson Learned**
> It is important for fleets to track and verify the costs of their battery electric terminal tractors in order to manage TCO.

### 9.2.1 Diesel Costs

Let’s start with diesel-powered units since these costs would be the starting point for any ROI analysis of purchasing or leasing a battery electric terminal tractor.

From a purchase price standpoint, a diesel-powered terminal tractor will cost somewhere between $100,000 and $120,000, depending on configuration, options, etc.

In many fleets, fuel consumption of the terminal tractors is not tracked closely as it is a very small percentage of the overall fuel bill for a given operation. Maintenance costs, on the other hand, generally are tracked more closely especially when repairs result in very high costs or downtime. In some operations, the maintenance costs are included as part of a lease agreement for the tractor rather than being tracked separately. Often, fuel cost and maintenance for terminal tractors are tracked using hours of operation rather than miles as is the case with other vehicles in a fleet. In fact, tracking by hours is probably a more relevant measure for a very low speed and high idle operation like a terminal tractor.

### 9.2.2 Electric Vehicle Costs

Understanding the costs of a battery electric terminal tractor can be even more challenging. Let’s start with purchase price. OEMs have stated that a new battery electric terminal tractor can cost between $275,000 and $350,000 depending on configuration, options, etc.

Fuel cost for a battery electric terminal tractor consists of the cost of electricity that is used to charge the vehicle. Of course, this cost is determined by the rate and how much electricity the unit consumes as it passes the electric meter going on to the property. If the electrical service for the property includes other loads like buildings, lights, etc. in addition to the vehicles, then it will be impossible to use the electric meter charges as input for the analysis. Several fleets and utilities have recommended using a separate meter to track only vehicle energy for this reason.

Why does this matter? It matters because, unlike diesel fuel that has very few losses between delivery and fuel tank, there are significant losses of power between the electrical meter and when it ends up in the batteries of the vehicle. Input from suppliers and fleets indicate that these losses are between 5% to 20%, depending on the charger selected and other choices about the installation. Therefore, to put 100 kWh of energy into the batteries of a battery electric terminal tractor, you need to flow approximately 110 kWh of electricity past the electric meter. Manufacturers and system suppliers are working to minimize these losses going forward.

Some charging systems will track the energy as it is delivered to the vehicle and the electronics on the vehicle also may track a similar number. Each will be a different number, and some will be of somewhat questionable accuracy, at least in the early days of BEV adoption. Keep in mind that these energy numbers are subject to both measurements and assumptions that model the system components, and all can be subject to inaccuracies for a variety of reasons. Manufacturers will work diligently over time to improve the accuracy of these estimates. The numbers recorded on the RoL-E vehicles were from the vehicle itself, not the charger or the electrical meter. Also, the RoL-E vehicles were tracked via mileage vs. hours because this is how the data was reported to NACFE. NACFE does not have the information from the Run measured in hours.

To further complicate this picture, the vehicle will track how the energy from the battery is consumed during operation. This includes power for propulsion and accessories during operation, power consumed while the vehicle is not moving, and power recovered during regenerative braking. The net result of this is the overall power consumed by the vehicle over a specific shift which is then replenished during charging. The RoL-E vehicles also tracked this energy consumption. Figure 21 shows the energy charged vs. the energy consumed for each of the three RoL-E vehicles. In one case, no charging data was available.

You can see that in one case, the power consumed by the vehicle was substantially more than the power that was replenished during charging. This, of course, is impossible and contrary to the laws of physics. NACFE has been unable to determine the source of this discrepancy. It is likely the higher of the two numbers is a more accurate measure of the actual vehicle power consumption.

## 10 Terminal Tractor Report Findings

The study team has six key findings based on the three battery electric terminal tractors that participated in RoL-E.

1. **NACFE considers terminal tractors one of the best, if not THE best, paths for a heavy-duty tractor fleet to learn about and implement a BEV in a fleet operation.**
   ● Choosing to purchase a battery electric terminal tractor will help mobilize the fleet to understand the many aspects of what will be required in future larger purchases of BEVs. This would include evaluating the areas of purchasing, TCO considerations, facilities, vehicle parking, operations changes, selecting and installing electric charging infrastructure, driver training and reactions, maintenance, etc.
   ● The battery electric terminal tractor is limited to a small area around the facilities, so it is always close to home. This allows for opportunity charging.
   ● Electric units are available to be a direct replacement of virtually all diesel-powered terminal tractor use cases with very few operational adjustments.
   ● Most terminal tractor duty cycles can get by with low power level (and thus less expensive) charging stations.
2. **The drivers in Run on Less – Electric all rave about their quieter operation and improved performance.** All the drivers mentioned that they liked the responsiveness of the battery electric terminal tractor, and they also liked the regenerative braking feature once they got used to it. In fact, Ryder driver Hancock, said, “For me the difference between an electric and a diesel tractor is response. When you drive an electric truck, when you press the accelerator, you get an immediate response.” Drivers all mentioned the quiet operation, and some mentioned that this made hearing dispatch orders much easier. Also, one driver mentioned that it was great not having to deal with diesel fuel odors and spills.

Deployments of zero emissions commercial vehicles is not taking place everywhere across the United States. According to a report, *Zeroing in on Zero-Emission Trucks* from CALSTART, “They are concentrated in areas with supportive electric truck policies and regulations.” The map below shows the deployment of zero emissions commercial vehicles by state.

Today, zero emissions vehicles have a high cost when compared to diesel-powered vehicles, so some states are offering incentives or grant funding opportunities to help defray the cost so more zero emission trucks will be purchased. The map also shows states that offer voucher incentive programs.
