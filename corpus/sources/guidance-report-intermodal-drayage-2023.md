From 2020 to 2023, the entire supply chain was shown to be very sensitive to disruptions of any one link as they tend to have a domino effect between supply chain elements. Port back-ups of ships impact the volume and migration of containers leading to shortages and overages of containers at the wrong locations to be useful to the supply chain [70]. The container shipping cost spike was not unique to North America. The Economist confirmed that by early 2021 shipping costs from Asia to Europe had more than tripled as shown in Figure 22 [72]. The nature of supply and demand of containers is a global challenge with regional ramifications.

Compounding container shortages is mis-location — containers in the wrong places. As the supply chain becomes constrained, freight rates tend to rise making hauling empty containers much less appealing than hauling paid freight. Empty containers end up at destinations unable to be filled by returning cargo. Supply and demand of containers then impacts the flow of exports possible out of the ports. These transportation factors tend to create oversupply and undersupply situations at distribution centers and warehouses, where products are at the wrong locations in the same way that the containers are mislocated.

### 5.2.9 Tariffs

Trade conflicts also impact the supply chain such as the perennial battle between US container and chassis manufacturers wanting to secure business in place of the state supported Chinese ones, with US shipping companies arguing to keep buying the Chinese products [67][68]. Deborah Lockridge, editor of *Heavy Duty Trucking*, stated, “The Department of Commerce determined that Chinese chassis producers were being unfairly subsidized at a rate of 44.32%, according to a news release from the Wiley Rein law firm, which represented the coalition” which includes Cheetah Chassis Corp., Hercules Enterprises, Pitts Enterprises, Pratt Industries, and Stoughton Trailers. The president of IANA has argued that shipping companies are hurt by high tariffs on the Chinese-built containers and chassis in docket submissions discussing potential new tariffs [67].

Tariff policies and lawsuits have been debated in the container and chassis industry for decades. In 2015, the International Trade Commission (ITC) ruled that Chinese-made dry containers were not appreciably harming the US domestic container manufacturing industry [73]. The subtlety in that ruling is that domestic containers are 53’ ones used in rail and highway intermodal operations, whereas the Chinese containers are largely the 20’ and 40’ shipborne ISO 668 containers.

### 5.2.10 Excise Taxes

Excise taxes are another key point with respect to containers. Rules regarding highway trailers are side-stepped by containers since containers by themselves are not trailers as they have no wheels. Compounding that is the fact that the chassis are not trailers as they, by themselves, carry no freight. Both are exempt from federal excise taxes which reduces their cost by at least 12% versus new highway trailers. Eric Rath writing in the *Transportation Law Journal* referred to Revenue Ruling 60-185, C. B. 1960-1, 412, stating, “Cargo-containers which are designed for shipment of property by water, rail and highway are not taxable as trailer or semitrailer bodies even though they resemble such bodies [75][76].” Further, “the basis for holding the cargo containers nontaxable is that they are considered to be articles designed or adapted by the manufacturer for purposes predominantly other than the transportation of property on the highway, even though incidental highway use may occur [75][76].” If the containers are primarily designed for use on-highway, they are subject to the excise tax.

### 5.2.11 Emissions and Aerodynamics

Like excise taxes, rules requiring aerodynamic devices on trailers, such as SmartWay requirements in California, do not apply to containers on chassis as neither by itself are legally defined as trailers. Examples of aerodynamic tractors and non-aero containers on chassis are shown in Figure 24. Further, an appellate court ruling in 2021 regarding the 2016 Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)/National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) Green House Gas (GHG) regulation of trailers concluded that “trailers, however, have no motor. They are therefore not ‘motor vehicles.’ Nor are they ‘vehicles’ when that term is used in the context of a vehicle’s fuel economy, since motorless vehicles use no fuel [74].” Since containers and chassis are not even considered trailers, they are further removed from these GHG requirements under this decision.

## 5.3 HISTORY OF TRAINS AND TRUCKS

Understanding the future potential for rail intermodal transportation to reduce emissions requires some perspective on the history of railroads in North America. This report can only briefly touch on a topic that has spawned copious amounts of research and publications over the decades. The story of trains is one of successes and failures in an endless pursuit of profitability. Operating a railroad comes with significant infrastructure capital and maintenance requirements as unlike the highway and road systems, the rails are not generally publicly owned or operated. Where trucks from multiple companies can make use of highways, they pay for that use through fuel taxes, permits and registration fees shared with automobiles. Railways have dedicated track largely exclusively running their own trains so the entire burden for building and maintaining the transportation network falls on them. Economics require the rails to prioritize operations to those lanes where there is demand. Freight flows by trucks, by rail, by ships, boats and barges, by pipelines and by air based on market needs. ### 5.3.1 Class I Railroads

The North American freight rail system is categorized by class according to annual revenue per the STB, where Class I railroads comprise the seven largest railroads with annual revenues exceeding $504 million. Since Amtrak is a passenger railway, we will be referring to the six Class I railroads carrying freight only going forward.

* Amtrak
* Burlington Northern Santa Fe (BNSF)
* CSX Transportation
* Canadian National Railway (CNR)
* Canadian Pacific Kansas City Limited (CPKC)
* Norfolk Southern Railway (NSR)
* Union Pacific (UP)

Class II and III carriers often get merged with the larger Class I carriers in frequent mergers and acquisitions, or they are just as likely to be sold off as standalone companies. Passenger rail also exists but is largely operated by arrangements to use Class I, II and III rail lines. Pre-1970s passenger rail was a substantial part of rail operations but lost market share to other modes over time with the development of faster passenger transportation alternatives like jet airplanes and personal cars on new highways.

The group of six rail companies largely operate de facto monopolies on their routes. Freight moves across country by being exchanged at various hand-off interchanges. Very rarely do these companies compete directly with each other on specific regional lanes, but competitors can contract for sharing haulage rights on specific tracks. This is a fundamental difference with the trucking industry where multiple companies can provide service on the same lanes and must compete with each other on many levels of performance and cost.

### 5.3.2 Railroad Trackage and Ton-Miles

The history of rail transportation in North America is one of rapid expansion with trackage peaking in the US in the 1920s and declining since then as technological and societal changes displaced rail as a primary mode of freight. Factors such as World War I, the Great Depression, World War II, the US highway expansion, the introduction of trucks, airplanes, and ever larger ships, changes in freight regulations, unions, the migration of passenger traffic to other modes, e-commerce, multiple bankruptcies, and many other factors have contributed to the decline of rail as the primary mode of freight transport.

US railroad trackage peaked in 1916 at roughly 250,000 miles and has been decreasing since then to today’s estimated 137,000 miles [19][16]. However, railroad ton-mileage has generally increased over time as the decreasing trackage has continuously been used more efficiently through heavier, longer, more capable and more technologically advanced trains. Overall demand for freight movement has also been steadily increasing raising all freight modes in response as ton-miles progressed from 4.2 trillion ton-miles in 1980 to more than 5.2 trillion ton-miles in 2020 as shown in Figure 28 [30].

### 5.3.4 Comparing Rail and Truck Emissions

Comparing emissions from rail and trucks is somewhat an exercise in comparing apples and oranges. The macro level comparison highlights that trains are relatively small contributors to the overall national carbon emissions. Rail’s portion of national GHG emissions in the US is less than 2%, while in Canada, rail contributes less than 5%, and in Mexico less than 1% [201][202][203][204]. Medium and heavy-duty trucking share of transportations emissions in the US are estimated at 58%, Canada 24%, and Mexico 29%. ### 5.3.8 Reciprocal Switching Rule

Many trucking companies can share one highway. In general, most Class I rail lines are dominated by their primary owner. This leads to challenges in providing competitive rail services to customers located on or near those rail lines.

Railroads may reach agreement to share rail lines. According to Jerry Pinkepank in a 1979 *Trains* article available at the Transportation Research Board, one such agreement is termed trackage rights, or bridging rights, where a competing railway may negotiate, for a fee, passage of their own locomotives, rolling stock and crew on the owning company’s rail line. A second agreement is termed traffic rights, where, again for a fee, the competing railway may be permitted to pick up and deliver on the owning company’s rail line. Additionally, there are contractual rights possible called interchange rights, where competing railways may take over shipments at specified terminals from the owning rail line to move them to their own system. Another contractual sharing agreement is called detour rights, which allows competing railroads to move their equipment on each other’s rail lines to circumvent outages, accidents, etc. to keep the freight moving. There are multiple permutations of these different agreements [119].

Sharing rail lines is essentially viewed as an exception where sharing highways is the norm. This permits greater competition between trucking companies and also between trucking companies and rail lines. Efforts are in process at the federal level to encourage greater competition for rail services.

In September 2023, the STB proposed new competitive rules that for the first time create service standards for railroads to address shipper concerns with slow, costly and or uncompetitive rail services. STB states in the Federal Register, “The Board is proposing a new set of regulations that would provide access to reciprocal switching when there is inadequate service [4].” Reciprocal switching is defined by Merriam-Webster as, “an interchange of inbound and outbound carload freight among railroads in which the cars are switched by one railroad to or from the siding of another under a regular switching charge that is usually absorbed by the carrier receiving the line-haul [2].” The proposed rules are summarized by Bill Stephens of *Trains*. “If a railroad fails to meet any one of three standards for 12

The three standards are based on the shipper obtaining 12 straight weeks of service data from the railroad. To avoid engagement of reciprocal switching, the railroad must have service reliability, service consistency and local service requirements [5].

Reliability is based on on-time performance, hitting its estimated time of delivery within a 24-hours window of the railroad’s original estimate at least 60% of the time.

Service consistency is based on the 12-week average transit times for loaded cars, unit trains and empties where the average transit time stays within 20% to 25% compared to the previous year’s same 12-week average.

Local service measures how quickly the railroad finds and pulls loaded and empty cars within a 12-hour service window over the 12-week period. To avoid enabling reciprocal switching, the railroad must keep its spot and pull rate within 80% for the 12 straight weeks.

The burden is on the shipper to audit the railroad’s performance and to justify enabling reciprocal switching, but it is a step toward formally recognizing through enacting regulations that rail service has much room to improve.

Canadian railroads have had available voluntary reciprocal switching at the choice of the shipper without any qualifying reasons.

Switching between railways tends to add additional transit time for freight because of the added exchanges.

### 5.3.9 Federal Railway Funding

Railway performance has received significant attention in 2023. In September 2023, the government awarded more than $1.4 billion to 70 projects in 35 states “that improve railway safety and boost capacity, with much of the money coming from the 2021 Infrastructure Law [6].” Improvements targeted include both freight and passenger rail. This follows announcements in June of $570 million from the Railroad Crossing Elimination (RCE) Grant Program funding projects in 32 states to improve traffic flow and reduce accidents at crossings [7]. This is in addition to $1.2 billion in funding in January 2023 for nine infrastructure projects which included two specifically for passenger trains to improve throughput and safety [8]. Clearly officials have concluded that railroads need financial help to improve performance. Funding spent on safety also improves rail system efficiency and reliability.

### 5.3.11 Diesel Pre-Buys

A biproduct of regulatory deadlines tends to be some level of pre-buy of the existing technologies [18].
This can be true for both trucks and rail.

The California port rules mandating any new port vehicle registrations to be zero emission in 2024, for 
example, have some fleets purchasing diesels in 2023 that can be grandfathered into use beyond 2024. 
There also may be life extension efforts for existing diesels at the ports so that they are in commercial 
service longer than they typically are. California previously banned pre-2010 model year trucks from 
operating at the ports. In 2035, the California regulations will require all port operators to exclusively 
use zero-emission vehicles. The pre-buy diesels will eventually have to be retired.

Locomotives tend to have long lives, from 25 to more than 50 years. During that time, they can be 
rebuilt or updated at less cost typically than purchasing a new unit. Locomotive rules, such as EPA’s Tier 
4 and California’s recent In-Use Locomotive Regulation, have caused life extension and rebuilds of 
existing locomotives [120][121][77][78][79].

### 6.1 INCREASING INTERMODAL RAIL MARKET SHARE

The opportunity to reduce emissions by proportionally moving more freight by rail versus trucks is a 
complex challenge. Increasing rail intermodal use by capturing market share from trucking’s long-haul 
segment would inherently increase regional and short urban haul truck market share.

As an example, a 100-railcar unit consist with 200 double stacked 53’ domestic containers would use at 
least 200 truckloads in first-mile service delivering the containers to an embarkation terminal railyard, 
then use at least 200 other truckloads in last-mile service distributing the containers from the 
destination terminal. Those 400 truckloads might travel fewer net miles than the non-intermodal 
alternative of 200 truckloads just doing long-haul, but that depends on many geographic factors and is 
not always an obvious win for intermodal. In supply and demand economics, decreasing demand for 
long-haul trucks also tends to increase price competition making trucking more affordable and attractive 
to fleets.

Shippers with facilities located on rail spurs and sidings would have an advantage by eliminating trucks 
in at least one segment of the transportation supply chain, but these opportunities have been reduced 
significantly over the last 50 years as rail trackage has been reduced to save costs.

Rail has been struggling for decades to win back market share from trucking. A two-page Southern 
Railway System advertisement, see Figure 66, in the January 1979 issue of *Trains* magazine predicts that 
the railroads’ share of freight ton-miles will increase to 24% by 1990. The advertisement quotes R.E. 
Chambers, intermodal division manager, Southern Railway, “This certainly flies in the face of the cliché 
about railroads that we’re a dying business [89].”

The market ultimately decides how it ships products. The market is preferentially choosing trucking as a 
priority over intermodal, although rail has made some inroads on specific lanes. The explanation for the 
shift is complex. Many factors have contributed relating to service parameters such as time-to-market 
for shipped products, differences in in-process-inventory required for different modes of transport, 
reliability to deliver as scheduled, convenience, extent of customer-centric operation, and more.

Rail at present is a slower means of transporting goods. In an e-commerce economy where speed of 
delivery has become critical to success, rail is at a disadvantage. Each hand-off of freight comes at the 
cost of added shipping process time. A familiar term to process managers is also work-in-process (WIP) 
inventory. The longer items are in transport, the greater the WIP inventory in the supply chain. WIP 
inventory is a non-value-added cost to many companies.

To offset the slower delivery and greater WIP inventory, rail needs to offer shippers transportation at a 
lower cost than trucking. Rail has been consistently attempting to reduce capital and operating costs for 
decades trying to achieve both profitability for investors and offer lower cost freight movement to 
shippers. The cost cutting efforts have often been at cross purposes to reliable on-time delivery crucial 
to shippers.

### Utility Impacts from Greening Intermodal

Rail and truck s share similar needs for infrastructure when transitioning to zero-emission and near-zero 
emission powertrains. Utilities need to have a vision into all the technology paths of all intermodal 
modes, including truck, rail, ship and aircraft as they combine to place new demands on the electric 
infrastructure. Whether using electricity directly in the case of a battery electric vehicle, or using a range 
of alternative fuels, all require additional electric power, for example in compressing gases, movement 
of fuels or processing the fuels.

Multiple studies have been conducted on electrifying drayage vehicles at ports. The National Renewable

## 6.3 ZERO-EMISSION TERMINAL TRACTORS

In its 2022 market segment report *Electric Trucks Have Arrived: The Use Case for Terminal Tractors*, 
NACFE concluded that 100% of the terminal tractor market could be electrified [133]. “NACFE considers 
terminal tractors one of the best, if not THE best, paths for a heavy-duty tractor fleet to learn about and 
implement a BEV in a fleet operation.” The primary conclusions from that report are:

• Choosing to purchase a battery electric terminal tractor will help mobilize the fleet to understand 
the many aspects of what will be required in future larger purchases of BEVs. This would include 
evaluating the areas of purchasing, TCO considerations, facilities, vehicle parking, operations 
changes, selecting and installing electric charging infrastructure, driver training and reactions, 
maintenance, etc.
• The battery electric terminal tractor is limited to a small area around the facility, so it is always 
close to home. This allows for opportunity charging.
• Electric units are available to be a direct replacement of virtually all diesel-powered terminal 
tractor use cases with very few operational adjustments.
• Most terminal tractor duty cycles can get by with low power level (and thus less expensive) 
charging station.

# 7 RECOMMENDATIONS

Three significant opportunities to reduce freight related emissions are:

• Shifting more market share from regional and long-haul trucking to intermodal rail.
• Replacing traditional diesel drayage tractors with zero-emission and near-zero emission tractors.
• Replacing traditional diesel terminal tractors with zero-emission and near-zero emission 
tractors.

Trucks are quicker than locomotives to transition to new technologies because the useful asset life of a 
truck may be less than 15 years whereas locomotives may have asset lives of 50 years. However, the 
aging North American locomotive fleet offers opportunity to make significant steps in new zero-emission locomotive technology once it becomes available.

Increasing intermodal rail freight from long-haul on-highway operations on select routes can reduce net 
emissions, but the opportunity may be tempered if the required drayage trucking demands at both ends 
of the rail line have significant mileage requirements.

Rail must improve its customer service focus, refining on-time reliability and shortening time in transit to 
better compete with trucking.

Many trucking companies can share one highway, where most Class I rail lines are dominated by their 
primary owner. This leads to challenges in providing competitive rail services to customers located on or 
near those rail lines. Improved flexibility with reciprocal switching could increase customer demand for 
rail services through greater rail company competition.

# 8 CONCLUSIONS

Railroading and trucking are intertwined freight segments that both compete and complement each 
other. Both are viable alternatives for moving freight but differ in focus, with trucks generally being 
faster and more flexible. Railroads can offer lower costs and greater volumes on specific lanes.

1. The competitive relationship between rail and trucking has seen trucking take the lion’s share of 
market. The two, however, are linked as shipping rates are tied to supply and demand on the 
two transportation modes. Greater use of one invariably drives volume down in the other and 
hence drives market rates down making them more attractive to shippers. If rail or trucking 
assets are operating at capacity, the other mode has opportunities to capture market share 
because the freight has to move.
2. Intermodal railroading and drayage trucking are a subset of the relationship between rail and 
trucking, wrapped inside the ubiquitous shipping container. Growth in intermodal rail 
operations inherently means growth in regional and urban short-haul trucking as trains do not 
go everywhere that trucks can. For this reason, predicting the net emissions benefits of greater 
intermodal use is much more complex than just replacing a long-haul truck with a train over a 
common distance.
3. Intermodal terminals are not located everywhere. Six major Class I railroads have differing 
service networks in North America. Freight generally has to be moved both to and from these
