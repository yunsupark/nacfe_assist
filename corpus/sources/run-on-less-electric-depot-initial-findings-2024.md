**Speaker (unclear):** Run on Less Electric Depot is now done.
We've watched the operations of these electric trucks and the charging at the depots, and we've learned so much.
Here are our initial findings that we have.
Run on Less Electric Depot contained many elements, starting with a 10-episode boot camp,
depot visits and participant profiles,
stories from the run, dashboard data,
findings being shared at industry events,
and detailed reports coming in 2024.
39 experts presented at the boot camp sessions, and 122 individuals were interviewed at the 10 sites.
Many benefits and challenges were confirmed or discovered.
We are in the early stages of electrifying North American trucking,
and Run on Less Electric Depot represents that market.
The 10 depots are operating about 850 total trucks,
where 291 are battery electric.
These are some of the first electrified depots in existence,
and included 11 different truck OEMs with 8 charger companies and 5 utilities.
The trucks included vans and step vans,
medium-duty box trucks, heavy-duty terminal tractors,
and heavy-duty regional haul return-to-base operations.
There are no sleeper tractors yet available,
so there were no long-haul disparate route participants.
We estimated the total power requirements of all trucks at the 10 depots
at 214 megawatt-hours per day.
Given all the experiences of the run, NACFE has some initial findings to share.
First, is that small energy depots are ready now.
Some sites have a lower number of trucks
and/or run routes that are shorter and do not require much energy or big battery packs on the trucks.
Urban trucks also tend to have long overnight dwell times,
allowing for more cost-effective charging,
improving their total cost of ownership.
Finally, the amount of effort for the utility to provide this power is much lower.
Second, large energy depots are gaining momentum,
given improvements in the trucks,
faster charging, and improved efforts by utilities, construction companies, and others.
One heavy-duty truck completed 410 miles on a single charge
and 1,076 miles in a 24-hour day hauling heavy beverages.
Longer regional haul return-to-base operations thought not possible
are becoming more practical,
and we suggest fleets and their supporting organizations
should all reevaluate their plans in this sector.
Lastly, energizing the sites to charge these trucks takes too long,
without much confidence that this will improve.
The 10 depots experienced 9 to up to 36 months to get energized,
and this forced the fleets to get creative deploying portable or temporary charging.
Many actions need to take place to better match the availability of the trucks
with the need to charge them on site.
Run on Less Electric Depot provided all of us with many insights
for how to act now to increase electric truck adoption,
and is offering many learnings to adjust our strategies going forward.
Thanks to everyone who participated, sponsored, and followed.
This is so important and timely work.
