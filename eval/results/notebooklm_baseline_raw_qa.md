## Q13 [out_of_scope]

**Q:** What does this report conclude about the total cost of ownership (TCO) comparison across the four Messy Middle powertrains?

**NotebookLM answer:**
The report concludes that there is no single "winner" for TCO; instead, economic parity is not a fixed future date but a specific operational window defined by a fleet's duty cycle, infrastructure strategy, and regional factors.
While a dedicated "TCO Ramifications Report" is scheduled for 2026 to provide a deeper analysis, the current reports offer the following conclusions regarding the cost of each powertrain:
Diesel / Renewable Diesel: Diesel remains the lowest-cost freight solution when environmental and social factors are excluded. However, its cost is expected to increase significantly (with estimates ranging from $2,300 to $42,000 per vehicle) as manufacturers implement new technologies to meet upcoming ultra-low NOx regulations.
Natural Gas / RNG: These vehicles have upfront costs approximately 1.5 to 2 times higher than diesel. However, they can achieve a favorable TCO because the fuel cost is roughly 50% that of diesel. Participating fleets like Wegmans report expecting a 2 to 2.5-year ROI due to these lower fuel costs. Additionally, incentives often allow RNG to be offered at price parity with fossil natural gas.
Battery Electric (BEV): BEVs are already achieving favorable TCO relative to diesel in specific applications like terminal tractors and short regional haul. While upfront costs remain high, they are becoming more competitive with diesel in similar duty cycles. Maintenance costs for BEVs are estimated to be 50% to 60% lower than diesel because they have significantly fewer moving parts.
Hydrogen Fuel Cell: This is currently the most expensive option, with fuel cell systems estimated to cost three to four times more than diesel. To reach TCO parity with diesel, the report indicates that the cost of green hydrogen must drop to approximately $3/kg.
Overall, the sources emphasize that fleets must move away from a simple "lowest cost" mindset and adopt a more complex decision-making framework that includes infrastructure, incentives, and sustainability goals alongside traditional TCO metrics.

## Q15 [out_of_scope]

**Q:** What has NACFE found about total cost of ownership for last-mile electric delivery vans?

**NotebookLM answer:**
NACFE’s research indicates that battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) are already a "strong case" for last-mile delivery, with some applications currently achieving a positive total cost of ownership (TCO) compared to internal combustion engines.
Key findings regarding the TCO for electric delivery vans include:
Upfront Cost Parity: Electric vans and step vans have upfront costs that are much closer to parity with their gas and diesel counterparts than heavier Class 8 vehicle segments.
Lower Maintenance Costs: Maintenance costs for BEVs are expected to be significantly lower because they have fewer moving parts, no complex exhaust emissions systems, and simpler reporting requirements.
Operational Efficiency: Last-mile duty cycles are characterized by urban stop-and-go driving, which is ideal for BEVs because it allows for energy recovery through regenerative braking.
Inexpensive Charging: Many last-mile depots operate with long dwell times overnight. This allows for slow, managed charging, which significantly reduces electricity costs and avoids expensive demand charges on power bills.
The Role of Incentives: While TCO is improving, federal, state, and local incentives—as well as programs like California’s Low Carbon Fuel Standard (LCFS) credits—are often currently required to reach full price parity with the diesel vehicles being replaced.
NACFE notes that standard TCO calculations often struggle to capture the full picture because they may fail to account for increased uptime, the impact of the operational learning curve, or the long-term benefits of fuel price stabilitycompared to volatile diesel prices. Overall, NACFE classifies last-mile delivery as a "small energy depot" application that is ready for electrification now due to lower energization costs and favorable TCO.

## Q20 [synthesis]

**Q:** NACFE's 2023 white paper laid out a suggested sequence for fleets choosing a powertrain (start with BEV, then hydrogen fuel cell, then natural gas/hybrids/renewable fuels, then efficient diesel as a fallback), while also acknowledging this was 'an oversimplification.' Did the real-world results of the 2025 Run on Less – Messy Middle, which tested all four options side by side, support a single best powertrain choice or confirm the need for fleet-specific evaluation?

**NotebookLM answer:**
The real-world results of the 2025 Run on Less – Messy Middle strongly confirmed the need for fleet-specific evaluation rather than supporting a single "best" powertrain choice. While the 2023 framework provided a logical starting point, NACFE’s subsequent research emphasizes that the industry has moved into a phase where multiple solutions must coexist based on operational realities.
The following findings from the 2025 demonstration support this conclusion:
No Single "Winner": The report explicitly states that there is "no single 'winner'" among the four powertrains. Instead, initial findings show that all four technologies are performing well in the long-haul segment when matched to appropriate duty cycles.
Operational Dependency: Choice depends entirely on the "intricacies of each fleet’s operation," including specific factors such as length of haul, terrain, and access to fueling or charging infrastructure. For example, predictable regional return-to-base routes may be ideal for BEVs, while high-mileage corridors currently better suit diesel or natural gas.
Economic Parity is a Window, Not a Date: The research found that economic parity between these technologies is not a fixed point in the future. Instead, it is a "specific operational window" defined by a fleet's unique duty cycle and infrastructure strategy.
Industry Sentiment: Participating fleets reinforced this view, with PepsiCo’s Director of Fleet Decarbonization noting, "we all understand there is no one-size-fits-all solution".
Ultimately, the sources indicate that the Messy Middle is a period of increasing market segmentation. While the 2023 sequence suggested a hierarchy, the 2025 results show that fleets must adopt a more complex decision-making framework that prioritizes matching the specific technology to the right duty cycle to be successful.
